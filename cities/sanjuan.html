Experience caribbean vibes with US convenience, beaches and tax benefits. A tropical climate and strong easy visas make San Juan an easy base, and at about $3,200 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep safety in mind.

Cost of Living in San Juan

San Juan sits at the pricier end of the Caribbean for remote workers, though it compares favorably to most U.S. cities. All prices are in USD, the official currency, since Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory.

  • Rent (1-bedroom, Condado or Miramar): $1,200-$1,600/month
  • Rent (1-bedroom, Santurce or Rio Piedras): $900-$1,200/month
  • Utilities (electricity + water): $150-$300/month; electricity runs high if you run AC all day, which you will
  • Groceries: $350-$500/month for one person; local markets cost less, imported goods cost more than the mainland
  • Eating out: $10-$15 for a casual lunch, $30-$60 for a sit-down dinner at a mid-range restaurant
  • Coworking hot-desk: $200-$350/month at most San Juan spaces
  • Local transport (Tren Urbano + buses): $30-$60/month if you rely on public transit; add $150-$250/month if you use Uber regularly

Total realistic range: $2,200-$3,500/month for a single nomad living comfortably. Budget closer to $2,200 in Santurce with a shared apartment, or $3,000+ if you want your own place in Condado with daily Ubers and regular restaurant meals.

Where to Work

San Juan has a genuine coworking infrastructure built around its growing remote-work community. Piloto 151 is the original and has multiple San Juan locations including Old San Juan and two Santurce spots, with 24/7 access and reliable fiber. Co.co.haus on Avenida Ponce de Leon in Santurce is well-regarded for fast internet, a solid coffee setup, and a generator that keeps things running during outages. Spece offers a more polished, upscale environment with lagoon views and is popular with startup founders. Mosaico Cowork on Ponce de Leon brings a cafe-style feel with a content studio for creators.

Cafe-work culture is limited compared to, say, Mexico City or Lisbon. Most coffee shops are not particularly laptop-friendly, and the heat means you will want reliable, air-conditioned space for long sessions. Coworking is the sensible default.

WiFi quality varies significantly. Coworking spaces with fiber internet and backup generators are reliable; residential internet can be inconsistent, and power outages are a real ongoing issue across the island. If you have video calls, budget for a space with a generator backup or confirm your apartment building has one.

Getting Around

Old San Juan is the most walkable area on the island: compact, cobblestoned, and manageable entirely on foot. Santurce and Condado are also reasonably walkable for daily errands and eating out.

Beyond those neighborhoods, San Juan is car-dependent. The Tren Urbano metro runs between Bayamon and Sagrado Corazon with fares around $1.50 per ride, but its coverage is limited for most nomad commutes. Buses are cheap ($0.75 per ride, $5 daily pass) but slow and infrequent. Uber is the practical solution for most trips and is widely available in the metro area.

Luis Munoz Marin International Airport (SJU) is 15-20 minutes from central San Juan. Uber from the airport to Condado or Santurce typically runs $15-$25. Fixed-rate taxis are also available from a kiosk at arrivals. Car rentals are straightforward from the airport if you plan to explore the island.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, so U.S. immigration law applies in full. For U.S. citizens, no passport or visa is required at all: travel is domestic and you can stay indefinitely.

Citizens of countries in the U.S. Visa Waiver Program can enter with a valid ESTA approval (costs $40, valid two years) and stay up to 90 days for tourism or business. Citizens of non-VWP countries need a standard B-1/B-2 U.S. visitor visa, which typically allows stays of up to 6 months, though it does not permit employment.

There is no Puerto Rico-specific digital nomad visa. Extensions beyond the standard VWP 90-day limit require filing for a change of status through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services. Confirm current requirements with official U.S. government sources before traveling.

Best Time to Visit

San Juan's temperatures stay between 71-88F (22-31C) year-round, so the variation is less about temperature and more about rain, humidity, and hurricane risk.

The dry season from December to April is peak time for good reason: lower humidity, minimal rain, and no hurricane threat. This is when San Juan is busiest and most expensive.

May is an underrated window: weather is still mostly clear, the tourist crowds thin out, and accommodation prices drop. June through November is the wet season and officially hurricane season (peak risk from August to October). Rain tends to come in afternoon bursts rather than all-day downpours, so mornings are often productive. That said, direct hurricane threats are relatively rare, modern forecasting gives several days of warning, and the island's post-Maria infrastructure investment has improved resilience.

For a first visit focused on remote work, December through May is the clearest window. If you want value over certainty, May through July offers a reasonable compromise.

Pros and Cons

  • No visa hassle for most Western passport holders
  • U.S. infrastructure: dollar payments, Amazon delivery, familiar banking
  • Strong food and nightlife scene for a city its size
  • English widely spoken everywhere in San Juan
  • Year-round warm weather with beach access
  • Growing nomad and startup community in Santurce
  • Power outages are real and can last hours; generator access matters
  • More expensive than other Caribbean or Latin American hubs
  • Car or Uber dependency outside walkable zones
  • Hurricane season risk from August to October
  • Cafe WiFi is unreliable for professional use; coworking is almost mandatory
  • Heat and humidity from May onward make outdoor work impractical

Who San Juan Is Best For

San Juan works well for U.S.-based remote workers who want an island lifestyle without the visa complexity or banking friction of other international destinations. If you value a walkable food and nightlife scene, solid coworking infrastructure, English everywhere, and tropical weather, Santurce in particular delivers that combination.

It also suits nomads who need U.S. tax residency options: Puerto Rico's Act 60 tax incentives attract a significant community of U.S. entrepreneurs and investors, which shapes the social scene in ways that some will find energizing and others will find too finance-heavy.

Consider skipping it if you are on a tight budget, primarily care about reliable power for video production or intensive computing, or want the lower cost-per-quality ratio of Southeast Asia or Eastern Europe. Digital nomads who rely heavily on cafes for deep work, rather than coworking memberships, may also find the setup frustrating.

Frequently Asked Questions About San Juan

How much does it cost to live in San Juan as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in San Juan is around $3,200 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in San Juan?

With a WiFi score of 6/10, connectivity in San Juan is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is San Juan safe for digital nomads?

San Juan scores 4 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is patchy for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is San Juan a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, San Juan is worth a look if you value easy visas and English. Costs sit near $3,200 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
