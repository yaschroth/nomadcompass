Setting Up as a Nomad in Logrono

The Rioja capital, where one street holds forty pincho bars. Logrono pairs safety with food and a mediterranean climate, all for about $1,250 a month. If anything, the size of the nomad scene lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Logrono

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Casco Antiguo and Calle Laurel

$$$
Culinary & Compact

Forty pincho bars in a few hundred metres

The old town, where Calle Laurel and the streets around it hold around forty bars, each specialising in a single pincho, and where the practice is to take one plate and one glass at each and move on. The Camino de Santiago passes through and the co-cathedral of Santa Maria de la Redonda stands at its edge.

Best for: Anyone whose reason for being in Logrono is Calle Laurel
Pros
  • The best pincho crawl in Spain outside San Sebastian
  • Everything historic within a five minute walk
  • The Camino passes directly through
Cons
  • Genuinely loud until late, every night
  • The most expensive rents in the city
  • Impossible during the San Mateo festival in September

El Espolon and the Gran Via

$$$
Bourgeois & Central

The nineteenth century city west of the old town

The district built as Logrono expanded beyond its walls, centred on the Paseo del Espolon, a wide park square with a bandstand where the city gathers, and running west along the Gran Via with the better apartment stock, the shops and the offices along it.

Best for: Longer stays that want good housing and quiet at night
Pros
  • The best apartment stock in the city
  • The Espolon park is the city's living room
  • Five minutes from Calle Laurel without the noise
Cons
  • Expensive by Logrono standards
  • Little architectural interest beyond the park
  • Parking is difficult

The Ebro riverside

$$
Riverside & Quiet

The northern edge, along the river

The area running down to the Ebro, with the stone bridge, the riverside park and the paths that run along both banks, and the Rioja Alta vineyards beginning on the far side. It is quieter than the centre and is where the city walks, runs and cycles.

Best for: Anyone who wants the river paths and a calmer street
Pros
  • Riverside parks and paths in both directions
  • Cheaper than the centre a few streets away
  • The vineyards begin on the far bank
Cons
  • Fewer restaurants and shops
  • Fog off the Ebro in winter
  • Ten minutes from Calle Laurel

Haro

$$
Wine Town & Historic

The Rioja Alta wine capital, about thirty five kilometres west

A small town in the upper Ebro valley that holds the densest concentration of historic Rioja bodegas anywhere, several of them within a few streets of the railway station where they were built in the nineteenth century to load wine straight onto trains bound for France after phylloxera destroyed the French vineyards.

Best for: Anyone whose interest in La Rioja is specifically the wine
Pros
  • More historic bodegas within walking distance than anywhere in Spain
  • Cheaper than Logrono and genuinely handsome
  • The Batalla del Vino wine fight in June is a real spectacle
Cons
  • Thirty five kilometres from Logrono
  • A town of twelve thousand with very little else
  • Bus and train connections are infrequent

Cost of Living in Logrono

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Logrono, so the $1,250 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 981 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,250 a month. A one-bedroom runs roughly $500 to $800, well below Bilbao an hour north and a fraction of Madrid. Market produce is very good and cheap, since La Rioja is agricultural country, and the wine is the obvious point: good Rioja costs a few euros a bottle here. Eating out is where the city excels and it is inexpensive, with the pincho bars on Calle Laurel serving a small plate and a glass for a couple of euros. Utilities are moderate. Anyone comparing with the Basque country should note that San Sebastian, two hours north, costs roughly double for a comparable flat and considerably more to eat in.

Where to Work in Logrono

Connectivity is very good. Spain has extensive fibre at high speeds and low prices, comprehensive mobile coverage and reliable power, and Logrono is well served. There is a small coworking presence and the University of La Rioja adds a young population. The time zone is central European, which in northern Spain is an hour ahead of solar time and produces the famously late Spanish day. Spain's digital nomad visa applies here as everywhere and has brought a substantial number of people to the country, though mostly to the larger cities.

Getting Around Logrono

Logrono is flat and compact and entirely walkable, with the old town, the cathedral and Calle Laurel within a few minutes of each other. Buses cover the rest and cycling is easy along the Ebro. Trains reach Zaragoza in two hours and Madrid in about four, and buses to Bilbao take under two hours, which makes Bilbao airport the practical international connection. The Camino de Santiago passes directly through the city, which means a steady stream of walkers through the centre from spring to autumn. Anyone walking the Camino should know that Logrono is the end of a long stage from Los Arcos and that the municipal albergue fills early in summer.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Logrono

Spain is in the European Union and the Schengen area, so European Union and EEA citizens may live and work here with no permit, registering for an NIE number and on the local padron. Everyone else needs a Schengen visa for short stays or a residence permit for longer ones. Spain operates a digital nomad visa under its 2023 startups law, valid up to a year initially and renewable to five, with an income threshold and a favourable tax regime for qualifying applicants, and it has become one of the most used schemes in Europe.

Best Time to Visit Logrono

May, June, September and October are the best months, warm at around twenty two degrees, with the grape harvest in September and October the region's busiest and most interesting period. July and August are hot at around thirty degrees. Winter is cold for Spain, often near freezing, with the Ebro valley catching fog and the Sierra de Cebollera holding snow. The San Mateo festival in September, which celebrates the harvest and includes the treading of the first grapes, fills the city for a week. Anyone visiting during the September harvest should book ahead, since the wineries run tours through it and the city fills for the San Mateo week.

Pros and Cons of Logrono

The case for Logrono is Calle Laurel, a single street with around forty pincho bars where each specialises in one thing and you move from bar to bar eating and drinking for a few euros at a time, which is a genuinely distinctive way to spend an evening and is why Spaniards come here. The Rioja vineyards are all around, several with architecturally serious wineries. The case against is a small city with limited cultural offer beyond food and wine, a cold winter for Spain, and very little English. La Rioja is also Spain's smallest autonomous community by population, which means the regional government is unusually accessible and the administration is quicker than in the larger regions.

Who Logrono Is For

Logrono suits anyone who takes wine seriously, since this is the centre of Spain's best known region and the wineries are open and welcoming, along with walkers on the Camino, which passes through. It suits people who want Spanish quality of life at northern Spanish prices with a genuinely excellent food scene. It does not suit anyone without Spanish, since English is thin here, and it does not suit anyone who needs a city, because Logrono has about a hundred and fifty thousand people and behaves accordingly. It also suits anyone drawn to dinosaur tracks, since the Cameros hills south of the city hold thousands of preserved footprints and several marked trails between them.
