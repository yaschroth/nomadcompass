Setting Up as a Nomad in Oranjestad

Constant trade winds, a desert coastline and the most reliable weather in the Caribbean. Expect $2,000 a month here. Climate and English are the standout strengths, with the size of the nomad scene the main caveat.

Best Neighborhoods in Oranjestad

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Oranjestad Centre

$$
Central & Colonial

The Dutch colonial capital and its main street

The compact centre around Caya Betico Croes, with pastel Dutch colonial facades, the free streetcar looping through it and the cruise terminal on the water. It is the administrative and commercial heart of Aruba and quieter than the hotel strips.

Best for: Anyone who wants to be in a town rather than a resort
Pros
  • Walkable centre with a free streetcar
  • Cheaper than the beach strips
  • Buses to the whole island from here
Cons
  • Busy on cruise days and dead otherwise
  • The town beach is unremarkable
  • Quiet in the evening

Palm Beach and Noord

$$$$
Resort & Busy

The high-rise hotel strip

The northwestern strip of tall hotels, casinos, restaurants and bars along Palm Beach, with the Noord district behind it holding supermarkets and residential streets. It is the busiest and most commercial part of Aruba and where the nightlife is.

Best for: Anyone who wants restaurants, bars and a beach on the doorstep
Pros
  • The island's restaurants and nightlife
  • Calm swimming and the pier for snorkelling
  • Fisherman's Huts for windsurfing at the north end
Cons
  • The most expensive area on Aruba
  • Crowded and heavily commercial
  • Traffic on the strip in the evening

Eagle Beach

$$$$
Beachfront & Low-rise

Low-rise, wide sand and the divi-divi trees

The stretch of beach south of Palm Beach, kept low-rise by planning rules, with the two wind-bent divi-divi trees that appear on every Aruba postcard. The beach here is wider and considerably less crowded than the high-rise strip.

Best for: Anyone who wants the best beach without the high-rise wall behind it
Pros
  • Wider and quieter than Palm Beach
  • Turtle nesting protected on the sand
  • Low-rise planning keeps the outlook open
Cons
  • Expensive and dominated by timeshares
  • Fewer restaurants than Palm Beach
  • Little to do on foot beyond the beach

San Nicolas

$
Industrial & Artistic

The old refinery town turned mural district

Aruba's second town at the southeastern end, built around the Lago refinery that closed and left it struggling, and reinvented over the last decade as a street art district with large murals across its buildings and a carnival tradition of its own.

Best for: Budget stays and anyone interested in the island's other side
Pros
  • By far the cheapest housing on Aruba
  • Serious street art across the town
  • Baby Beach and Rodgers Beach nearby
Cons
  • Forty minutes from Oranjestad
  • Still visibly post-industrial
  • Limited restaurants and services

Savaneta

$$
Village & Local

The old capital and the fish shacks

A fishing village on the south coast, Aruba's original capital, with Zeerover at its centre, a fish shack where you order by the pound and eat over the water. It is the most local part of the developed island and considerably cheaper.

Best for: Anyone wanting local prices and a quiet coast
Pros
  • Zeerover and genuinely local eating
  • Much cheaper than the beach strips
  • Quiet, shallow water for swimming
Cons
  • Twenty-five minutes from Oranjestad
  • A car is effectively required
  • Almost nothing aimed at visitors

Cost of Living in Oranjestad

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Oranjestad, so the $2,000 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 861 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $2,000 a month as a solo remote worker in Oranjestad, which sits between Curacao and Barbados on price and reflects an island whose economy is tourism from end to end. A one-bedroom flat runs roughly $800 to $1,300, with anything near Palm Beach or Eagle Beach at the top of that and the inland neighbourhoods considerably cheaper. Groceries are imported and the markup is real, though the Dutch supply chain keeps the choice wide. Eating where Arubans eat, at a local snack or the fish shacks, costs a fraction of the resort strip. The florin is pegged at around 1.79 to the dollar and dollars circulate freely. This is our own estimate rather than measured data.

Where to Work in Oranjestad

Aruba's connectivity is good and its power supply reliable, both underwritten by a tourism industry that cannot afford otherwise. Fibre reaches the developed western side of the island and mobile data is inexpensive with complete coverage. Coworking exists in a modest way around Oranjestad, though most remote workers here use apartments and hotel business centres. English is spoken universally alongside Papiamento, Dutch and Spanish, and Arubans switching between four languages in one conversation is entirely normal. The island runs four hours behind central European time, which puts a European afternoon at the start of the Aruban day.

Getting Around Oranjestad

Aruba is small, flat and easy. Oranjestad's centre is walkable and a free streetcar loops through it, and the Arubus network runs frequently up the western coast past the hotel strips to Palm Beach and down towards San Nicolas. That corridor is where almost everything is. A car is needed only for Arikok National Park, which covers nearly a fifth of the island on the wild windward side, and for the Natural Pool and the north coast, where the tracks demand four-wheel drive. Queen Beatrix airport is minutes from town with direct flights to Amsterdam, Miami and much of the eastern United States.

Visas for Oranjestad

Aruba's One Happy Workation programme is a ninety-day remote work arrangement, originally opened to United States nationals and since broadened, with a light application process and a modest fee. It is short by regional standards and closer to a formalised long visit than a residence permit. Dutch nationals have residence rights. Everyone else gets up to ninety days on arrival for most Western nationalities, with extensions possible through the immigration authority. Like Curacao, Aruba is part of the Kingdom of the Netherlands but not of the European Union, so Schengen rules do not apply.

Best Time to Visit Oranjestad

Aruba has the most consistent weather in the Caribbean and it is genuinely a selling point rather than a slogan: the island lies below the hurricane belt, averages under 450 millimetres of rain a year, and sits around thirty-one degrees every month with trade winds that never stop. There is no bad season. The busy and expensive window runs from mid-December to April, when North America arrives, and the quieter, cheaper months are May, June, September and October, with weather that is essentially identical. Carnival, which runs through January and February, is the island's largest event.

Pros and Cons of Oranjestad

Aruba offers the most reliable weather of anywhere in the Caribbean, wide white beaches, constant wind that makes it one of the world's best windsurfing and kitesurfing spots, a desert interior protected as a national park, safety that is genuinely high, and direct flights to Amsterdam and the eastern United States. Against that, it is heavily and visibly built for package tourism, the ninety-day permit is short, the island is small and largely developed along one coast, and there is very little nature outside the park. It is polished rather than characterful.

Who Oranjestad Is For

Oranjestad suits someone who wants guaranteed weather, real safety and easy logistics, and who is happy on a small, developed island for a season rather than a year. Windsurfers and kitesurfers get more from Aruba than from almost anywhere, since the wind is dependable in a way it is nowhere else in the region. It is a poor fit for anyone wanting a long stay, given the ninety-day permit, or anyone hoping for jungle, rivers or mountains, none of which exist here. The eastern coastline is where the island stops looking like a resort.

Frequently Asked Questions About Oranjestad

How much does it cost to live in Oranjestad as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Oranjestad is around $2,000 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Oranjestad?

Oranjestad rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is good for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Oranjestad safe for digital nomads?

Oranjestad scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Oranjestad a good city for digital nomads overall?

Oranjestad stands out for great weather and widely spoken English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
