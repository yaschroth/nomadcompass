Setting Up as a Nomad in Xian

An ancient Silk Road capital ringed by intact city walls, guarded by the Terracotta Army and famous for its Muslim-quarter street food. For remote workers, Xian leans on culture and food, with a typical budget near $1,100 a month. The main trade-off is the language barrier.

Best Neighborhoods in Xian

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Inside the City Walls (Beilin)

$$$
Historic & Walkable

Bell Tower, Muslim Quarter and old lanes

The historic core enclosed by Xi'an's intact Ming walls, centred on the Bell and Drum Towers, with the Muslim Quarter food lanes on one side and quiet grey-brick hutong-style alleys on the other. Two metro lines cross here. Nowhere else in the city is this walkable or this atmospheric.

Best for: Short-stay nomads who want history outside the window and everything on foot.
Pros
  • Metro Lines 1 and 2 interchange at the Bell Tower
  • You can cycle the full circuit of the city wall
  • Endless cheap street food in the Muslim Quarter
Cons
  • Constant tour groups around the Bell Tower
  • Older buildings, thin walls and street noise
  • Winter air quality in the basin is genuinely bad

Xiaozhai

$$
Student & Cheap

Student crossroads and shopping hub

The junction south of the walls where several universities meet a cluster of malls, cinemas and underground shopping streets. Cheap noodle shops, milk tea counters and budget chain hotels line the roads, and the Big Wild Goose Pagoda sits a short walk east. Busy, young and priced for students.

Best for: Budget nomads who want cheap rent, cheap food and a metro stop.
Pros
  • Cheapest decent rooms near the metro
  • Excellent value food at all hours
  • Line 2 and Line 3 both serve the area
Cons
  • Extremely crowded pavements and buses
  • Noisy and short on quiet places to work
  • Little charm, mostly 1990s concrete blocks

Gaoxin (High-Tech Zone)

$$$$
Modern & Corporate

Xi'an's business and tech district

The western high-tech zone along Gaoxin Road and Keji Road, where Xi'an's software firms, multinationals and better hotels concentrate. Modern serviced apartments, specialty coffee, international schools and a couple of decent hospitals sit within a few blocks. This is where most working foreigners in Xi'an end up.

Best for: Remote workers who want new-build apartments, real coffee and reliable services.
Pros
  • Newest apartment stock with lifts and heating
  • Best specialty cafes and coworking in the city
  • Foreign-friendly clinics and international schools
Cons
  • Most expensive rents in Xi'an
  • Wide roads and long blocks make it tiring to walk
  • Roughly 30 minutes from the historic centre

Qujiang New District

$$$
Green & Spacious

Parks, museums and Tang-themed avenues

A planned southeastern district built around Tang Paradise, the Datang Everbright City night street and the Big Wild Goose Pagoda park, with lakes, museums and wide landscaped boulevards. Housing is newer and lower density than the centre, and families dominate. Line 3 and Line 5 connect it back into town.

Best for: Nomads who want quiet, clean streets and parks to run in after work.
Pros
  • Lots of green space, lakes and museums
  • New, well-built apartments at fair prices
  • The best night walking street in the city
Cons
  • The Tang-themed areas get heavily touristed
  • Spread out, you rely on the metro or taxis
  • Thin on independent cafes away from the attractions

Weiyang & North Station

$$
New & Affordable

Newer northern grid by the high-speed rail

The northern administrative district around Xi'an North Railway Station and the Daming Palace ruins park, all new towers, wide avenues and metro Line 2 running straight south into the walls. Rents undercut Gaoxin and Qujiang substantially, and the high-speed rail hub is a few minutes away.

Best for: Nomads who travel by rail often and want cheap, modern space to come home to.
Pros
  • Cheap modern apartments with good light
  • High-speed rail station on the doorstep
  • Daming Palace park for running and cycling
Cons
  • Sterile new-build streets with little atmosphere
  • Around 25 minutes by metro to the old city
  • Dust from ongoing construction, worse in spring

Cost of Living in Xian

Budget around $1,100/month as a solo remote worker in Xian (editorial estimates in USD, converted from RMB), making it one of China's more affordable major cities. A one-bed apartment near the centre inside or close to the ancient city walls runs about $350-480, dropping to $230-330 in outer districts a short metro ride away. Food is a highlight and a bargain: a famous roujiamo (meat sandwich) or bowl of biangbiang noodles is $1.50-3, and a hearty Muslim Quarter feast for one lands under $8. Home cooking from local markets keeps groceries near $160-230. The metro costs roughly $0.30-0.75 a ride, so transport rarely exceeds $25/month. Coworking hot-desks run about $80-130/month, though many work from cafes for a $3-4 coffee. Utilities and fast broadband add $40-65. Xian delivers rich history and excellent food at a noticeably lower cost than the eastern coastal megacities.

Where to Work in Xian

Xian's coworking scene is smaller than Chengdu's or Shanghai's but functional, with hot-desks broadly $80-130/month clustered in the high-tech zone and near the universities in the south of the city. Fibre broadband and 5G are fast and cheap, and mobile data is plentiful. As everywhere in mainland China, the Great Firewall blocks Google, Gmail, WhatsApp, Instagram, and many Western sites, so install and test a reliable VPN before you arrive, keep a backup, and expect occasional instability during video calls. Set up WeChat and Alipay early, since daily life and payments run on them. Cafe-work culture is present, with a decent independent coffee scene in the university districts and the trendier areas near the walls, though staff rarely speak much English, so translation apps are essential. Xian sees fewer foreign remote workers than the coastal hubs, so expect a more local, less English-friendly environment. Always confirm your VPN before scheduling meetings.

Getting Around Xian

Xian has an excellent, cheap, and expanding metro system, with modern trains and fares around $0.30-0.75 that connect the walled centre, the high-speed rail station, and the airport line. The historic core inside the Ming-era city walls is compact and walkable, and you can even cycle the top of the walls themselves. Beyond the centre the city sprawls, so the metro plus Didi (China's Uber) covers the gaps; Didi is easy once linked to WeChat or Alipay, with typical rides $3-6. Bike-share handles short hops. You do not need a car. Xianyang International Airport, northwest of the city, is well connected by a dedicated metro line and airport buses and handles growing domestic and some international routes. High-speed rail links Xian to Chengdu, Beijing, and Luoyang in a few hours. The Terracotta Army is reachable by public bus or Didi. Between metro, Didi, and bikes, getting around is cheap and straightforward.

Visas for Xian

China expanded visa-free access substantially in 2024-2025, offering unilateral visa-free entry of up to 30 days to passport holders from many countries, including much of the EU plus Australia, New Zealand, Japan, and others; confirm whether your nationality is currently on the list, as it keeps changing. A separate transit visa-free policy allows eligible travelers to stay up to 240 hours (10 days) when transiting to a third country, and Xian is among the covered entry points. There is no dedicated digital nomad visa, and these entries are officially for tourism, business, or transit rather than employment, so remote work for a foreign employer occupies a grey area best kept low-profile. Longer stays require a proper visa (such as M or F) arranged in advance through a Chinese embassy or consulate. Because eligibility, durations, and covered ports of entry change frequently, always verify the current rules for your passport on official Chinese government sources before you travel.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit Xian

Xian has a temperate continental climate with four distinct seasons, more variation than the humid southern cities. The best times to visit are spring (April-May) and autumn (September-October), when days are mild and dry, roughly 60-77F, with clearer skies and comfortable sightseeing weather for the walls and Terracotta Army. Summers (June-August) are hot and can be humid, with highs in the upper 80s to mid-90s F and occasional heavy rain, plus dusty haze. Winters (December-February) are cold and dry, with highs near 40F, freezing nights, and some of China's worst seasonal air pollution as coal heating and the sheltered basin location trap smog; this is the main season to avoid if air quality concerns you. Spring can bring occasional dust from the north. Overall, aim for the April-May or September-October shoulder windows for the most pleasant temperatures and the cleanest air.

Pros and Cons of Xian

Pros: Xian is affordable even by Chinese standards, safe, and steeped in history as an ancient capital, with the Terracotta Army, intact city walls, and the atmospheric Muslim Quarter on your doorstep. The food is outstanding and cheap, blending Shaanxi and Silk Road flavours. The metro is excellent and inexpensive, ride-hailing is easy, and high-speed rail connects you across the country. It offers a deeply local Chinese experience at low cost. Cons: the Great Firewall makes a reliable VPN mandatory for Western services, and English is spoken far less than in coastal hubs, so the language barrier is real. Winter air pollution can be severe, and winters are cold with poor indoor heating in some buildings. The foreign nomad community is small, daily life runs entirely on Chinese apps, and long-term visas remain difficult. Summers are hot and hazy. It is more historic than cosmopolitan.

Who Xian Is For

Xian suits the history-loving, budget-focused nomad who wants an authentic inland Chinese base rather than a glossy coastal expat scene. It is ideal if you are fascinated by ancient China, enjoy exploring imperial sites and Silk Road culture on your doorstep, and see hearty Shaanxi food as a daily draw. The rock-bottom cost of living lets a modest income go a long way, and the excellent metro and rail links make it a comfortable hub for exploring central China. It fits independent, culturally curious travelers who are comfortable with a significant language barrier and treat the VPN and app setup as routine. It is a poor fit for anyone needing a large English-speaking community, seamless Western connectivity, clean winter air, or an easy long-stay visa. Come to Xian for history, food, and value; be ready for a very local, less international day-to-day experience.
