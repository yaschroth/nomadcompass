Setting Up as a Nomad in San Pedro de Atacama

An adobe oasis town in the world's driest desert, a base for the Valle de la Luna, salt flats, geysers, and star-filled skies. What pulls nomads here is nature, backed by clean air and safety. Budget around $1,100 a month. The size of the nomad scene is the weak spot.

Best Neighborhoods in San Pedro de Atacama

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Town Center (Caracoles & the Plaza)

moderate-high
Bustling, touristy, sociable and central

The walkable adobe heart of San Pedro

The compact grid around Caracoles street, the main plaza and the historic adobe church is where San Pedro's energy concentrates. Tour agencies, restaurants, bars, hostels and shops line dusty pedestrian-friendly streets, putting everything within a short stroll. It is convenient and lively, if touristy and pricier.

Best for: First-timers who want to walk everywhere and book tours easily
Pros
  • Everything within a short walk, including tours and dining
  • Easy to meet other travellers and join excursions
  • Best concentration of cafes, wifi spots and services
Cons
  • Most touristy and expensive part of town
  • Can feel busy and dusty at peak hours

Solcor & Sequitor edges

moderate
Peaceful, residential, rural-edge and starry

Quieter adobe lanes on the town's fringe

Toward the Solcor and Sequitor ayllus, the town thins into calmer dirt lanes, small cabanas and guesthouses set among tamarugo trees and irrigation channels. It trades the buzz of Caracoles for silence and stars, a short walk or bike ride from the center, and often better value on longer stays.

Best for: Slow travellers and stargazers wanting quiet and space
Pros
  • Much quieter and darker for stargazing
  • Often better value on longer stays
  • Still walkable or a short bike to the center
Cons
  • Fewer restaurants and services nearby
  • Wifi can be even more variable out here

Road toward Valle de la Luna

moderate-high
Scenic, isolated, desert-immersed and dramatic

Desert-facing stays near the Valley of the Moon

Along the western road heading out toward the Valle de la Luna and the Cordillera de la Sal, a scattering of lodges and cabanas sit closer to open desert. You get sunset views over surreal salt formations and quick access to the valley, at the cost of being a drive or long bike from town.

Best for: Photographers and those prioritising landscape over convenience
Pros
  • Close to Valle de la Luna and stunning sunsets
  • Open desert scenery and big skies
  • Away from town noise and crowds
Cons
  • Need a bike or vehicle for most trips into town
  • Very limited dining and shops nearby

Calama (nearby gateway city)

budget-moderate
Functional, urban, workmanlike and unglamorous

The practical regional hub with the airport

About 1.5 hours away, Calama is the mining city that serves as the region's transport and services hub, home to airport CJC, larger supermarkets, banks and more reliable connectivity. It lacks San Pedro's charm and desert magic, but works as a cheaper, better-connected base for errands, stocking up or steadier remote work.

Best for: Nomads needing reliable wifi, supplies and the airport
Pros
  • Airport CJC and long-distance transport connections
  • Bigger supermarkets, banks and generally cheaper goods
  • More reliable internet than San Pedro
Cons
  • Little charm and far from the desert attractions
  • 1.5-hour bus each way to reach San Pedro's sights

Cost of Living in San Pedro de Atacama

San Pedro de Atacama is pricier than its size suggests. It is a remote oasis town where almost everything is trucked in across the desert and where tourism sets the rates, so expect to pay more than you would in a comparable small Chilean town. A lean but comfortable month for a nomad typically lands around $900 to $1,700, depending on how much you eat out and how many excursions you book. Groceries and restaurant meals carry a visible remoteness premium, and the headline cost of a stay here is really the tours: geysers, salt flats and lagoon trips add up fast.

  • Budget hostel bed or basic room: roughly $18 to $40 per night; simple private stays and cabanas run higher.
  • A casual restaurant meal on Caracoles street: around $12 to $22; wine and craft beer add up.
  • Group excursions (Valle de la Luna, Tatio geysers, Salar lagoons): commonly $25 to $70 each.
  • Local groceries cost more than in Calama or Santiago; stock up in Calama if you pass through.

Where to Work in San Pedro de Atacama

Be honest with yourself before treating San Pedro as a work base: this is a remote high-desert town, and connectivity is variable. Fibre has reached parts of town, but many properties still lean on shared or slower connections, and wifi can drop or crawl at busy hours or after weather events. There is no real coworking scene, so your setup will be your accommodation plus a few laptop-friendly cafes. Buy a local prepaid SIM from Entel or Movistar (Entel generally has the strongest desert coverage) as a mobile-data backup, and top up enough for a hotspot. When booking a stay, ask directly about the actual wifi speed rather than trusting a listing that simply says wifi is available.

  • Get an Entel or Movistar prepaid SIM in Calama or in town for hotspot backup.
  • Ask your host for a real speed check before committing to a longer stay.
  • Cafes and a few bakeries around Caracoles work for short sessions and calls.
  • Plan deep-focus work for mornings; afternoons and evenings are tour and social time.

Getting Around San Pedro de Atacama

The town itself is tiny and made for walking. The compact grid of adobe streets around the plaza and Caracoles is easily covered on foot, and many people rent a bicycle to reach nearby sights like the Pukara de Quitor or the edge of the Valle de la Luna. Everything beyond that, the El Tatio geysers, the Salar de Atacama and its flamingo lagoons, the altiplanic lagoons and Rainbow Valley, is reached by tour van or 4x4, either on an organised excursion or a rented vehicle. Regular buses connect San Pedro to Calama (about 1.5 hours) and its airport (CJC), with long-distance coaches to Santiago and cross-border routes to Argentina and Bolivia. The Uyuni salt-flat crossing into Bolivia departs from here.

  • Walk the town; rent a bike for closer desert sights and viewpoints.
  • Book tour vans or 4x4s for geysers, salt flats and high-altitude lagoons.
  • Buses run to Calama and airport CJC; long-distance to Santiago, Argentina and Bolivia.
  • Respect the altitude and intense sun: pace yourself, hydrate, and cover up.

Visas for San Pedro de Atacama

San Pedro de Atacama follows standard Chilean entry rules, since it is simply a town in the Antofagasta region rather than a special zone. Many nationalities, including most Western passport holders, can enter Chile visa-free as tourists for a stay commonly up to 90 days, receiving a tourist card on arrival that you should keep for your departure. Chile does not currently offer a dedicated digital-nomad visa, so remote workers here are travelling on the ordinary tourist entry. Rules, permitted durations and any reciprocity requirements change and depend entirely on your nationality, so treat this as general editorial orientation and confirm the current details with official Chilean government sources or a consulate before you travel.

  • Chilean tourist entry commonly allows up to 90 days for many nationalities.
  • Keep the tourist card (PDI slip) you receive on arrival for exit.
  • No dedicated digital-nomad visa exists; remote workers use tourist status.
  • This is editorial guidance, not legal advice; verify with official sources.

Best Time to Visit San Pedro de Atacama

This is the driest non-polar desert on Earth, so it is dry and sunny almost year-round, and the choice is really about temperature and altiplano conditions rather than rain in town. The shoulder seasons, roughly March to May and September to November, tend to be the most pleasant, with mild days and clear skies. Winter (June to August) brings very cold nights, which is a gift for stargazing under some of the clearest skies anywhere, but you will want serious warm layers for pre-dawn geyser trips. The so-called Bolivian winter of January and February can bring afternoon storms and rain to the high altiplano, occasionally affecting or closing some of the higher-altitude lagoon and geyser tours.

  • March to May and September to November: mild, clear and comfortable.
  • June to August: freezing nights, superb stargazing, bring heavy layers.
  • January to February: altiplano rains can disrupt some high-altitude tours.
  • Sun is intense at altitude every month; sunscreen and a hat are essential.

Pros and Cons of San Pedro de Atacama

San Pedro is a bucket-list base wrapped in a small, dusty town, and knowing that balance up front saves disappointment. On the upside, you are living beside some of the most surreal landscapes on the planet, with world-class stargazing, clean dry air, a walkable centre and a decent sense of safety. On the downside, it is expensive for its size, wholly dependent on tourism, and short on the infrastructure a productive remote worker leans on: wifi is inconsistent, the scene is transient, and altitude and dryness take a physical toll. Treat it as a place to slow down and explore rather than to grind out a heavy work sprint, and it delivers beautifully.

  • Pros: astonishing desert and altiplano scenery, elite stargazing, clean air, walkable and fairly safe.
  • Pros: a natural launch point for Uyuni, the Salar and world-famous observatories.
  • Cons: variable and sometimes slow wifi, high prices, total tourism dependence.
  • Cons: altitude, extreme dryness, cold nights and a small transient community.

Who San Pedro de Atacama Is For

San Pedro de Atacama is for the astronomy-loving, landscape-hungry slow traveller more than the deadline-driven digital nomad. If your idea of a good month is watching the Milky Way rise over the desert, ticking off geysers and salt flats, and letting work take a back seat around excursions, this town rewards you richly. If you need reliable high-speed internet, a dependable coworking desk and a stable long-term community, you will find it thin here and should base yourself somewhere larger, treating San Pedro as a memorable one to two week detour instead. Come with realistic expectations about connectivity, cost and altitude, lean into the desert rhythm, and it becomes one of the most unforgettable stops on any South American route.

  • Ideal for stargazers, photographers and desert-and-altiplano explorers.
  • Great as a slow-travel base woven around tours rather than a work hub.
  • Less suited to heavy remote-work sprints needing fast, reliable wifi.
  • Best for travellers comfortable with altitude, dryness and higher costs.
