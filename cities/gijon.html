Experience asturian coast with cider, surf, and authentic Spanish life. Plan on roughly $1,580 a month. Its strengths are nature and safety, while the language barrier is where it scores lower.

Gijón sits comfortably below Madrid and Barcelona in price, making it one of the more affordable mid-size Spanish cities for remote workers. Rent is the biggest variable: a furnished one-bedroom in the central districts (El Centro, Cimadevilla, La Arena) runs roughly $865-1,040/month. Move ten minutes out toward Pumarín or La Calzada and the same space drops to $690-805. Shared flats in the center start around $405-520 per room.

A realistic solo remote worker budget lands between $1,610-2,190/month including rent, depending on your neighborhood and how often you eat out. Gijón's cider culture keeps bar tabs low: a bottle of sidra naturale costs $3.50-4.50 at most sidrerías.

Where to Work

Gijón has a small but functional coworking scene. Bri Coworking Gijón, on Calle de la Muralla a block from San Lorenzo Beach, is the most established option, with 300 Mbps fiber, printing, bike parking, and flexible morning/afternoon/full-day passes ($195/month full-time). WorkINGijón in the city center is compact but well-connected, with no hourly restrictions. Espora Centro de Negocios near Plaza Mayor offers a more traditional business center setup with a receptionist and fast broadband.

Café culture here is more about conversation than laptops. You will find cafes tolerant of a working session with a coffee, but dedicated "laptop café" culture is not strong. A few places near the university and around Marqués de San Esteban do accommodate it. For serious focus time, the coworking spaces are a better bet. Public WiFi exists in the main squares but is unreliable for video calls.

Home fiber broadband is fast and cheap ($26-35/month for 600 Mbps plans), so many nomads simply work from their apartments for sustained focus and use coworking for social days or meetings.

Getting Around

The city center is compact and walkable. You can cover most of it on foot: San Lorenzo Beach to Cimadevilla is about a 15-minute walk, and the main commercial streets, markets, and coworking spaces cluster within an easy radius. A bike works well given the flat coastal terrain, and there is a public bicycle scheme.

The city bus network (TUA) covers the whole municipality reliably. A single fare is $1.50 and a monthly pass is $35, handling any trip you cannot walk. Taxis start at around $5.50 base fare; an end-to-end city trip costs roughly $9.

Asturias Airport (OVD) sits about 40 km west of Gijón. ALSA runs direct buses roughly every 30-60 minutes; the trip takes around 45 minutes and costs $3.50-7. A taxi or rideshare runs $46-63. The airport has scheduled flights to Madrid, Barcelona, and several European cities, though connections are limited compared with major hubs. Gijón's RENFE rail station links to Oviedo (25 min) and longer routes to Madrid (about 4.5 hours by express train).

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

EU and EEA citizens can live and work in Spain without a visa and must register after three months. US, Canadian, UK, and Australian passport holders get 90 days visa-free within a 180-day Schengen window, which is the standard tourist stay limit and cannot be extended within the zone.

Spain's Digital Nomad Visa (Visado para Nómadas Digitales) launched in 2023 under the Startup Law. It targets remote workers and freelancers whose income comes primarily (at least 80%) from non-Spanish sources. The income requirement for 2026 is $3,280/month for a single applicant. You apply at a Spanish consulate abroad for a one-year visa, or from within Spain in your first 90 days for a three-year residence authorization. Approval typically takes 20-45 working days. Approved holders can access the Beckham Law flat 24% tax rate. Always verify current requirements and income thresholds at the official Spanish immigration authority (extranjeros.inclusion.gob.es) before applying.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit

Gijón sits on the Cantabrian coast of northern Spain, which means a temperate oceanic climate very different from the dry south. Rain is a constant companion through autumn and winter (October through February is the wettest stretch). The city sees around 1,300 mm of rain per year.

For remote work, June through September is the sweet spot. Temperatures sit between 18-23°C (64-73°F), days are long, and the beaches are usable. July is the driest month. August gets crowded and a little warmer, and the city fills with Spanish summer tourists, which pushes café prices up slightly.

May and late September/October are shoulder options worth considering: fewer crowds, comfortable temperatures, and lower accommodation prices. Winter (November to March) is mild by northern European standards (rarely below 8°C), but grey, rainy, and with fewer visitors. It can work if you want cheap rent and empty streets, but the lack of sunlight affects many people's productivity and mood over a long stay.

Pros and Cons

  • Genuine city life at mid-tier prices: real restaurants, culture, festivals, and a university town energy without paying Barcelona rates
  • San Lorenzo Beach is walkable from the center: one of the best urban beaches in northern Spain, great for a mid-day break
  • Excellent Asturian food scene: sidra, fabada, seafood, and cheeseboards at honest prices
  • Low crime, relaxed pace: feels safe, unpretentious, and authentic rather than tourist-packaged
  • Good rail and bus links: Oviedo is 25 minutes away; Madrid reachable by overnight train
  • Fast, affordable home internet: fiber coverage is widespread in the city
  • Rain and overcast skies are frequent: if you need sun for mental health, this climate will frustrate you
  • Small coworking scene: fewer than five serious spaces; options are limited if you want variety
  • English is not widely spoken: outside hotels and tourist spots, Spanish (and Asturian) is the working language; basic Spanish is genuinely necessary for daily life
  • Flight connections are limited: Asturias Airport serves a handful of routes; travel abroad often means connecting through Madrid or Barcelona
  • Digital nomad community is small: you will not find an established nomad infrastructure the way you would in Valencia or Malaga

Who Gijón Is Best For

Gijón rewards people who want to actually live somewhere rather than nomad-hop. It suits remote workers who are drawn to northern Spain's landscape and culture: green hills, seafood, cider, a genuine port city vibe, and a slower social pace than the Mediterranean south. You will fit in well if you speak some Spanish, enjoy walking and the coast, and do not depend on a large English-speaking community or a busy nomad scene.

It is also a solid choice for EU citizens who want affordable Spanish city life without the tourist saturation of Barcelona or the price tag of Madrid, and for anyone on the Spanish Digital Nomad Visa who wants a quieter base.

Skip Gijón if you need consistent sunshine year-round, rely on a built-out coworking ecosystem, or want a city where English gets you through most situations without effort. Nomads who prefer warm weather, a large international community, or frequent cheap flights abroad will find Valencia, Malaga, or Las Palmas a better match.

Frequently Asked Questions About Gijón

How much does it cost to live in Gijón as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,580 a month in Gijón for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Gijón?

Gijón rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Gijón safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Gijón is 8/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Gijón a good city for digital nomads overall?

Gijón stands out for nature and safety among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,580 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.

Which area of Gijon is best for digital nomads?

Cimadevilla, the old fishing quarter on the headland, is the most characterful base, while the center around the marina and San Lorenzo beach is convenient and walkable. The city is compact and flat along the seafront, so most of it is easy to cover on foot or by bike.

Do I need a visa to work remotely from Gijon?

Gijon is in Spain and the Schengen Area, so many visitors can stay up to 90 days in any 180 without a visa, and EU citizens can stay freely. For longer stays, Spain's digital nomad visa is an option for non-EU remote workers. Check the current requirements for your passport first.

Is Gijon safe for digital nomads?

Gijon is widely considered one of the safer, more relaxed Spanish cities, comfortable for solo travel and long stays with normal precautions. Its northern, coastal setting gives it a calm, local feel rather than a party-town atmosphere.

What is Gijon like compared to southern Spain?

This is green Spain: Asturias is lush and cooler, with more rain and milder summers than the Mediterranean south. Expect a Atlantic beach city with strong cider and seafood culture, fewer tourists, and a genuinely local pace, which suits nomads who want authenticity over sun-guaranteed heat.
