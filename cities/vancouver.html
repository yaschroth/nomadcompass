Cost of Living in Vancouver

Be honest with yourself before you commit: Vancouver is one of the most expensive cities in North America, and rent is where that hits hardest. All prices below are in Canadian dollars, with a rough US dollar figure in parentheses (roughly 1 CAD equals 0.73 USD in 2025, so check the current rate).

  • Rent, central one-bedroom (downtown, West End, Yaletown): CAD $2,300-2,800 (about USD $1,680-2,040) per month. Coal Harbour and other premium pockets run higher.
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Burnaby, New Westminster, Surrey, farther east): CAD $1,800-2,200 (about USD $1,310-1,610), with a longer commute in exchange.
  • Groceries for one person: CAD $350-450 (about USD $255-330). Staples like milk, eggs, and bread cost more here than elsewhere in Canada.
  • Eating out: a mid-range restaurant meal runs CAD $20-30 (about USD $15-22) per person; a fast-food combo is around CAD $15 (about USD $11).
  • Coworking hot-desk: roughly CAD $350-450 (about USD $255-330) per month, or a day pass in the CAD $30-45 range.
  • Local transport: a two-zone monthly Compass pass is about CAD $149 (about USD $109); most short trips run CAD $2.70-4.00 with stored value.

Realistically, a single remote worker renting a central one-bedroom should budget CAD $3,500-4,800 per month (about USD $2,550-3,500) all in. You can push the lower end by living outside downtown, cooking at home, and skipping a paid desk. This is not a city where you save money by accident.

Where to Work

Vancouver has a deep coworking scene. WeWork runs several downtown locations (including 333 Seymour Street), and Spaces operates hot desks across multiple neighborhoods including near Marine Gateway. Both offer hot desks, dedicated desks, day passes, and meeting rooms with fast WiFi, coffee, and phone booths. If you want to sample several spaces without committing, aggregators like Deskpass and Coworker list day passes across the city.

Cafe-work culture is strong, especially on the East Side and in Mount Pleasant, Commercial Drive, and Kitsilano, where independent coffee shops are used to laptops. Etiquette is to buy something regularly and free up your table at peak times. WiFi is genuinely good across the city: cafes, libraries, and coworking spaces are reliable, and home internet is fast if a little pricey. Vancouver Public Library branches are a solid free fallback for a quiet, connected afternoon.

Getting Around

Metro Vancouver runs on TransLink, and it is one of the better transit systems in North America. The SkyTrain (Expo, Millennium, and Canada lines) is fully automated and frequent, buses fill the gaps, and the SeaBus ferry links downtown to North Vancouver in about 12 minutes. Fares are zone-based: pick up a reloadable Compass Card or just tap a contactless credit card or phone at the gate. Stored-value fares (roughly CAD $2.70 for one zone up to CAD $5.10 for three) beat cash, and a two-zone monthly pass sits around CAD $149.

The core of the city is very walkable and bike-friendly, with the Seawall and protected lanes making car-free life realistic downtown, in the West End, and on the East Side. To and from the airport, the Canada Line connects YVR to downtown Waterfront Station in about 26 minutes. Note the YVR AddFare surcharge when leaving the airport, which pushes the trip downtown to roughly CAD $8.35 in 2025 (this fee was set to rise, so check current pricing).

Visas and Staying Long-Term

For citizens of many visa-exempt countries (including much of the EU, the UK, Australia, Japan, and others), visiting Canada as a tourist normally allows a stay of up to six months. If you are arriving by air, you will usually need an Electronic Travel Authorization (eTA), which costs CAD $7, is linked to your passport, and stays valid for up to five years or until the passport expires. US citizens do not need an eTA. An eTA does not grant the right to work or study, and the length of stay is ultimately decided by the border officer.

If you want to stay beyond your authorized period, you can apply to extend your visitor status from inside Canada before it expires. Rules vary by nationality and change often, so confirm your specific situation and any work-related restrictions with the official Government of Canada immigration website before you travel.

Best Time to Visit

Vancouver has a mild oceanic climate, which is a polite way of saying it rarely gets very cold and it rains a great deal. The wet season runs roughly October through April, and November is the wettest month. Annual rainfall is around 2,000mm plus, so grey, drizzly winters are the norm rather than the exception. If constant overcast weather drags you down, this matters more than the temperature.

The clear winner for remote work is June through September, when the city dries out and turns genuinely beautiful: long days, sunny stretches, mountains and ocean on full display, and July and August as the driest months. Late spring (May) and early fall (September) are pleasant shoulder seasons with fewer crowds. If you can time a stay, come for the summer and you will see why people put up with the winters and the prices.

Pros and Cons

What you gain:

  • Stunning natural setting with ocean, mountains, and trails minutes from downtown.
  • Excellent public transit and a walkable, bike-friendly core.
  • Reliable, fast WiFi and a mature coworking and cafe scene.
  • Clean, safe, English-speaking, with a strong food scene and diverse communities.
  • Glorious, dry summers that are hard to beat anywhere.

What you trade:

  • Very high cost of living, with rent among the steepest in North America.
  • A genuine housing crunch: finding a good short-term rental is competitive and expensive.
  • Long, grey, rainy winters that wear on many people.
  • Nightlife and city energy are more low-key than in comparably sized cities.

Who Vancouver Is Best For

Vancouver suits remote workers with a comfortable budget who value the outdoors above all: if you want to finish work and be hiking, paddling, or skiing within the hour, few cities compete. It rewards people who prefer a calm, clean, well-run city over a cheap or chaotic one, and it works especially well for a summer stay.

Skip it, or at least think twice, if you are on a tight budget, if you need constant sunshine to stay motivated, or if you want a low-cost base to stretch your income. Nomads chasing affordability or nonstop nightlife will find better value elsewhere. Come for the setting and the summers, and go in with eyes open about the cost and the rain.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vancouver

How much does it cost to live in Vancouver as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $4,000 a month in Vancouver for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Vancouver?

Vancouver rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Vancouver safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Vancouver is 7/10, good for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Vancouver a good city for digital nomads overall?

Vancouver stands out for nature and English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $4,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
