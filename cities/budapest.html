Setting Up as a Nomad in Budapest

Experience thermal baths by day, ruin bars by night, Central Europe's hidden gem. For remote workers, Budapest leans on nightlife and culture, with a typical budget near $2,200 a month. The main trade-off is air quality.

Cost of Living in Budapest

Budapest remains one of Europe's best-value capital cities for remote workers, though prices have crept up in central districts as more foreigners arrive. Here is what to expect on a monthly basis.

  • Rent, city center (Districts V, VI, VII): 160,000-230,000 HUF (~$430-620 USD) for a one-bedroom
  • Rent, outer districts (IX, XI, XIII): 100,000-150,000 HUF (~$270-400 USD) for a one-bedroom
  • Groceries: 40,000-60,000 HUF (~$110-160 USD) cooking at home regularly
  • Eating out: A sit-down lunch at a local restaurant runs 2,000-4,000 HUF ($5-11 USD); dinner at a mid-range place 5,000-10,000 HUF ($13-27 USD)
  • Coworking hot-desk: 30,000-70,000 HUF (~$80-190 USD) per month; daily passes 3,000-6,000 HUF ($8-16 USD)
  • Local transport monthly pass: 8,950 HUF (~$24 USD)
  • Utilities (electricity, heating, water): 25,000-40,000 HUF (~$67-108 USD) depending on season

Total monthly range: A frugal nomad in an outer-district apartment spending carefully can get by on $1,100-1,400 USD. A comfortable central setup with regular dinners out and a proper coworking membership runs $1,700-2,500 USD. Budapest stays well below equivalent Western European cities at every budget level.

Where to Work

Budapest has a solid and growing coworking scene. KAPTÁR, just steps from the Basilica in District V, is the city's most established space: enterprise-grade WiFi, a strong freelancer community, and flexible passes from around 5,500 HUF per day. Puzl CowOrKing, a tech-focused space in District XIII, opened in a renovated factory in 2024 and offers 800 seats with ergonomic furniture and 24/7 access. Szikra Coworking in District VII is a smaller, community-oriented option popular with creatives.

Cafe culture here is genuinely laptop-friendly, more so than in many European cities. Madal Cafe (multiple locations in District V) is the go-to: fast WiFi, quality specialty coffee, generous table space, and no real pressure to leave after one drink. Massolit Books and Cafe in the Jewish Quarter offers a relaxed, bookshop atmosphere and good connectivity. Lumen Cafe in the Palace Quarter draws a creative crowd and has ample seating.

WiFi quality across Budapest is genuinely strong. Hungary ranks in the top ten globally for internet speeds, and you will rarely struggle to find a reliable connection. Cafes and coworking spaces alike deliver speeds comfortable for video calls. The main catch: some popular cafes fill up by late morning on weekdays, so arriving before 10am secures the best spots.

Getting Around

Budapest's public transit is cheap, frequent, and covers almost everywhere you need to go. The network includes four metro lines, extensive tram routes, and buses running through the night. A single ticket costs 500 HUF ($1.35 USD), and a monthly pass is just 8,950 HUF ($24 USD), which is genuinely one of the best transit deals in Europe. Validate every ticket: inspectors are common and the on-the-spot fine is steep.

The city is highly walkable, particularly the flat Pest side. Districts V through VIII sit close together and many nomads find they rarely need transit at all during a typical workday. Cycling is increasingly viable thanks to a growing network of lanes and the MOL Bubi bike-share scheme.

Airport transfers: Budapest Ferenc Liszt Airport is about 16km from the city center. The 100E Airport Express bus is the cheapest option at 2,500 HUF ($6.70 USD) for a single ticket, running to Deak Ferenc ter in around 35-45 minutes. Taxis and rideshares (Bolt is widely used) cost roughly 6,000-10,000 HUF ($16-27 USD) depending on traffic and destination.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

As an EU member state, Hungary is part of the Schengen Area. Passport holders from the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and most other Western countries can enter visa-free for up to 90 days within any 180-day period across the entire Schengen zone.

Hungary offers a dedicated White Card, a digital nomad residence permit for non-EU nationals. It allows you to live legally in Hungary for one year, with a one-year renewal option (two years total). Requirements include proof of remote income averaging at least EUR 2,000 per month over the six months before application, comprehensive health insurance, and proof of accommodation. You cannot work for Hungarian clients on this permit. EU and EEA citizens have the right to reside freely and can register as residents without a permit.

Always verify current requirements with official sources before travel. Check the Hungarian Office for Immigration and Asylum (oif.gov.hu) and your country's Hungarian embassy for the most current rules.

Best Time to Visit

Spring (March to May) and early autumn (September to October) are the best windows for remote work in Budapest. Temperatures sit between 12°C and 22°C (54-72°F), the city is not yet overwhelmed with tourists, and outdoor cafe seating is actually pleasant. May is particularly good: long days, markets in full swing, and a lively energy without the summer heat.

Summer (June to August) brings reliable warmth and Budapest's outdoor festival season, but temperatures can push past 35°C (95°F) in July and August, and the most popular coworking cafes get crowded with visitors. If you run hot or need to concentrate, summer is the hardest season to enjoy working here.

Winter (December to February) is cold and often grey, with temperatures near or below freezing. That said, it has real appeal: the thermal bath culture comes into its own, Christmas markets run through December, and the city is far less crowded. As long as you are comfortable commuting in cold weather, it is a productive time to be here.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Very low cost of living by European capital standards
  • Pro: Top-tier internet speeds and solid coworking infrastructure
  • Pro: Rich cultural life: thermal baths, architecture, excellent food and wine scene
  • Pro: Safe city, walkable center, cheap and reliable public transit
  • Pro: English widely spoken among under-40s in central areas
  • Pro: White Card gives non-EU nomads a legal path to stay up to two years
  • Con: Locals can be reserved and it takes time to build genuine social connections
  • Con: Summers are increasingly hot and sometimes uncomfortably so for work
  • Con: Central rents have risen sharply and landlords increasingly target short-term foreign tenants
  • Con: Smoking culture is still common in bars and some outdoor spaces
  • Con: The political environment is polarizing, and some find the media landscape limiting
  • Con: Language barrier with older generations; Hungarian is notoriously difficult to pick up

Who Budapest Is Best For

Budapest suits nomads who want a high quality of life at a manageable cost without leaving Europe. If you care about history, architecture, food, and nightlife, and you want reliable infrastructure without paying London or Amsterdam prices, this city consistently delivers. It works especially well for solo nomads or couples with a moderate budget, people applying for or already holding a White Card, and those doing a longer Central European stint who want a comfortable base with good flight connections.

It is less ideal for people who thrive on easily making local friends or who need a large, active English-speaking nomad community to feel settled. Those who dislike cold winters or hate working in summer heat will also find the seasonal extremes frustrating. If you are chasing Southeast Asia costs or a beach lifestyle, Budapest will feel like a compromise.

Frequently Asked Questions About Budapest

How much does it cost to live in Budapest as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Budapest is around $2,200 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Budapest?

With a WiFi score of 8/10, connectivity in Budapest is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Budapest safe for digital nomads?

Budapest scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Budapest a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Budapest is worth a look if you value nightlife and culture. Costs sit near $2,200 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
