Setting Up as a Nomad in Port of Spain

Where steelpan and soca were invented, and the Caribbean's biggest carnival still runs the calendar. What pulls nomads here is English, backed by culture and nightlife. Budget around $1,400 a month. The size of the nomad scene is the weak spot.

Best Neighborhoods in Port of Spain

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Woodbrook

$$$
Lively & Central

The old suburb turned restaurant and bar district

A grid of gingerbread houses west of the centre, once residential and now the city's concentration of restaurants, bars and small offices, with Ariapita Avenue running through it. It fills every weekend and is the heart of Trinidad's going out.

Best for: Anyone who wants the city's nightlife and restaurants within walking distance
Pros
  • Ariapita Avenue's bars and restaurants
  • Walking distance to the Savannah and the centre
  • Gingerbread architecture through the side streets
Cons
  • Noisy at weekends
  • Parking is difficult
  • Prices above the city average

St Clair

$$$$
Established & Green

The embassy quarter beside the Savannah

The most desirable residential district, on the western side of the Queen's Park Savannah, with large old houses, embassies and the Magnificent Seven, a row of extravagant colonial mansions along the park's edge. It is quiet, green and expensive.

Best for: Longer stays wanting safety, space and the Savannah on the doorstep
Pros
  • The Savannah for running and walking
  • Safest part of the city
  • The Magnificent Seven and the Botanic Gardens
Cons
  • The most expensive rents in Port of Spain
  • Mostly houses rather than flats
  • Quiet in the evening

Newtown

$$$
Mixed & Practical

Between the Savannah and downtown

A mixed district of offices, apartment blocks and older houses sitting between St Clair and the commercial centre, with the Queen's Hall theatre and several of the city's panyards nearby. It is the practical compromise between price and position.

Best for: Anyone wanting a central position without St Clair prices
Pros
  • Walking distance to both the Savannah and downtown
  • Cheaper than St Clair for the same access
  • Panyards nearby in the Carnival season
Cons
  • Mixed in character, some blocks are tired
  • Traffic on the main routes
  • Fewer restaurants than Woodbrook

Cascade and St Ann's

$$$
Suburban & Cool

Up the valley, cooler and quieter

Residential valleys running north from the Savannah into the Northern Range foothills, with older houses, more greenery and noticeably cooler air than the flat city below. The President's House and the Botanic Gardens sit at the valley mouth.

Best for: Anyone wanting quiet, green surroundings and a short drive to the centre
Pros
  • Cooler and greener than the city
  • Very quiet residential streets
  • Ten minutes to the Savannah
Cons
  • A car is effectively required
  • Few shops or restaurants locally
  • Flash flooding in the valley after heavy rain

Chaguaramas

$$
Marine & Recreational

The peninsula west, with the boatyards and the islands

The peninsula west of the city, a former United States naval base now holding boatyards, marinas, a national park and the boats out to the Bocas islands. It is where Trinidad goes at the weekend and where the yachting community is based.

Best for: Sailors and anyone who wants the outdoors on the city's edge
Pros
  • Marinas, boatyards and the Bocas islands
  • Hiking and zip lines in the national park
  • Cheaper than the city proper
Cons
  • Twenty-five minutes from Port of Spain
  • Little residential housing
  • Busy at weekends and empty midweek

Cost of Living in Port of Spain

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Port of Spain, so the $1,400 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 981 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,400 a month as a solo remote worker in Port of Spain, which is cheap for the Caribbean and reflects an economy built on oil and gas rather than tourism. A one-bedroom flat runs roughly $500 to $900, with Woodbrook, St Clair and Newtown at the top of that and the areas east of the city considerably cheaper. Food is inexpensive and Trinidad's is the best in the Caribbean by a distance, with doubles from a street cart costing almost nothing. Fuel is subsidised. The Trinidad and Tobago dollar floats within a managed band and foreign exchange can be hard to obtain locally. This is our own estimate rather than measured data.

Where to Work in Port of Spain

Trinidad's connectivity is good by regional standards, underwritten by an energy sector that needs it: fibre from Flow and Digicel reaches most of the developed western corridor at solid speeds and mobile coverage is comprehensive. There is a small coworking scene around Port of Spain, serving local startups rather than visitors. Power is reliable. The island runs four hours behind central European time. English is the only language required. The professional network here is real but concentrated in energy, finance and government, and it is not one a visiting remote worker plugs into easily.

Getting Around Port of Spain

Port of Spain is compact around Independence Square and the Queen's Park Savannah, the largest roundabout in the world and the city's green centre, and that core is walkable by day. Maxi taxis, the colour-coded minibuses, and route taxis cover the city and the corridor east towards Arima cheaply. Traffic on the Churchill Roosevelt and Beetham highways is severe at peak. Most residents drive, on the left. Piarco airport is forty minutes east. The ferry to Tobago takes around three hours and is how most people reach the quieter island, which is where the beaches are.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Port of Spain

Trinidad and Tobago has no remote work visa. Most Western nationalities enter visa-free for up to ninety days, and Commonwealth citizens generally receive the same, with extensions applied for at the immigration division in Port of Spain. The process is bureaucratic rather than difficult. Anyone planning to stay longer needs a work permit tied to an employer, or a residence permit through marriage or descent. The country is not organised around long-stay visitors and the immigration system reflects that; plan around ninety days rather than assuming an extension. Anyone coming for Carnival should apply well ahead if a visa is needed at all, since the weeks around it are the busiest the immigration system sees.

Best Time to Visit Port of Spain

January to May is the dry season, with daytime temperatures around thirty-one and the lowest rainfall, and it contains the event that defines the calendar. Trinidad Carnival, on the Monday and Tuesday before Ash Wednesday, is the largest in the Caribbean and the reason a great many people come at all; the season of fetes and panyard rehearsals runs for weeks before it and the city is fully booked. June to December is the wet season, with heavy afternoon rain. Trinidad sits south of the hurricane belt and is rarely struck, which is a genuine advantage over the rest of the region.

Pros and Cons of Port of Spain

Trinidad invented the steelpan, calypso, soca and limbo, and its Carnival is the one every other Caribbean carnival is modelled on. The food is the best in the region, mixing African, Indian, Chinese, Creole and Syrian traditions on the same street. The Northern Range has rainforest and the Caroni swamp fills with scarlet ibis at dusk. It is cheap and outside the hurricane belt. Against that, the crime rate is the serious qualification: Trinidad has one of the higher murder rates in the Americas, and both residents and foreign governments treat parts of Port of Spain as no-go areas.

Who Port of Spain Is For

Port of Spain suits someone drawn to music, food and Carnival who is prepared to take the security situation seriously and organise their movements around it rather than ignore it. It is culturally the richest place in the Caribbean and almost no remote workers go there. It is a poor fit for anyone who wants a beach city, since Trinidad's coast is not its strength and Tobago is a separate trip, and for anyone who needs a long-stay visa. Read current government travel advice for Trinidad specifically, and take local advice on neighbourhoods seriously.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Port of Spain?
