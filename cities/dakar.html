Cost of Living in Dakar

Dakar sits at the affordable end of the spectrum for a capital city, though costs vary sharply depending on where you live and how you eat. The local currency is the West African CFA franc (XOF), pegged to the euro. As a rough guide, 1 USD buys around 600 XOF.

A furnished one-bedroom apartment in central areas like Plateau or Mermoz runs 300,000-600,000 XOF/month ($500-1,000). Push further out toward Grand Dakar or Parcelles Assainies and you can find decent flats for 150,000-250,000 XOF/month ($250-420). Monthly groceries from local markets cost 20,000-40,000 XOF ($33-67) if you cook Senegalese staples like rice, fish, and vegetables. Western-style supermarkets double that figure. A meal at a local dibiterie or rice-and-fish spot costs 2,500-4,000 XOF ($4-7); a sit-down restaurant meal in a mid-range expat-friendly place is 10,000-20,000 XOF ($17-33). A coworking hot desk runs roughly 60,000-100,000 XOF/month ($100-165). Local transport by car rapide or dakar dem dikk bus costs 225-500 XOF per ride; a monthly pass is around 45,000 XOF ($75).

  • Budget total (local lifestyle, cheaper area): 350,000-550,000 XOF/month ($580-920)
  • Comfortable total (central flat, coworking, mixed dining): 750,000-1,200,000 XOF/month ($1,250-2,000)

Where to Work

Dakar has a legitimate and growing coworking scene, driven partly by its regional tech and startup ecosystem. Jokkolabs (Point E/Plateau area) is one of the first coworking spaces in Africa, established in 2010, and remains a solid community-focused option with reliable fiber internet. Impact Hub Dakar (Keur Gorgui area) offers a professional environment with meeting rooms and a strong entrepreneur network. AfricaWorks Dakar (Route de Ouakam, near Point E) is the most polished option, with serviced offices, dedicated desks, and premium amenities. Diallo Cowork in Almadies caters to an international crowd with air conditioning, fiber optic internet, and services like translation and business address.

Cafe-work culture exists but is more limited than in Southeast Asia. Spots like Kotao Coffee (Almadies and Plateau locations) attract laptop users and offer decent WiFi, but many traditional cafes are not set up for long sessions. Mobile data on Orange or Free SIM cards is affordable and widely available as a backup, though speeds are inconsistent outside the business districts. Power cuts happen, especially in the rainy season, so coworking spaces with generators are a meaningful advantage.

Getting Around

Dakar is notorious for traffic congestion, and this is not an exaggeration. The peninsula geography funnels everything through a handful of roads, and rush hours can turn a 5 km journey into a 45-minute crawl. For day-to-day movement, the cheapest option is the public bus network (Dakar Dem Dikk) or the car rapide minibuses, but both are slow and crowded. Official yellow taxis are plentiful and cheap by Western standards; always agree on a price before you get in or insist on the meter. Ride-hailing apps (Yango and Heetch operate in Dakar) are more reliable for consistent pricing. Almadies, Ngor, and Plateau are walkable within their own neighborhoods, but walking between major districts is neither practical nor advisable after dark.

Blaise Diagne International Airport (DSS) replaced the old Leopold Sedar Senghor Airport and sits about 45 km from central Dakar. The drive takes 45-90 minutes depending on traffic and time of day. Official taxi fares run 15,000-25,000 XOF ($25-42); budget services cost less. There is no direct rail link to the airport yet, though a BRT corridor expansion is planned.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Senegal is generous on entry. Citizens of EU countries, the USA, UK, Canada, and most West African ECOWAS nations enter visa-free for up to 90 days. Most other passport holders can get a visa on arrival at Dakar's international airport for a short stay, or apply in advance for a tourist visa allowing up to 90 days. There is no dedicated digital nomad visa for Senegal as of mid-2026. To extend your stay beyond 90 days, you apply in person at the Direction de la Surveillance du Territoire (DST) in Dakar before your entry stamp expires; extensions are granted at the authorities' discretion and require proof of accommodation and a stated reason. Visa runs to a neighboring country and returning are also common. Always verify entry requirements with the official Senegalese immigration authorities or your home country's embassy before travel, as policies can change.

Best Time to Visit

Dakar has two seasons: a dry season from November to May and a rainy season from June to October. For remote work, November through February is the sweet spot. Temperatures stay comfortable around 22-27 degrees Celsius, humidity is low, skies are clear, and the harmattan wind (dry, dusty air from the Sahara) is manageable. March and April remain pleasant but get warmer. May brings rising heat and occasional humidity before the rains arrive. The rainy season (July and August especially) brings heavy downpours, intense humidity, flooding in parts of the city, and occasional power outages that disrupt work. It is also the low season for tourism, which means lower accommodation prices and a quieter city if that appeals to you.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Very affordable cost of living, especially outside the expat enclaves
  • Warm, genuinely welcoming culture rooted in the Senegalese concept of teranga (hospitality)
  • Rich food scene with fresh seafood and flavorful Senegalese cuisine
  • Growing tech and startup ecosystem with real coworking infrastructure
  • Visa-free access for most Western passport holders for 90 days
  • English increasingly spoken in professional and coworking environments
  • Atlantic coast beaches and the relaxed Almadies neighborhood for downtime

Cons

  • Traffic congestion is severe and unavoidable without careful scheduling
  • WiFi reliability outside coworking spaces is inconsistent
  • Power cuts are a real risk, particularly during the rainy season
  • English fluency is low outside business and tourist areas; French and Wolof dominate
  • Petty theft and bag-snatching are common in crowded areas and on the Corniche
  • Air quality can suffer during the harmattan season (January-March)
  • No nomad visa; 90-day entry limit requires planning for longer stays

Who Dakar Is Best For

Dakar suits nomads who want an authentic West African urban experience without sacrificing basic coworking infrastructure. If you are drawn to a city with real cultural depth, great food, and low costs outside the tourist bubble, and you are comfortable operating partly in French, Dakar rewards you. It works especially well for people connecting with Africa's startup and creative scenes, those on a longer trip through West Africa, or nomads who want a base that is genuinely different from the Bangkok-Lisbon-Medellin circuit.

Skip Dakar if you need rock-solid, always-on internet for video calls or large file transfers without a reliable coworking membership in hand, if traffic stress drains your productivity, or if operating in a non-English environment feels like too steep a daily overhead. It is also not the best fit for nomads who want a polished digital nomad community with organized meetups and peer networking; that infrastructure exists but is thinner here than in more established nomad hubs.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dakar

How much does it cost to live in Dakar as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,600 a month in Dakar for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Dakar?

Dakar rates 4 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work patchy. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Dakar safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Dakar is 5/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Dakar a good city for digital nomads overall?

Dakar stands out for low costs and nightlife among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,600 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
