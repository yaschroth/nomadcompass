Living in Kaohsiung as a Digital Nomad

Cost of Living in Kaohsiung

Kaohsiung is one of the more affordable large cities in East Asia, and it runs noticeably cheaper than Taipei on almost every line item, especially rent. As a solo remote worker you can live comfortably here on roughly $1,000 to $1,600 per month, depending on where you rent and how often you eat out versus cook. Prices below are in New Taiwan Dollars (TWD) with rough USD equivalents.

  • Rent, central 1-bedroom: NT$18,000 to NT$28,000 (about $580 to $900) per month in walkable districts near the metro.
  • Rent, cheaper areas: NT$7,000 to NT$15,000 (about $230 to $500) for a studio or room a little further out.
  • Groceries: NT$6,000 to NT$9,000 (about $190 to $290) per month if you cook regularly.
  • Eating out: A meal at a local spot or night market runs NT$70 to NT$150 (about $2 to $5). Kaohsiung's night markets (Ruifeng, Liuhe) make eating out cheap and easy, so many people skip cooking entirely.
  • Coworking hot desk: Roughly NT$4,000 to NT$8,000 (about $130 to $260) per month for a flexible membership, less if you buy day passes.
  • Local transport: NT$800 to NT$1,500 (about $25 to $50) per month using an EasyCard or iPass on the metro and buses.

Add utilities and a phone plan and a realistic all-in total lands around $1,000 to $1,600 per month. Trim the rent and lean on night markets and you can go lower. Summer air conditioning will push your electricity bill up noticeably, so budget for it.

Where to Work

Kaohsiung's coworking scene is smaller than Taipei's but it does exist and it is growing. Second Space, a members club right next to Central Park metro station, is the best known spot aimed squarely at international remote workers, with fast WiFi, day and monthly options, and 24/7 access. Beyond that, you will find a scattering of local coworking offices and business centers, plus flexible desk providers you can book online.

Cafe working is common and accepted here, and cafes are cheap. Look for independent coffee shops and chains around the Central Park, Formosa Boulevard, and Kaohsiung Arena areas, where you can nurse a drink for a few hours. Not every cafe has strong WiFi or plenty of outlets, so it pays to scout a couple you like. On WiFi generally: Taiwan's internet is fast and reliable, home fiber is excellent, and mobile data is cheap and widespread, so a prepaid SIM or eSIM as a hotspot backup is an easy safety net.

Getting Around

Getting around Kaohsiung is genuinely easy. The Kaohsiung MRT (KMRT) has two metro lines, Red and Orange, that cross at Formosa Boulevard station (worth seeing for the Dome of Light). Fares are distance based, starting at NT$20, and you tap in with a stored-value card. The city also has a Circular Light Rail that completed its full loop in 2024 and links waterfront sights like the Pier-2 Art Center and Dream Mall.

Buy an EasyCard or iPass when you arrive. One card covers the metro, light rail, city buses, and YouBike public bikes, and it gives you a small fare discount over single tickets. YouBike is a great way to fill gaps between stations, and the flatter parts of the city are pleasant to cycle. Walkability is decent near metro nodes but patchy elsewhere, and scooters dominate the roads, so cross carefully. For the airport, Kaohsiung International Airport (KHH) sits right on the Red Line at Kaohsiung International Airport station, so the metro takes you downtown in about 20 to 30 minutes for pocket change. Taxis are cheap too.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Many passport holders, including citizens of the United States, the UK, most of the EU, Canada, Australia, and Japan, can enter Taiwan visa-free for up to 90 days. Visa-waiver entries generally cannot be extended, so plan a border run or a proper visa if you want to stay longer.

For longer stays there are two main routes. The Taiwan Employment Gold Card is a combined work permit and residence visa valid for one to three years, aimed at professionals across fields such as technology, finance, economics, culture, and law. It does not tie you to an employer and includes tax perks. Separately, Taiwan launched a digital nomad visa in January 2025 for citizens of visa-exempt countries: it grants an initial six months, is extendable up to a maximum of two years, and has income requirements (broadly around $40,000 a year for applicants 30 and over, less for those in their twenties) plus a savings threshold. Rules and thresholds change, so confirm the current details with official sources (Taiwan's Bureau of Consular Affairs and the Gold Card and digital nomad visa portals) before you commit.

Best Time to Visit

Kaohsiung has a hot, humid subtropical climate with two clear seasons. Summers (roughly May through September) are very hot and wet, with a real rainy season and a typhoon risk that peaks from June to September and can disrupt travel. Winters are mild and dry, with comfortable daytime temperatures and far less rain.

The best window for remote work is mid-November through early April. You get warm but manageable days, low humidity by local standards, and minimal rain, which makes cafe hopping, cycling, and weekend trips far more pleasant. If you come in peak summer, expect to structure your day around the heat and to rely heavily on air conditioning.

Pros and Cons

  • Cheaper than Taipei across rent, food, and daily costs.
  • Warmer, sunnier, and more laid-back than the capital.
  • Excellent, cheap public transport with metro, light rail, and YouBike.
  • Fast, reliable internet and cheap, delicious food, especially night markets.
  • Waterfront, parks, and easy access to southern Taiwan's nature.
  • Brutally hot and humid summers, plus typhoon season.
  • A smaller nomad and coworking scene than Taipei, so fewer built-in social options.
  • A real language barrier: English is limited outside tourist and business settings.
  • Air quality can dip in the cooler months.
  • Fewer international flight connections than Taipei.

Who Kaohsiung Is Best For

Kaohsiung suits remote workers who want Taiwan's safety, food, and infrastructure at a lower price and a slower pace than Taipei. It is a strong pick if you value warm weather, cheap living, easy transport, and a calmer city, and if you are comfortable making your own routine rather than relying on a large ready-made nomad community. Long-stay types eyeing the Gold Card or digital nomad visa will find it a comfortable base.

You should probably skip it if you need a big, buzzing coworking and social scene, if you cannot tolerate intense summer heat and humidity, or if you want a place where English gets you through daily life without effort. In those cases Taipei, or a stint split between the two, may serve you better.

Frequently Asked Questions About Kaohsiung

How much does it cost to live in Kaohsiung as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,000 a month in Kaohsiung for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Kaohsiung?

Kaohsiung rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Kaohsiung safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Kaohsiung is 9/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Kaohsiung a good city for digital nomads overall?

Kaohsiung stands out for safety and food among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
