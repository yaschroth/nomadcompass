Discover island sophistication, yacht harbors, Gothic cathedral, and year-round sun. Plan on roughly $2,370 a month. Its strengths are climate and nature, while cost is where it scores lower.

Palma is not cheap by Spanish standards. The Balearic Islands run pricier than the mainland because much of what you buy arrives by ferry, and summer demand pushes everything higher. Expect a solo remote worker to spend roughly $2,190 to $3,340 per month depending on flat, season, and habits.

Housing is the swing factor. A studio or one-bedroom in a central, walkable barrio like Santa Catalina or the Old Town runs about $1,040 to $1,610. Move to an outer area such as Son Ferriol or the ring around the center and you can find $805 to $1,150. Long leases are cheaper per month than short furnished nomad rentals, which carry a premium.

  • Groceries: roughly $345 to $460 per month for one person; island prices sit a bit above mainland Spain.
  • Eating out: a basic restaurant meal is about $14 to $21; dinner for two at a mid-range spot runs $69 to $92.
  • Coworking hot-desk: day passes are commonly $29 to $52; a monthly membership is often $175 to $290.
  • Local transport: a monthly EMT bus pass is inexpensive, and single rides are cheaper paid by contactless card than in cash.

Watch the seasonality. Short-term rents and daily costs climb sharply from June through September, when the island fills with tourists. Winter (November to March) is markedly quieter and cheaper, and you can sometimes negotiate off-season monthly deals that would be impossible in July.

Where to Work

Palma has a real coworking scene rather than a single token office. Well-known spaces include Palmapolitan, a design-led favorite with freelancers and creatives; Hub Mallorca, central and event-heavy with a business vibe; Nidus39, quieter with a leafy terrace; and Fortuny 3, a larger space with a garden, meeting rooms, and a kitchen. Convendum and Regus cover the more corporate end. Day passes run roughly $29 to $52.

Cafe-work culture exists but is lighter than in bigger nomad hubs. Plenty of cafes in Santa Catalina and the center welcome a laptop for a coffee and a couple of hours, though you should read the room in summer when tables are in demand. For all-day sessions, a coworking desk is the reliable move.

On WiFi: mainland-quality fiber is widespread across Palma, and connections in apartments and coworking spaces are generally fast and stable. If you take video calls, confirm the specific flat has fiber rather than relying on assumptions, and keep a mobile data plan as backup, which is standard practice anywhere.

Getting Around

Palma is compact and the Old Town is genuinely walkable, so many nomads barely need transit day to day. When you do, the EMT city bus network covers the city well, with a cheap monthly pass and single fares that are lower when paid by contactless card. Cycling is popular and the seafront promenade makes for an easy commute.

Getting to and from Palma Airport (PMI) is simple. The A1 airport bus runs to Plaza de Espanya in the heart of the city in about 20 to 30 minutes, roughly every 12 to 17 minutes depending on season. A one-way ticket is around $6. Taxis and pre-booked transfers are widely available if you land late or travel heavy. From Plaza de Espanya you can also catch regional TIB buses and the island train to reach the rest of Mallorca.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

If you hold a US, UK, Canadian, Australian, or other visa-exempt passport, you can stay in the Schengen Area, which includes Spain, for up to 90 days within any rolling 180-day period without a visa. Those days count across all of Schengen, not per country, and entry and exit days both count. With the EU Entry/Exit System now operational, overstays are tracked automatically, so do not push past 90 days.

To stay longer, Spain offers a digital nomad visa for remote workers employed by or contracting with companies outside Spain. As of 2026 it requires proof of income around $3,280 per month (tied to 200% of the Spanish minimum wage, with higher thresholds if you bring family), plus a relevant degree or about three years of experience and proof you have worked with your clients or employer for at least three months. Applied for from a consulate abroad it is typically issued for up to one year; applied for from inside Spain it can grant a residence authorization of up to three years, both renewable, and it comes with a favorable tax regime for many applicants.

Best Time to Visit

Palma has a classic Mediterranean climate: hot summers and mild winters. July and August average highs near 29 to 30 C (about 86 F), with warm seas and packed beaches. Winters are gentle, roughly 10 to 15 C (50 to 59 F), rarely cold, and much quieter.

For remote work, the sweet spots are spring (April to June) and early autumn (September to October). You get warm, comfortable weather, swimmable seas, and far smaller crowds and prices than peak summer. Winter suits you if you want the cheapest rents and a calm island to focus in, accepting shorter days, some closed venues, and a sleepier social scene. Midsummer is beautiful but hot, expensive, and crowded, which is the trade-off to weigh.

Pros and Cons

What works well:

  • Walkable, beautiful Old Town with a real city feel plus beaches and mountains close by.
  • Solid coworking scene, fast fiber, and a growing nomad and startup community.
  • Easy airport connections and short bus ride into the center.
  • Mild shoulder seasons that are ideal for working while still enjoying the outdoors.

What to weigh honestly:

  • Heavy summer overtourism and noise, with rents and daily costs spiking June to September.
  • Higher prices than mainland Spain due to island import costs.
  • Island isolation: leaving means a flight or ferry, and winter can feel quiet and slow.
  • Housing is competitive and short-term furnished rentals carry a premium.

Who Palma Is Best For

Palma suits remote workers who want a genuine European city with beaches, good food, reliable infrastructure, and an outdoor lifestyle, and who value walkability and a Mediterranean pace over a bargain. It rewards those who come in spring, autumn, or winter and can afford island prices, and it fits people planning a longer stay on the digital nomad visa.

Skip it, or at least avoid midsummer, if you are on a tight budget, if you dislike crowds and heat, or if you need a large, always-buzzing nomad hub with a dense cafe-work culture year round. If constant easy overland travel to other cities matters to you, an island base will feel limiting.

Frequently Asked Questions About Palma de Mallorca

How much does it cost to live in Palma de Mallorca as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,370 a month in Palma de Mallorca for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Palma de Mallorca?

Palma de Mallorca rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work good. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Palma de Mallorca safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Palma de Mallorca is 8/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Palma de Mallorca a good city for digital nomads overall?

Palma de Mallorca stands out for climate and nature among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,370 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
