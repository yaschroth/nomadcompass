Setting Up as a Nomad in Stockholm

A Nordic innovation hub spread across 14 islands with world-class design. For remote workers, Stockholm leans on fast WiFi and English, with a typical budget near $4,200 a month. The main trade-off is cost.

Cost of Living in Stockholm

Stockholm is expensive, and you should plan for that from the start. The krona has softened against the dollar in recent years (roughly 1 SEK to $0.10 in 2025 and 2026), which takes a little sting out of the numbers, but this is still a high-cost Northern European capital. The trade-off is quality: clean, safe, well-run, and beautiful. Here is a realistic monthly picture for one person.

  • Rent, central one-bedroom: around 14,000 to 18,000 SEK ($1,400 to $1,800) in areas like Norrmalm, Vasastan, or Södermalm. First-hand contracts are almost impossible to get quickly, so most newcomers rent second-hand (andrahand), which carries a premium.
  • Rent, cheaper area: around 10,000 to 13,000 SEK ($1,000 to $1,300) further out along the metro, in areas like Solna, Sundbyberg, or the southern suburbs.
  • Groceries: roughly 3,000 to 4,000 SEK ($300 to $400) per month if you cook at home. Budget chains like Lidl and Willys cut this noticeably versus ICA and Coop.
  • Eating out: a casual sit-down meal runs 150 to 250 SEK ($15 to $25), a mid-range dinner 250 to 350 SEK ($25 to $35). Alcohol is heavily taxed and pricey.
  • Coworking hot desk: a flexible membership is roughly 4,000 to 5,500 SEK ($400 to $550) per month at established spaces.
  • Local transport: the SL 30-day pass is 1,070 SEK ($107), covering all metro, buses, trams, and commuter ferries.

All in, a single remote worker should budget roughly 22,000 to 32,000 SEK ($2,200 to $3,200) per month, with rent as the swing factor. Note that Sweden is effectively cashless: bring a contactless card, and expect that many places do not take cash at all. Locals pay each other with Swish, which is tied to a Swedish bank account you likely will not have on a short stay.

Where to Work

Stockholm has a mature coworking scene aimed at its large startup and tech sector. Convendum runs several polished central locations with a single membership covering all of them. United Spaces has multiple sites, including a well-known waterfront building and a Södermalm location. A House near Stureplan is design-focused and community-driven, and The Park offers hot desks across the city. Most sell monthly flexible memberships rather than cheap single-day drop-ins, so if you are staying a few weeks, a month membership is usually the better value.

Cafe-work culture exists but is gentler than in some cities. The anchor of the day is fika, the Swedish coffee-and-pastry break, which is social more than transactional, so treat a cafe as a place to sit for an hour or two rather than camp all day. Plenty of specialty coffee spots welcome laptops, especially outside peak hours. WiFi is genuinely excellent across the country: fast, stable, and near-universal in cafes, libraries, and public buildings. Mobile data is cheap and coverage is strong, so a local or eSIM plan makes a reliable backup for calls.

Getting Around

Stockholm's transit, run by SL, is one of the best reasons to base here. The metro (Tunnelbana) is fast, frequent, and famously decorated, and it is backed by buses, trams, commuter trains, and archipelago ferries, all on one ticket. A single 75-minute ticket is 44 SEK, and the 30-day pass at 1,070 SEK ($107) pays off quickly. You tap a contactless card or the SL app at the gates. The city itself is very walkable and flat enough in the center, spread across islands connected by bridges, and it is pleasant to cover on foot in the lighter months.

The main airport is Arlanda (ARN), about 40 kilometers north. The fastest option is the Arlanda Express train, roughly 18 minutes to Central Station at about 340 SEK ($34) one way. Cheaper and slower is the Flygbussarna airport coach at around 129 SEK ($13), taking 40 to 50 minutes. Budget-conscious travelers can also take the SL commuter train, which is included in an SL pass apart from a fixed station surcharge at Arlanda.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Sweden does not have a dedicated digital nomad visa, unlike Portugal, Spain, or Estonia. For most non-EU visitors, your stay is governed by the standard Schengen rule: up to 90 days in any 180-day period, visa-free or on a short-stay Schengen visa depending on your nationality. That 90 days counts across the whole Schengen area, not just Sweden, so time in other member countries eats into it.

You may work remotely for a foreign employer or your own foreign clients during a tourist stay, but you cannot take up local Swedish employment on that basis. Be aware of the tax angle: spending more than 183 days in Sweden within a 12-month period can make you tax-resident. EU and EEA citizens have full freedom of movement and can stay and work without these limits. For longer stays, the practical routes are work permits or self-employment permits, which are more involved. Rules and eligibility change, so confirm the current requirements with Sweden's Migration Agency (Migrationsverket) before you plan a long stay.

Best Time to Visit

Stockholm's seasons are dramatic, and they should shape when you come. Winter is very dark and cold. Around the December solstice you get only about six hours of daylight, sunrise is late, sunset is mid-afternoon, and the gray can wear on you. If you are prone to seasonal low mood, this is a hard time to be productive here.

Summer is the opposite and is the city at its best. June and July bring extremely long days, with light late into the evening and a genuinely joyful, outdoor mood as the whole city decamps to parks, water, and the archipelago. The sweet spot for remote work is roughly May through September, when daylight is generous, the weather is mild, and the city feels open. If you value quiet and lower prices, late spring and early autumn are excellent shoulder windows. Reserve deep winter for people who actively want cozy, dark-season Stockholm and have a plan for the light.

Pros and Cons

Pros:

  • Excellent, reliable WiFi and strong mobile coverage everywhere.
  • World-class public transport that makes a car pointless.
  • Very safe, clean, orderly, and high quality of life.
  • Widespread fluent English, so daily life is easy without Swedish.
  • Stunning summers with long daylight and easy access to nature and the archipelago.

Cons:

  • High cost of living, especially rent, eating out, and alcohol.
  • Dark, cold winters with very short days that can drag on productivity and mood.
  • Tough short-term housing: first-hand contracts are scarce, and second-hand rentals cost a premium.
  • No digital nomad visa and a hard 90-day Schengen cap for many non-EU visitors.
  • Near-cashless setup can be awkward without a Swedish bank account and Swish.

Who Stockholm Is Best For

Stockholm suits remote workers who prioritize quality of life over low cost, and who can absorb higher prices. It is a natural fit if you work in or around tech and design, want a safe and efficient European base, value clean air and nature, and plan to come in the lighter half of the year. English speakers will feel comfortable fast.

Skip Stockholm, or at least skip the winter, if you are on a tight budget, if dark and cold months hit your mood or output hard, or if you need to stay in one place for many months without EU rights and a proper permit. Budget-focused nomads and anyone chasing warmth and sun year-round will get better value elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions About Stockholm

How much does it cost to live in Stockholm as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Stockholm is around $4,200 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Stockholm?

With a WiFi score of 9/10, connectivity in Stockholm is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Stockholm safe for digital nomads?

Stockholm scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Stockholm a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Stockholm is worth a look if you value fast WiFi and English. Costs sit near $4,200 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
