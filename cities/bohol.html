Setting Up as a Nomad in Bohol

The island of the Chocolate Hills and tarsiers, ringed by Panglao's white sand and some of the cheapest good diving in Asia. Bohol pairs nature with English and a tropical rainforest climate, all for about $800 a month. If anything, the size of the nomad scene lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Bohol

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Alona Beach and Tawala

$$$
Beachfront & Touristy

Bohol's tourist strip, dive shops and beach bars

Alona Beach in barangay Tawala on Panglao is the busiest strip in the province, a short arc of white sand backed by dive shops, resorts, smoothie cafes and beach bars. The lanes behind it hold guesthouses, scooter rentals and the island's main coworking spaces. Fibre internet is strongest here.

Best for: Nomads who want beach, coworking and other travellers within walking distance
Pros
  • The only real nomad community in the province
  • Best internet, cafes and coworking on the island
  • Diving, island hopping and rentals all on the doorstep
Cons
  • Tourist pricing on food and accommodation
  • Beach bars and karaoke run loud into the night
  • The beach itself is crowded and narrow at high tide

Doljo and Danao, Panglao

$$
Quiet & Coastal

Quiet west-coast beaches ten minutes from Alona

The western tip of Panglao around Doljo and Danao beaches is a stretch of coconut groves, small villas and low-key resorts on wide, mostly empty sand. There are a handful of cafes but no strip, so you ride a scooter to Alona for anything social. Sunsets face straight out to sea.

Best for: Nomads who want beach living without the noise and can ride a scooter
Pros
  • Wide, uncrowded beaches with real sunsets
  • Villas and long-stay rentals cost less than Alona
  • Ten minutes by scooter to the Alona amenities
Cons
  • A scooter is essential, nothing is walkable
  • Very few restaurants, cafes or shops nearby
  • Internet is more variable than on the Alona strip

Dauis

$
Local & Convenient

The causeway town between Panglao and the city

Dauis sits on the northeast corner of Panglao island at the causeway bridges to Tagbilaran, built around its old coral-stone church and the Hinagdanan Cave area. It is residential rather than touristy, with local markets, a growing set of cafes and quick access in both directions.

Best for: Long-stay nomads who want island rents and city errands both in reach
Pros
  • Ten minutes to Tagbilaran malls and hospitals
  • Local prices for food, markets and rentals
  • Still on Panglao island with beaches close by
Cons
  • No beach in the town centre itself
  • Causeway traffic backs up at peak hours
  • Little in the way of nomad or expat social life

Tagbilaran City

$
Urban & Practical

The provincial capital, malls, hospitals and cheap rent

Tagbilaran is Bohol's small capital, a working port city with the island's malls, hospitals, banks, ferry pier and government offices packed into a few busy streets. It is not scenic, but it is where you sort visas, medical care and serious shopping, and long-term rents are the cheapest in the province.

Best for: Budget nomads and anyone who needs reliable services more than a sea view
Pros
  • Cheapest apartments and food in Bohol
  • Hospitals, immigration, banks and malls all here
  • Ferry pier connects to Cebu several times daily
Cons
  • Noisy, hot and congested with tricycle traffic
  • No beach and very little charm
  • Almost no cafe or coworking scene for laptops

Baclayon

$
Historic & Slow

Old coastal town with a heritage church and calm water

Baclayon sits on the coast just east of Tagbilaran, an old town built around one of the oldest stone churches in the Philippines. The coastal road is lined with mangroves, small resorts and family homes, and the seafront is calm rather than swimmable. Life here is slow and firmly local.

Best for: Nomads on a long stay who want quiet coastal living and local prices
Pros
  • Very cheap long-term houses and rooms
  • Quiet coastal setting close to Tagbilaran services
  • Easy jump-off point for the Loboc river and Chocolate Hills
Cons
  • The shoreline is mangrove and reef, not swimming beach
  • You need a scooter for almost everything
  • No nomad community and minimal English-language services

Cost of Living in Bohol

Around $800 a month works on Bohol, with the split between Tagbilaran and Panglao mattering more than anything else. In Tagbilaran city a furnished studio or one-bedroom costs $200 to $330; on Panglao near Alona Beach or Dumaluan the same unit runs $280 to $500 because of tourism demand, though monthly rates fall sharply outside December to April. Food is $180 to $260: carinderia meals at $1.50 to $3, fresh fish and rice cheaply at markets, and Alona restaurants at $7 to $14. A Globe or Smart SIM with a data bundle costs $8 to $15 monthly. Coworking is scarce, with day passes around $8 to $12 where offered. Electricity is expensive in the Philippines: $50 to $120 with steady air conditioning, plus $30 to $50 for fibre. Scooter rental is $70 to $110 monthly. Verdict: good value, with power the main budget risk.

Where to Work in Bohol

Panglao island is where most remote workers base themselves, either around Alona Beach for restaurants and dive shops or in the quieter Danao, Doljo and Dumaluan areas for lower rents and less noise. Tagbilaran, 20 minutes away over the causeway, is the practical city with banks, hospitals, malls and the Bureau of Immigration office. Dedicated coworking has come and gone on Panglao, so verify what is currently trading rather than relying on old listings; in practice most people work from air-conditioned cafes near Alona and Tawala, from dive-resort common areas, or from home. Converge and PLDT fibre reaches much of Panglao at 50 Mbps to 300 Mbps, and Starlink has become a common backup among villa owners. The real caveats are brownouts, which are frequent and can last hours, plus typhoon-driven outages; a large power bank and a well-loaded mobile data plan are essential rather than optional.

Getting Around Bohol

Bohol runs on tricycles, multicabs and scooters. Tricycle hops within Tagbilaran or around Alona cost $0.40 to $2, and a Tagbilaran to Panglao run is $6 to $10 by private tricycle or van. Ceres buses serve the interior towns, Loboc, Carmen and the Chocolate Hills, for $1 to $4. Ride-hailing coverage is patchy and Grab is not reliably available, so a scooter at $8 to $12 a day or $70 to $110 a month is effectively essential for anyone staying more than a couple of weeks. Panglao Bohol International Airport, opened in 2018, sits about 20 minutes from Alona with domestic flights to Manila, Cebu and Davao plus occasional international charters. Fast ferries are the other lifeline: OceanJet, Lite Ferries and Supercat run Tagbilaran to Cebu in about two hours for $10 to $22, with services to Camiguin, Siquijor and Dumaguete.

Visas for Bohol

Most Western passport holders enter the Philippines visa-free for 30 days, provided they hold an onward or return ticket. Extensions are handled at Bureau of Immigration offices, including one in Tagbilaran: the first extension adds 29 days for roughly $60, after which longer six-month blocks become possible, and tourists may ultimately stay up to 36 months. Anyone staying beyond 59 days must obtain an ACR I-Card, costing about $100. There is also a mandatory eTravel registration online before arrival, which is free. The Philippines approved a digital nomad visa framework in 2025 for one-year renewable stays aimed at remote workers with foreign income, but implementation has been slow and application details remain limited, so most nomads still use the tourist extension route, which is straightforward and widely used. Long-term retirement residency via the SRRV is a separate option for those over 35 with capital to deposit.

Best Time to Visit Bohol

Bohol's best window is March to May: dry, hot at 30C to 34C, with the calmest seas and the best diving visibility around Balicasag and Pamilacan. February is also excellent and slightly cooler. June to October is the southwest monsoon, with humid days, heavy but often brief showers and greener interiors; prices drop and Panglao empties, which suits budget-focused stays. The riskiest window for the central Visayas is November to January, when tropical cyclones tracking west can hit hard, as Typhoon Odette did in December 2021, causing weeks of power and communication outages. December to early January is simultaneously peak tourist season, with Alona busy and rents highest. Whale shark and dolphin activity around Pamilacan peaks in the dry months. Festival highlights include the Sandugo Festival in Tagbilaran each July and Sinulog spillover from nearby Cebu in January.

Pros and Cons of Bohol

Bohol's appeal is straightforward: white-sand beaches at Alona and Dumaluan, some of the best diving and freediving in the Philippines at Balicasag and Napaling, the Chocolate Hills and Loboc River inland, tarsiers, warm water year round, low rents outside peak season, English spoken almost universally, and an international airport plus fast ferries to Cebu. Food and beer are cheap and the pace is relaxed. The counterweight is infrastructure: brownouts are frequent and can last hours, typhoons are a genuine seasonal risk with slow recovery, fibre coverage is uneven and Starlink is often needed as backup, coworking spaces are scarce and impermanent, medical care beyond basic cases means flying to Cebu, ride-hailing is unreliable so a scooter is mandatory, road quality varies, Alona has become noisy and overbuilt, and the nomad community is small and seasonal.

Who Bohol Is For

Bohol suits divers, freedivers and beach-focused remote workers who want Philippine costs, warm water and island life while retaining an airport and hospital access on the same island. It works for self-sufficient solo nomads and couples with redundant connectivity, for anyone comfortable riding a scooter daily, and for long-stay travellers using the generous tourist extension system to spend months in the region. It also fits people who value nature, the Chocolate Hills, river trips and empty inland roads, over urban amenities. Skip it if the work cannot tolerate power cuts or a typhoon-season outage, if a structured coworking and meetup scene is what makes a base productive, or if consistent city infrastructure, public transport and healthcare matter. Those wanting the same country with more urban reliability should compare Cebu City; those wanting a larger nomad crowd should compare Siargao or Manila.

Frequently Asked Questions About Bohol

How much does it cost to live in Bohol as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Bohol is around $800 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Bohol?

Bohol rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Bohol safe for digital nomads?

Bohol scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Bohol a good city for digital nomads overall?

Bohol stands out for nature and the outdoors and widely spoken English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $800 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
