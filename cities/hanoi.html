Hanoi is one of Southeast Asia's most affordable capitals for remote workers. A comfortable life here costs roughly 22,000,000-35,000,000 VND per month (around $900-1,400 USD), depending on how centrally you live and how often you eat out.

  • Rent, central districts (Hoan Kiem, Ba Dinh, Tay Ho): 10,000,000-18,000,000 VND/month ($400-720) for a furnished one-bedroom.
  • Rent, outer areas (Cau Giay, Thanh Xuan): 6,000,000-10,000,000 VND/month ($240-400) for comparable space.
  • Groceries: 2,000,000-4,000,000 VND/month ($80-160) if you shop at local wet markets and supermarkets.
  • Eating out: A bowl of pho or bun cha at a local spot costs 40,000-70,000 VND ($1.60-2.80). Mid-range restaurant meals run 150,000-300,000 VND ($6-12). Budget 2,500,000-5,000,000 VND/month ($100-200) for a mix.
  • Coworking hot-desk: 1,500,000-3,000,000 VND/month ($60-120) at most shared spaces; day passes start around 100,000-150,000 VND ($4-6).
  • Local transport (Grab rides, occasional bus): 500,000-1,500,000 VND/month ($20-60).
  • Total realistic range: 22,000,000-35,000,000 VND/month ($880-1,400 USD) for a comfortable nomad lifestyle. Budget travelers can survive on under $700 with a cheaper room and local food.

Where to Work

Hanoi has a maturing coworking scene and a deep-rooted cafe culture that suits laptop workers well. Fixed broadband speeds average 150-220 Mbps at home or in dedicated coworking spaces. Cafe WiFi is more variable, typically 10-30 Mbps at busy spots and up to 50-70 Mbps at the better work-friendly ones.

Coworking Spaces

Toong has five locations across the city and is the most popular chain among nomads. Its French-villa aesthetic, free tea and coffee, and monthly rates starting around 1,900,000 VND ($75) make it the default choice. COGO Coworking in Dong Da is one of the few spaces open 24/7, with day passes at around 100,000 VND ($4) and monthly memberships from 2,000,000 VND ($80). UPGen has seven locations and a more corporate feel, with 24/7 access and full amenities.

Cafe Work Culture

Vietnamese coffee culture actively accommodates laptop workers. Oriberry Coffee on Xuan Dieu in Tay Ho is a long-standing favorite among expats with reliable WiFi and spacious seating. Spacebar Coworking Cafe in Tay Ho blends a cafe menu with a proper coworking setup, popular with creatives and remote workers. Most mid-range cafes will not pressure you to keep ordering, though during lunch rush WiFi can slow noticeably on shared connections.

One honest note: power outlets in cafes can be scarce. Arrive early, bring your own adapter, and confirm connectivity before settling in for the day.

Getting Around

Hanoi is a city built for motorbikes, and that shapes everything about daily movement. Traffic in the Old Quarter and central districts is dense and chaotic, particularly during morning and evening rush hours.

Daily Transport

Grab is the essential app. It covers both GrabCar and GrabBike (motorbike taxi), with transparent pricing in English. For short hops, a GrabBike costs 15,000-40,000 VND ($0.60-1.60). A GrabCar across the city rarely exceeds 150,000 VND ($6). Traditional taxis from Mai Linh or Vinasun are reliable alternatives; avoid unmarked cabs.

Hanoi has one metro line in operation (Cat Linh to Ha Dong), with more under construction. It is useful for commutes in the west of the city but does not yet serve the Old Quarter or Tay Ho. Public buses exist and are very cheap but can be confusing without Vietnamese.

Walkability is high in compact areas like the Old Quarter and around Hoan Kiem Lake, but the city's size and traffic make walking impractical for longer journeys.

Airport

Noi Bai International Airport (HAN) is 30-45 km north of the city center. Expect a 35-60 minute ride depending on traffic. Grab from the airport to the Old Quarter costs roughly 200,000-350,000 VND ($8-14). The Airport Bus No. 86 runs directly to Hoan Kiem Lake for about 45,000-60,000 VND ($1.80-2.40) but takes longer. Pre-booking a Grab or a hotel transfer is the easiest option on arrival.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Vietnam has significantly loosened its entry rules in recent years. Citizens of about 25 countries, including Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the UK, Japan, and South Korea, currently receive 45 days visa-free on arrival.

For everyone else, the e-visa is the standard route. It is valid for up to 90 days with single or multiple entry, costs $25 (single) or $50 (multiple), and takes 3-5 working days to process online. It is accepted at all major airports and dozens of land and sea crossings.

Vietnam does not offer a dedicated digital nomad visa. After your e-visa or visa-free period expires, many nomads do a visa run to a neighboring country (Laos, Cambodia, Thailand) and re-enter. This is a common practice but comes with some uncertainty; official policy can shift. In mid-2025, Vietnam introduced a 5-year Talent Visa aimed at skilled professionals in tech and creative fields, though the application criteria are demanding.

Always verify current entry requirements with the official Vietnam Immigration Portal or your nearest Vietnamese embassy before travel, as policies change.

Best Time to Visit

Hanoi has four distinct seasons, none of them perfect, which makes timing matter more than it does in most Southeast Asian cities.

October to December is the sweet spot for remote work. Temperatures drop to a pleasant 20-25°C, humidity falls, skies clear, and the city takes on a golden quality. Air quality also tends to be better in the transition from wet season.

February to April offers mild spring conditions with temperatures of 15-25°C, though a persistent drizzle and mist called crachin can last for days in February and early March. Late March and April are genuinely lovely.

May to September is hot (up to 38°C), very humid, and subject to heavy afternoon downpours. You can still work productively from air-conditioned spaces, but outdoor exploration is sweaty and the rain is unpredictable. On the upside, this is also when air quality tends to improve compared to winter months.

December to February brings real cold, with lows of 10-15°C and bone-damp air. Hanoi in January feels nothing like the tropics. Pack layers and expect grey skies.

For combining comfort and productivity, aim for October through November or March through April.

Pros and Cons

  • Very low cost of living with excellent value on food, rent, and coworking
  • Outstanding food scene with world-class street food at minimal cost
  • Rich culture and history with easy day trips to Ha Long Bay, Ninh Binh, and Sapa
  • Strong expat and nomad community in Tay Ho with regular meetups and events
  • Fast and affordable internet at coworking spaces and many cafes
  • 90-day e-visa covers most stays without bureaucratic headaches
  • Air quality is a genuine concern: Hanoi repeatedly ranks among the most polluted cities globally, with PM2.5 levels often in the unhealthy range during winter months
  • Traffic is chaotic and crossing busy roads as a pedestrian requires nerve
  • No dedicated nomad visa and visa runs are needed for longer stays
  • Weather is unpredictable across all four seasons, with cold winters and oppressive summers
  • Petty theft, particularly phone and bag snatching from motorbikes, is a consistent issue in tourist areas
  • English proficiency varies widely outside tourist zones, which can complicate daily errands

Who Hanoi Is Best For

Hanoi rewards nomads who want genuine immersion in a non-tourist-facing city at very low cost. If you thrive on cultural depth, complex food, and the energy of a 7-million-person working capital, Hanoi will keep you engaged for months.

It is a strong fit for budget-conscious nomads who want to stretch their runway, foodies and culture seekers drawn to Vietnamese history and cuisine, and experienced Asia travelers comfortable navigating chaotic traffic and administrative ambiguity around visas.

It is less ideal for people with respiratory sensitivities given the persistent air quality problems, anyone who needs a simple long-term visa path (Chiang Mai or Bali are easier), or nomads who prioritize a polished, English-friendly infrastructure. Hanoi demands a bit more adaptability than Ho Chi Minh City or more tourist-oriented Southeast Asian hubs, but the payoff in authenticity and value is real.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hanoi

How much does it cost to live in Hanoi as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,500 a month in Hanoi for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Hanoi?

Hanoi rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Hanoi safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Hanoi is 7/10, good for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Hanoi a good city for digital nomads overall?

Hanoi stands out for food and culture among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,500 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
