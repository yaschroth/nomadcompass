Cost of Living in Galway

Galway is noticeably cheaper than Dublin but has grown expensive by Irish standards over the past few years, driven by tight housing supply and high demand from students and tech workers. Budget carefully before committing to a longer stay.

  • Rent, city centre (1-bed): €1,800-2,200/month (~$1,970-2,410 USD). The Latin Quarter, Eyre Square, and the West End command a premium.
  • Rent, outer areas (Salthill, Knocknacarra, Renmore): €1,400-1,700/month (~$1,530-1,860 USD). A 15-20 minute cycle or bus ride from the centre.
  • Groceries: €250-350/month (~$275-385 USD) shopping at Lidl, Aldi, or Dunnes.
  • Eating out: A sit-down lunch runs €14-18; an evening meal at a mid-range spot is €25-40 per person with a drink. Budget €300-500/month if you eat out regularly.
  • Coworking hot-desk: €270-300/month (~$295-330 USD) at a dedicated space like PorterShed or Innova Rooms. Day passes typically cost €25-35.
  • Local transport: A monthly bus pass is €60 (~$66 USD); the city centre is walkable enough that many residents skip it entirely.
  • Total realistic range: €2,400-3,200/month (~$2,630-3,510 USD) covering rent, food, coworking, transport, and some social spending.

Where to Work

PorterShed is the most established coworking anchor in the city, with locations at Bowling Green and Market Street. It caters to startups and remote tech workers, offers gigabit fibre through a SIRO partnership, and has a genuine community around it. Hot-desk and dedicated desk memberships are available; contact them directly for current pricing as it changes seasonally.

Innova Rooms on Tuam Road (near Liosban Retail Centre) is more utilitarian but reliable: hot desks at €29/day or €299/month, 24-hour access, private pods if you need quiet, and all-inclusive amenities. It sits slightly outside the centre, so you will need a bike or bus.

The cafe-work culture in Galway is real but selective. Coffeewerk and Press on Quay Street is the standout: excellent espresso, a calm atmosphere, and reliable WiFi, though you should not plant yourself here for a full working day without spending appropriately. Most traditional pubs will not welcome laptop workers at all.

City-wide WiFi in fibre-connected buildings is solid at 50-100 Mbps. Older rental flats can be inconsistent, so confirm broadband before signing a lease. Mobile 4G/5G from Three, Eir, or Vodafone covers the city well and is a workable backup.

Getting Around

The city centre is small and genuinely walkable. Most of what you need, coffee, food, the Latin Quarter, Eyre Square, the Spanish Arch, fits inside a 20-minute walk. Cycling is practical and increasingly popular; dedicated lanes exist on main arteries, though some are intermittent.

Bus Eireann operates the city bus network. Fares are cheaper with a TFI Leap Card (about €1.33 per journey versus €1.90 cash). Buses run from roughly 6am to midnight, with 15-20 minute frequency on main routes during the day.

There is no train directly into Galway from Dublin (there is a rail line but it is slow, around 2.5 hours). Citylink and GoBus coaches from Dublin city centre take 2-2.5 hours and are the standard intercity option.

For airports: Shannon is about an hour south by car or coach, with a Bus Eireann Expressway service (around €13 by Leap). Dublin Airport is 2.5 hours by coach. Ireland West Airport Knock is about an hour north and handles some UK and European routes. None of these are a quick trip, so factor airport runs into your planning if you travel often.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Ireland is not in the Schengen Area, so its entry rules are separate. Most nationalities, including US, Canadian, Australian, and UK citizens, can enter visa-free for up to 90 days for tourism or remote work for a non-Irish employer. EU and EEA citizens can stay and work indefinitely under freedom of movement.

Ireland does not offer a dedicated digital nomad visa. The closest option for non-EU remote workers wanting to stay longer is the Stamp 0 permission, which requires proof of approximately €50,000 per year in income and €100,000 in savings. It does not explicitly authorise work and is difficult to obtain in practice.

There is no straightforward long-term path for most non-EU digital nomads beyond the standard 90-day visa-free window. Always confirm current requirements with the Irish Naturalisation and Immigration Service (inis.gov.ie) before making plans.

Best Time to Visit

Galway has an oceanic climate: mild year-round but consistently wet and overcast. Temperatures rarely drop below 3-4°C in winter or climb above 20°C in summer. Rain is possible in any month; the west of Ireland receives over 1,100mm per year.

May to July is the best window for remote workers. Days are exceptionally long (light until 10-11pm in June), temperatures are in the 14-18°C range, and the city is lively without being overwhelmed by tourists. The Galway Arts Festival in July is a genuine cultural highlight but drives up accommodation prices sharply.

August is pleasant but the most crowded. September and October offer quieter conditions and still-tolerable weather. November through March is grey, wet, and cold by feeling if not by thermometer, with short days and frequent Atlantic storms.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Compact, walkable city with genuine character and a strong arts and music scene
  • Pro: English-speaking, with no language barrier for daily life or networking
  • Pro: Excellent food scene relative to city size, from Michelin-level to great local pubs
  • Pro: Close to dramatic Atlantic coastline, Connemara, and the Aran Islands for weekend escapes
  • Pro: Tight-knit nomad and startup community centred around PorterShed
  • Con: Rents have risen sharply; supply is tight and vacancies go fast
  • Con: No straightforward long-stay visa option for non-EU nationals beyond 90 days
  • Con: Weather is persistently grey and rainy, especially outside May-August
  • Con: No close international airport; travel days are long
  • Con: City is small; you will exhaust the novelty faster than in a larger destination

Who Galway Is Best For

Galway suits remote workers who want a genuine, unhurried European city rather than a digital nomad hub optimised for their lifestyle. If you value an authentic local culture, clean air, dramatic nature on your doorstep, a walkable centre, and do not need the warmth or low cost of a Southeast Asian base, Galway delivers well for a 1-3 month stay.

It is a particularly good fit for EU passport holders, who face no visa friction, and for anyone who finds large cities draining. The 90-day visa-free window is generous enough for a solid stint.

Skip it if your budget is tight (the cost-to-quality ratio is lower than comparable cities in Portugal or Eastern Europe), if grey weather affects your motivation badly, or if you need frequent cheap international flights. It is also a poor choice if you rely on a co-working community for daily social life: options exist but the scene is small.

Frequently Asked Questions About Galway

How much does it cost to live in Galway as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,800 a month in Galway for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Galway?

Galway rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Galway safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Galway is 8/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Galway a good city for digital nomads overall?

Galway stands out for English and nightlife among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,800 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
