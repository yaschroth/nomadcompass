Setting Up as a Nomad in Potosí

One of the world's highest cities at over 4,000m, a UNESCO colonial silver town beneath the Cerro Rico mountain that funded an empire. Potosí pairs culture with low costs and a alpine climate, all for about $600 a month. If anything, the size of the nomad scene lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Potosí

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Centro Historico

2
Historic, atmospheric, walkable

The UNESCO colonial heart around the Plaza 10 de Noviembre

Potosí's atmospheric core, wrapped around the Plaza 10 de Noviembre and the Casa Nacional de la Moneda. Grand colonial churches, ornate facades, cafes, and the best of the city's guesthouses sit within a walkable, cobbled grid, all steep and breathless at this altitude but rich with history at every turn.

Best for: History lovers and first-time visitors who want to be in the thick of it
Pros
  • Steps from the main sights and the mint
  • Best concentration of cafes, wifi, and lodging
  • Endlessly photogenic colonial streets
Cons
  • Steep cobbled streets are punishing at altitude
  • Can be cold and quiet after dark

Cerro Rico side

1
Gritty, working, mining-focused

The mining slopes below the silver mountain

The neighborhoods climbing toward the Cerro Rico, where the city's mining life and tour operators concentrate. This is the gritty, hardworking, higher side of town, closest to the cooperative mines and the sobering tours that define Potosí's story. Raw and real rather than comfortable, with commanding views back over the center.

Best for: Travelers focused on mine tours and mining history
Pros
  • Closest to the Cerro Rico and mine-tour operators
  • Authentic working-city atmosphere
  • Dramatic views over Potosí
Cons
  • Even higher and harder on the lungs
  • Limited comfort, dining, and lodging

Villa Imperial / residential outskirts

1
Quiet, residential, local

Quieter local living beyond the tourist core

The calmer residential districts spreading out from the historic center, where everyday Potosinos live. Cheaper rooms and long-stay rentals, neighborhood markets, and simple comedores make this a practical, low-cost base for slow travelers happy to trade sightseeing convenience for local rhythm and a bit more quiet.

Best for: Budget slow travelers wanting a local, low-key base
Pros
  • Cheaper rents and long-stay options
  • Authentic neighborhood life and markets
  • Quieter and more residential
Cons
  • Farther from the main sights
  • Fewer cafes with reliable wifi

Sucre (lower-altitude base)

2
Comfortable, elegant, livable

A gentler, warmer capital about 3 hours away

Not part of Potosí but its natural companion base, Sucre sits roughly three hours away at a much lower, kinder altitude. Bolivia's whitewashed constitutional capital is warmer, more comfortable, and far better set up for actually working, making it the sensible place to stay longer and daytrip or side-trip up to Potosí.

Best for: Remote workers who want a real base near Potosí
Pros
  • Much lower altitude and milder climate
  • Better wifi, cafes, and nomad amenities
  • Elegant, walkable UNESCO city
Cons
  • Three hours from Potosí by bus
  • Not Potosí itself, so requires a trip up

Cost of Living in Potosí

Potosí is one of the cheapest cities you could base yourself in, though "base" is a generous word for a place this high and this cold. A frugal but comfortable month here lands in roughly the $500 to $1,000 range, and you can live near the bottom of that if you rent locally and eat at markets and simple almuerzo spots. Rooms and basic apartments are inexpensive, restaurant set lunches cost a couple of dollars, and long-distance buses are cheap. The real costs are less obvious: warm bedding, heaters or extra blankets, gas for hot water, and the time you lose acclimatizing to the altitude. Prices are quoted and best paid in bolivianos and cash, as cards are unreliable outside larger businesses.

  • Budget room or basic apartment: roughly $150 to $350 per month
  • Almuerzo (set lunch) at a local spot: around $2 to $4
  • Local micros and city taxis: well under a dollar to a couple of dollars
  • Long-distance bus to Sucre or Uyuni: a few dollars each way
  • A guided cooperative mine tour: commonly $15 to $25

Where to Work in Potosí

Be honest with yourself: Potosí is not a comfortable remote-work base. There is no real coworking scene and no nomad community to plug into, wifi is basic and variable, and at over 4,000 metres the altitude itself makes concentration, sleep, and energy harder for the first several days. Plan around all of that. A few cafes and better guesthouses near the Plaza 10 de Noviembre have usable wifi for email and light tasks, but treat any heavy upload, video call, or deadline as optional rather than guaranteed. Your most reliable connection is your own phone data.

  • Buy a local SIM (Entel or Tigo) on arrival and load a generous data plan as your primary connection
  • Acclimatize first: ideally arrive overland from other highland cities rather than flying straight in
  • Hydrate constantly, take it slow, and watch for soroche (altitude sickness): headache, nausea, breathlessness
  • Keep expectations low for calls and large uploads; schedule critical work for lower-altitude Sucre if you can
  • Bring a power bank and warm layers; unheated rooms make long work sessions cold

Getting Around Potosí

The compact colonial center is walkable, which is the good news, but every walk is steep and breathless when the air holds so little oxygen. Give yourself far more time than the distances suggest and pause often on the climbs. For anything beyond the center, cheap shared micros run set routes and taxis are inexpensive and easy to flag. The Cerro Rico and the cooperative mines are reached on organized tours rather than under your own steam, and those tours include transport and gear. Intercity travel is by bus from the terminal, with a small airport handling limited flights.

  • Walk the historic center, but slowly, the cobbled streets are steep and the altitude punishing
  • Micros and taxis cover the rest of the city cheaply
  • Buses run to Sucre (about 3 hours), Uyuni (about 4 hours) and on to La Paz
  • The Cerro Rico mine tours are reached by tour operator, not independently
  • A small airport offers limited connections; many travelers arrive and leave overland

Visas for Potosí

Potosí follows Bolivia's national entry rules, so there is nothing city-specific to arrange. Many nationalities receive a tourist entry on arrival that commonly allows up to 90 days of stay per calendar year, sometimes granted in shorter increments that can be extended at a migration office. A number of nationalities, including US citizens, are subject to a reciprocity visa fee and additional paperwork, so check your own passport's requirements well before travel. Rules, fees, and day allowances change, and this is editorial guidance rather than legal advice, so always confirm current terms with an official Bolivian source or consulate before you go.

  • Tourist entry commonly permits up to 90 days per year, sometimes in shorter renewable blocks
  • Some nationalities (for example US citizens) pay a reciprocity fee and provide extra documents
  • Extensions are handled at Bolivian migration (migracion) offices
  • Verify current fees, day limits, and entry conditions with official sources before travel

Best Time to Visit Potosí

Potosí is cold all year: the altitude keeps temperatures low regardless of season, and nights are frequently freezing. The dry season, roughly May to October, brings the clearest skies, the crispest light on the colonial facades, and the most reliable conditions for travel and mine tours, but the nights are bitterly cold and frost is normal. The wet season, roughly November to March, is a touch milder by day but cloudier, with more rain and grayer skies. There is no warm window here, so the real choice is between clear-and-freezing and milder-and-cloudier. Whenever you come, pack serious warm layers and expect big day-to-night temperature swings.

  • Dry season (May to October): clearest skies and best light, but very cold, often freezing nights
  • Wet season (November to March): milder days, cloudier skies, more rain
  • Cold year-round, with sharp day-to-night temperature swings at altitude
  • Pack thermal layers, a warm sleeping setup, and sun protection for the high-altitude glare

Pros and Cons of Potosí

Potosí is a place you visit for its extraordinary history rather than its comfort. Once one of the richest cities on Earth thanks to the silver of the Cerro Rico, it is now a UNESCO World Heritage site layered with grand colonial churches, the remarkable Casa Nacional de la Moneda, and a mining culture that is still very much alive and still very hard. The atmosphere is genuinely unique and the cost of living is tiny. Against that, weigh the harsh cold, the thin air that affects nearly everyone, the basic infrastructure and patchy wifi, and the near-total absence of a nomad scene.

  • Pros: astonishing colonial and mining heritage, UNESCO status, very low cost, clean high-altitude air, few crowds
  • Cons: harsh cold, punishing altitude and soroche risk, basic infrastructure, variable wifi, no nomad community
  • The cooperative mine tours are sobering and ethically complex, not a light day out
  • Best treated as a slow, history-focused stay rather than a productive work base

Who Potosí Is For

Potosí rewards a particular kind of traveler: the history-and-altitude-loving slow nomad who is happy to trade comfort and connectivity for atmosphere and depth. If you are moved by colonial architecture, mining history, and places that wear their past openly, and if you can handle real cold and thin air with patience, you will find few cities as fascinating. If you need reliable wifi, warmth, a social scene, or a smooth work setup, this is not your base, and nearby Sucre at a lower altitude is a far gentler place to actually get work done. Come to Potosí to understand it, not to be comfortable in it.

  • Great for: history buffs, slow travelers, altitude-hardened explorers, culture-first visitors
  • Not for: comfort seekers, deadline-driven remote workers, anyone sensitive to cold or altitude
  • Consider pairing a short, meaningful Potosí visit with a longer, easier stay in Sucre

Frequently Asked Questions About Potosí

How much does it cost to live in Potosí as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Potosí is around $600 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Potosí?

Potosí rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Potosí safe for digital nomads?

Potosí scores 6 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is average for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Potosí a good city for digital nomads overall?

Potosí stands out for culture and affordability among remote workers. With a typical budget near $600 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
