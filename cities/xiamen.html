Setting Up as a Nomad in Xiamen

A breezy island city on China's southeast coast, with colonial Gulangyu, palm-lined ring roads and some of the cleanest air in the country. For remote workers, Xiamen leans on safety and climate, with a typical budget near $1,200 a month. The main trade-off is the language barrier.

Best Neighborhoods in Xiamen

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Zhongshan Road Old Town (Siming)

$$$
Central & Walkable

Arcaded streets and the ferry to Gulangyu

The historic core of Siming District, where colonnaded qilou shopfronts run down to the harbour and the Gulangyu ferry pier. Snack streets, old teahouses and boutique guesthouses fill the lanes behind. Dense, flat and fully walkable, with metro Line 1 running underneath.

Best for: Nomads who want to live without a scooter and be surrounded by old Xiamen.
Pros
  • Everything walkable, plus metro Line 1
  • Ferries, seafront promenade and street food at the door
  • Character-filled older apartments
Cons
  • Heavy tourist foot traffic on the main pedestrian streets
  • Old buildings with poor sound insulation and no lift
  • Tourist pricing on food along Zhongshan Road itself

Zengcuo'an

$$
Beachy & Creative

Converted fishing village turned cafe hub

A former fishing village on the south coast, now a warren of narrow lanes packed with independent cafes, guesthouses, live music bars and craft stalls, right behind the Huandao Road beach. Students from nearby Xiamen University and young Chinese creatives dominate. The closest thing Xiamen has to a nomad quarter.

Best for: Cafe-hoppers and creatives who want beach access and cheap monthly guesthouse rooms.
Pros
  • Enormous cafe density, easy to work from
  • Beach and the coastal cycling path minutes away
  • Cheap monthly rates in converted village houses
Cons
  • Packed with domestic tourists at weekends and holidays
  • Lanes are cramped, damp and noisy at night
  • Long metro or bus ride to the business districts

Software Park Phase II

$$$
Modern & Techy

Xiamen's tech campus district

A landscaped campus of low-rise tech offices between the hills and the sea, housing much of Xiamen's software and gaming industry. Canteens, coffee chains, gyms and new residential blocks serve the engineers who work here. Practical rather than pretty, with fast internet and reliable infrastructure.

Best for: Developers and startup people who want to be near Xiamen's actual tech scene.
Pros
  • Best connectivity and newest office stock in the city
  • Modern apartments with lifts and proper gyms
  • Coastal road and beaches a short ride south
Cons
  • Dead in the evenings and at weekends
  • Corporate atmosphere with little local character
  • Rents pushed up by tech salaries

Wuyuanwan (Huli)

$$$$
Upscale & Green

Upmarket bay-side new development

A planned waterfront district around Five Yuan Bay in Huli, built with a wetland park, a yacht marina, wide cycling paths and high-end residential towers. It draws families and better-paid professionals who want space and clean air. Metro Line 3 links it back to the island centre.

Best for: Nomads with a bigger budget who want a modern flat, a park and sea air.
Pros
  • New towers with pools, gyms and management
  • Wetland park, marina and safe cycle routes
  • Quiet, clean and family-friendly
Cons
  • Priciest housing on the island
  • Sparse nightlife and few independent cafes
  • Feels sterile compared to old Siming

Jimei

$
Student & Cheap

University town across the bridge

A mainland district north of the island built around Jimei University and the Tan Kah Kee school complex, with its distinctive swallowtail-roofed architecture, a long waterfront promenade and a big student population. Rents run far below Siming and street food is cheap. Metro Line 1 crosses the sea to get here.

Best for: Budget nomads and long-stayers who will trade a commute for half the rent.
Pros
  • Rents roughly half of central Siming
  • Very cheap student food and services
  • Handsome campus architecture and a seafront walk
Cons
  • Around 40 minutes by metro to the island centre
  • Quiet during university holidays
  • Almost no English spoken or foreign community

Cost of Living in Xiamen

Xiamen supports a comfortable single-person month at about $1200. Furnished monthly rentals on Xiamen Island, in Siming district near Zhongshan Road or the university, run $350 to $600 for a one-bedroom, and sea-view apartments in the newer Huli towers sit at the top of that band; mainland-side Jimei and Haicang drop to $250 to $350. Food is a highlight and a bargain: local seafood, satay noodles and Hokkien snacks keep monthly eating near $180 to $280. A 30GB SIM costs $10 to $18. Coworking desks are $90 to $170, well below the tier-one cities. Utilities average $35 to $70, driven mainly by summer air-conditioning. Metro, bus and BRT together cost $15 to $25 a month. Value verdict: Xiamen is one of the best cost-to-quality trades in coastal China, offering clean air, a subtropical waterfront and tier-one infrastructure at roughly two-thirds of Shanghai's price.

Where to Work in Xiamen

Base on Xiamen Island. Siming district puts you near the seafront ring road, Zhongshan Road's old shophouses and Xiamen University; the Shapowei and Zengcuoan areas draw a younger, creative crowd with a dense cluster of independent cafes. Huli is newer, more corporate and closer to the airport. The city's coffee culture is unusually strong for a second-tier Chinese city, and cafe work in Shapowei or around Baicheng Beach is straightforward, though most independents are small and seating is limited. Coworking exists through domestic operators and the Software Park II and III campuses in Siming, which host a real tech workforce and serviced offices at reasonable rates. Broadband commonly delivers 100 to 300 Mbps and 5G coverage is comprehensive across the island. The nationwide firewall applies, so a VPN is essential for Western tools. Power is reliable. Typhoon season can bring occasional outages and closures in late summer.

Getting Around Xiamen

Xiamen has a growing metro network, with Lines 1, 2 and 3 covering the island and the mainland districts and fares of about $0.30 to $1. Line 1 crosses the sea on a causeway, which is a genuinely scenic commute. Buses cost around $0.15 and the BRT elevated busway is fast across the island. Didi is the ride-hailing standard, with typical island trips at $3 to $8. Shared bikes are everywhere at roughly $0.20 per half hour, and Xiamen is one of the better Chinese cities for cycling thanks to the seafront path and a dedicated elevated bike skyway. The island core is genuinely walkable and pleasant on foot. Gaoqi International Airport sits on the island itself, only fifteen minutes from Siming by taxi at around $6. High-speed rail links Fuzhou in about ninety minutes and Shenzhen in three and a half hours. No scooter or car is needed; electric scooters are in fact restricted on the island.

Visas for Xiamen

The rules are national, so Xiamen follows the same framework as Beijing and Shanghai. There is no Chinese digital nomad visa. Citizens of most EU countries, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea can enter visa-free for stays of up to 30 days under the scheme China has progressively widened since late 2023. US and Canadian passport holders are not covered and must obtain an L tourist visa in advance, generally permitting 30 or 60 days per entry, with long multiple-entry validity common for Americans. Xiamen Gaoqi is a designated port for the 240-hour visa-free transit policy, allowing up to ten days when travelling onward to a third country, which suits a short island stopover well. A 30-day extension may be requested from the Xiamen exit-entry bureau but is granted at the officer's discretion. Police registration within 24 hours applies to anyone not staying in a hotel.

Best Time to Visit Xiamen

Xiamen is subtropical and mild, with a comfortable stretch that is longer than most of China's. October to December is the best window: dry, sunny, highs of 22C to 28C and low humidity, ideal for the seafront and for day trips to the Fujian tulou earth buildings. March to May is warm and pleasant but increasingly humid, with spring sea fog common. June through September is hot and sticky, regularly above 32C with high humidity, and this is also typhoon season; Fujian takes direct hits most years, and a serious storm can close the airport and shut the city for a day or two. Rainfall peaks between May and August. January and February are the coolest months, around 12C to 18C, damp but rarely cold. Chinese New Year fills Gulangyu island and the seafront with domestic tourists, as do the May and October national holiday weeks.

Pros and Cons of Xiamen

Xiamen is widely rated one of China's most liveable cities and the reasons hold up. The air is clean by mainland standards, the island is compact, green and edged by a continuous seafront promenade and bike skyway, and the climate is mild for most of the year. Seafood and Hokkien cuisine are excellent and cheap, the metro and airport are modern, Gulangyu island and the Fujian tulou are on the doorstep, and the pace is markedly calmer than Shanghai or Shenzhen while the infrastructure is barely worse. Costs sit around two-thirds of tier-one levels. The drawbacks start with isolation: the foreign community is small, English is much less common than in Shanghai, and there is no established nomad scene, so a Mandarin base or high tolerance for solitude matters. The firewall applies in full, coworking options are limited compared with tier-one cities, typhoons disrupt late summer, and domestic tourist crowds swamp the main sights during national holidays.

Who Xiamen Is For

Xiamen suits the self-sufficient nomad who wants a coastal Chinese city with real quality of life and does not need a ready-made expat network: solo workers with steady clients, Mandarin learners looking for a calmer environment than Beijing, cyclists and runners drawn to the seafront, and anyone burnt out on megacity density but unwilling to give up fast trains, cheap metro and same-day delivery. It is a strong second or third China destination for someone who has already learned how the country works. It is a poor fit for beginners who need English signage and hand-holding, for social nomads who want coworking friends and meetups on tap, and for anyone whose job cannot tolerate VPN-dependent access to Western tools. Those needing long visa runways will also find the 30-day ceiling restrictive, and travellers sensitive to humidity should avoid the June to September window entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions About Xiamen

How much does it cost to live in Xiamen as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Xiamen is around

,200 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Xiamen?

Xiamen rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Xiamen safe for digital nomads?

Xiamen scores 9 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.
