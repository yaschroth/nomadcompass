Setting Up as a Nomad in Palawan

Experience best island in the world, underground rivers, lagoons, and pristine nature. For remote workers, Palawan leans on nature and low costs, with a typical budget near $1,500 a month. The main trade-off is internet speed.

Cost of Living in Palawan

Palawan is one of Southeast Asia's more affordable island destinations, though costs vary significantly between Puerto Princesa (the provincial capital) and resort towns like El Nido or Coron. Budget travelers can live well; comfort seekers will still spend less than in most European or North American cities.

  • Rent, central area (Puerto Princesa): PHP 14,000-20,000/month (~$240-360 USD) for a furnished studio or one-bedroom near Rizal Avenue
  • Rent, cheaper area: PHP 9,000-14,000/month (~$160-240 USD) in suburban barangays or farther from the center
  • Groceries: PHP 6,000-9,000/month (~$100-160 USD) cooking mostly at home with local produce, rice, eggs, and chicken
  • Eating out: PHP 200-300 ($3.50-5.50) for a local carinderia meal; PHP 600-900 ($10-16) at a sit-down Western or seafood restaurant
  • Coworking hot-desk: PHP 200-400/day (~$3.50-7), or PHP 3,000-6,000/month (~$55-110) at spaces like Workhub Palawan or Think Space
  • Local transport: PHP 15-50 ($0.25-0.90) per tricycle ride within town; PHP 8-15 per jeepney trip in Puerto Princesa
  • Total monthly range: PHP 35,000-65,000/month (~$620-1,150 USD) for a comfortable but not extravagant nomad lifestyle in Puerto Princesa. El Nido runs 30-50% higher due to its tourist economy.

Where to Work

Puerto Princesa is the only town in Palawan with reliable coworking infrastructure. Workhub Palawan on Rizal Avenue offers unlimited WiFi, air conditioning, printing, a meeting room, and coffee for a daily or monthly fee. Think Space Co-working and Study Lounge is another well-rated option with fast fiber and private conference rooms suited to video calls.

Palawan Coliving in Tiniguiban doubles as both accommodation and a coworking venue, which suits nomads who want everything in one place. In El Nido, purpose-built coworking is sparse: Hama Coffee is your most reliable cafe-work option, with consistent WiFi and a staff that tolerates long working sessions. AP Kala at Town Square also has WiFi and a power-friendly setup, though it fills with tourists by midday.

The WiFi situation is honest: fiber connections reach urban Puerto Princesa and deliver 50-100 Mbps at good spots. El Nido is patchier; speeds can drop to 5-15 Mbps during peak hours, and power outages happen, especially during typhoon season. A local SIM with a data plan (Smart or Globe, around PHP 999-1,299/month) is essential as a backup. Starlink is available at some coliving and hotel properties and dramatically improves reliability.

Getting Around

Within Puerto Princesa, tricycles (motorbike-sidecar combos) are the default mode of transport. Negotiate the fare before you board; tourist rates start around PHP 50-80 for short hops. Jeepneys run fixed routes for PHP 8-15 and are the cheapest way to get between districts. Grab (the Southeast Asian rideshare app) operates in Puerto Princesa and is increasingly convenient for metered fares.

Getting from Puerto Princesa Airport to the city center takes 10-15 minutes by tricycle (roughly PHP 100-150). El Nido's smaller Lio Airport sits about 4 km north of town; a tricycle costs PHP 150-200. The overland van journey between Puerto Princesa and El Nido takes 5-6 hours over a winding mountain road and costs PHP 650-900 by shared van with operators like Cherry Bus. Air Swift operates direct flights between El Nido and Manila, cutting the journey to under an hour if budget allows.

Palawan is long and thin with no ring road. Beyond Puerto Princesa and El Nido, transport becomes infrequent and multi-step. If you plan to move around the island regularly, build in buffer time and do not rely on tight schedules.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of most Western countries (US, UK, EU, Australia, Canada) enter the Philippines visa-free for 30 days. You can extend at any Bureau of Immigration office for an additional 29 days (initial extension), then continue extending in roughly two-month blocks, up to a maximum of 2 years total stay. Each extension costs around PHP 3,000-4,000 ($55-70) in fees. The Puerto Princesa BI office handles extensions without requiring a trip back to Manila.

The Philippines launched its Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) in June 2025 under Executive Order No. 86. It requires proof of remote work, foreign-sourced income of at least $24,000/year, valid health insurance, and a clean police record. It grants a one-year stay with multiple-entry privileges and is renewable. As of mid-2026 the program is still in pilot phase and availability depends on reciprocal agreements with your home country. Always confirm current rules with the Bureau of Immigration at immigration.gov.ph or your nearest Philippine embassy before travel.

Best Time to Visit

The dry season runs from late November through May, with February and March considered the sweet spot: skies are clear, seas are calm, and temperatures hover around 27-30C (80-86F). This is also the peak tourist season, so accommodation fills up and prices are at their highest in El Nido and Coron.

December and January are excellent for work-travel balance: reliable weather, slightly fewer crowds than February-April, and a festive atmosphere in Puerto Princesa. April and May bring intense heat and humidity but remain dry.

The wet season (June to October) brings regular afternoon downpours, occasional typhoons in the north, and choppy seas that ground island-hopping boats. Internet connections can become less reliable during storms. If your work is deadline-sensitive and you rely on stable power and connectivity, the wet season in northern Palawan (El Nido especially) carries real risk. Puerto Princesa is somewhat more protected and can still function well for remote work in the early wet season months.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Some of Southeast Asia's most spectacular scenery: limestone karsts, turquoise lagoons, and white-sand beaches within easy reach
  • Pro: Genuinely low cost of living by island standards, with strong English and a welcoming local culture
  • Pro: Puerto Princesa has improving coworking and fiber internet infrastructure, and a Starlink rollout at many accommodation options
  • Pro: Slow-paced, safe, and easy to live in for extended periods; far less chaotic than Manila or Cebu City
  • Pro: Visa extensions are straightforward and handled locally without a trip to the capital
  • Con: Reliable fast WiFi is limited to Puerto Princesa; El Nido remains inconsistent for video calls and heavy uploads
  • Con: The island is long and transport between towns is slow, expensive by local standards, or requires a flight
  • Con: The nomad community is thin: you will find travelers but not a built-in coworking culture or meetup scene
  • Con: Wet season brings genuine weather disruption, not just inconvenience, especially in the north
  • Con: El Nido and Coron run on tourism pricing; a beachfront lifestyle here costs more than comparably beautiful spots elsewhere in Asia

Who Palawan Is Best For

Palawan works best for remote workers who can keep a flexible schedule and do not need 9-to-5 reliability from their internet. If you are between projects, on a creative break, or doing async work, the environment is exceptional. Writers, designers, photographers, and consultants with flexible deadlines tend to thrive here.

It suits people who want to spend meaningful time outdoors, snorkeling, island-hopping, or hiking, and treat work as something you do efficiently in the mornings before the rest of the day opens up. Puerto Princesa is the better base for anyone who genuinely needs daily coworking access, while El Nido is ideal for shorter stays where the natural beauty is the point.

Skip Palawan if you need consistent high-speed internet for video production or large file transfers, want an active nomad social scene with regular events, or plan to base yourself in one of the resort towns for months at a time. It is also a harder sell if you dislike heat and humidity, since air conditioning is your primary relief for six or more months of the year.

Frequently Asked Questions About Palawan

How much does it cost to live in Palawan as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Palawan is around $1,500 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Palawan?

With a WiFi score of 3/10, connectivity in Palawan is patchy for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Palawan safe for digital nomads?

Palawan scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Palawan a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Palawan is worth a look if you value nature and low costs. Costs sit near $1,500 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
