Cost of Living in Miami

Miami is one of the most expensive cities in the United States, and rent is the reason. Expect to pay a real premium for a place near the water or the action.

  • Rent, central (Brickell, Downtown, South Beach): a one-bedroom runs roughly $2,600-3,500 per month, and waterfront or brand-new buildings push well past that.
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Little Haiti, Flagami, West Miami, further inland): more like $1,800-2,300 for a one-bedroom, though you will likely need a car out here.
  • Groceries: around $400-500 per month for one person, higher than the US average.
  • Eating out: $15-25 per person at a casual spot, and $75-120 for a mid-range dinner for two. A latte is about $5, a beer around $8.
  • Coworking hot desk: monthly memberships start near $200-250, with day passes commonly in the $25-40 range.
  • Transport: budget for a car. A used car, insurance, gas, and parking can add several hundred dollars a month. If you skip the car, rely on rideshare and transit and expect $150-300 depending on how far you go.

Realistic monthly total for one person: roughly $3,500-5,500 all in, depending heavily on your neighborhood and whether you run a car. Miami sits well above the US average on almost every line, so treat it as a high-cost base.

Where to Work

Miami has a dense, well-established coworking scene, concentrated in Brickell, Downtown, and Wynwood. WeWork holds multiple locations across those areas with reliable internet and meeting rooms. Mindspace runs a polished space at the edge of Wynwood and Midtown. The LAB Miami in Wynwood is the original creative warehouse space, with hot desks starting around $250 per month. Buro is a quieter boutique option in Brickell built for focused work, and Quest Workspaces puts you in the center of Brickell.

Cafe-work culture exists but is uneven. Wynwood and the Design District have plenty of specialty coffee shops that tolerate laptops, while Brickell leans more toward quick-turnover business lunches. Home and building WiFi is generally fast and dependable in newer central apartments, but do not assume every cafe has strong, stable WiFi, so a coworking day pass or a good mobile hotspot is worth having as backup.

Getting Around

Miami is a car city, and that is the honest starting point. It sprawls, blocks are long, summer heat makes walking miserable, and most neighborhoods assume you drive. Many nomads rent or buy a car, and rideshare covers the gaps.

That said, a car-light life is possible if you stay central. The Metromover is a free automated people-mover looping through Downtown and Brickell, genuinely useful if you live and work in that core. The Metrorail is a single elevated line running from Kendall through Coral Gables and Downtown up toward Hialeah, and it connects to Metromover at Government Center and Brickell. Brightline, the higher-speed intercity train, runs from MiamiCentral station Downtown up to Fort Lauderdale, West Palm Beach, and Orlando, which is handy for weekend trips without a car.

For the airport: Miami International (MIA) connects to Metrorail via the free MIA Mover train from the terminal, and a Metrorail ride Downtown is about $2.25 and takes roughly 20 minutes. Brightline also runs a same-day connector shuttle between MiamiCentral and MIA for about $12.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Most travelers from common Visa Waiver Program countries (much of Europe, the UK, Australia, Japan, and others) can enter the United States for tourism for up to 90 days without a visa. You still need an approved ESTA before you travel, tied to an e-passport, and as of late 2025 the ESTA fee is $40, valid for two years or until your passport expires.

The 90 days is a hard limit under the Visa Waiver Program, and you cannot extend a Visa Waiver stay from inside the country, so longer stays generally mean a proper visa. There is no US digital-nomad visa, so remote workers usually cycle through on the 90-day allowance or pursue a work or business visa, which is a serious process. US citizens and green-card holders can stay indefinitely. Rules and fees change, so confirm your exact situation with official US sources (travel.state.gov and the CBP ESTA site) before booking.

Best Time to Visit

The clear winner for remote work is December through April. Days sit in the 70s and low 80s, humidity eases off, skies are clear, and rainfall is low. It is the most comfortable stretch to actually get work done and enjoy the city, though prices and crowds peak then too.

Summer, roughly June through September, is hot and sticky, with daytime highs near 90 and humidity often around 80 percent, plus near-daily afternoon thunderstorms that usually pass quickly. It is also the cheapest window if you can handle the heat. Note that hurricane season runs June through November, with the highest risk from mid-August through the end of September. Storms are manageable if you watch forecasts, but it is a real factor when planning a longer stay.

Pros and Cons

Pros:

  • Dense, mature coworking scene and strong internet in central buildings.
  • Warm winters that are genuinely pleasant for outdoor life and focused work.
  • Deeply bilingual, with Spanish spoken everywhere and a strong Latin American cultural pull.
  • Beaches, an international food scene, and easy weekend trips by Brightline.

Cons:

  • High cost of living, driven hard by rent.
  • Heavy car reliance outside the Downtown and Brickell core.
  • Brutal summer heat and humidity, plus daily storms.
  • Hurricane season is a genuine seasonal risk from June to November.

Who Miami Is Best For

Miami suits nomads with a solid budget who want a warm, energetic, international base and do not mind paying for it. It is a strong fit if you thrive on nightlife, beaches, and a Latin, bilingual culture, and if you plan to stay central so you can lean on Metromover and rideshare instead of buying a car. It is especially good over the winter months.

Skip it if you are on a tight budget, want a walkable or transit-first city, or dislike heat and humidity. If your main goal is cheap, low-stress focused work, other US or Latin American cities will stretch your money much further.

Frequently Asked Questions About Miami

How much does it cost to live in Miami as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $5,000 a month in Miami for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Miami?

Miami rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Miami safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Miami is 5/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Miami a good city for digital nomads overall?

Miami stands out for nightlife and English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $5,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
