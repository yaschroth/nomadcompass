Setting Up as a Nomad in Ankara

Turkey's working capital: ministries, universities, and none of Istanbul's crowds. Ankara pairs low costs with fast WiFi and a continental climate, all for about $880 a month. If anything, the language barrier lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Ankara

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Kizilay

$$
Central & Dense

The city's central square, students and cheap eating

Ankara's functional heart, where the metro lines cross and where the bookshops, cheap restaurants, student bars and political life of the capital concentrate. It is dense, loud and entirely practical, with the ministries a short walk south and everything reachable on the metro.

Best for: Anyone who wants the metro, the bookshops and cheap eating on the doorstep
Pros
  • Both metro lines and every bus route
  • Dense cheap restaurants and student bars
  • Everything practical within a short walk
Cons
  • Loud, crowded and short of green space
  • Demonstrations and heavy policing at times
  • Older apartment stock

Cankaya and Kavaklidere

$$$$
Diplomatic & Leafy

The embassy districts, leafier and considerably calmer

The southern hillside where the embassies, the better restaurants and the wine bars of Tunali Hilmi sit among tree-lined streets. It is the most comfortable part of Ankara, with the largest concentration of foreign residents and the best food in the city.

Best for: Longer stays wanting a calm district and the international community
Pros
  • The best restaurants and wine bars in the city
  • Embassies and the international community nearby
  • Tree-lined and quieter than Kizilay
Cons
  • The highest rents in Ankara
  • Steep streets in places
  • Heavy security presence around some embassies

Ulus and the Citadel

$
Historic & Cheap

The old town below the walls, with the best museum in Turkey

The historic core around the Ottoman citadel, holding the Museum of Anatolian Civilisations, the Roman temple, the old bazaar and the surviving timber houses inside the walls. It is the oldest and cheapest part of Ankara and by a distance the most interesting to walk.

Best for: Budget stays that want the citadel and the museums rather than the modern city
Pros
  • The Museum of Anatolian Civilisations on the doorstep
  • The lowest rents anywhere central
  • Genuine Ottoman streets inside the walls
Cons
  • Run down in parts and less comfortable at night
  • Older housing in poor condition
  • Few modern services

Bahcelievler and Emek

$$
Student & Lively

The university belt around Hacettepe and the campuses

The western residential districts around the universities, with the seventh street bar strip, cheap student restaurants and low-rise apartment buildings. It has the liveliest and cheapest social life in Ankara and a population that is overwhelmingly under twenty-five.

Best for: Budget stays that want the student bars and the cheapest central rents
Pros
  • Cheap student bars and restaurants throughout
  • Rents well below Cankaya
  • Metro and buses into the centre
Cons
  • Quiet outside term
  • Plain apartment blocks
  • Loud on term-time nights

Cayyolu and the Western Suburbs

$$$
Suburban & Modern

New apartment complexes out west, space and quiet

The planned residential expansion west of the city along the metro extension, with modern gated apartment complexes, supermarkets, schools and considerably more space than anything central. It is where Ankara's professional families have moved, and the metro takes about half an hour into Kizilay.

Best for: Longer stays wanting a modern, well-built flat and quiet
Pros
  • Modern, well-insulated apartments
  • Metro line direct into the centre
  • Cleaner air and more green space
Cons
  • Half an hour to the centre
  • Car-dependent for anything off the metro
  • Generic and short on street life

Cost of Living in Ankara

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Ankara, so the $880 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 801 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget roughly $880 a month as a solo remote worker in Ankara, which makes it appreciably cheaper than Istanbul for a similar standard of city living. A one-bedroom apartment in Cankaya, Kizilay or Bahcelievler runs about $300 to $450, and the newer districts out towards Cayyolu cost about the same for more space and a longer commute. Groceries land around $180 to $250, helped by neighbourhood pazar markets that undercut supermarkets on produce by a wide margin. A lunch of doner or pide is $3 to $5, a proper restaurant dinner with drinks $15 to $25, and a monthly transit pass about $25. Utilities run $60 to $110 and spike in winter, when Ankara's continental climate makes heating a real line item rather than an afterthought.

Where to Work in Ankara

Ankara is a government and university city rather than a tourist one, and its working spaces reflect that. Coworking clusters around Kizilay, Cankaya and the technology parks attached to METU and Bilkent, where rates run about $90 to $150 a month and the other members are more likely to be Turkish founders and engineers than visiting nomads. Cafe culture is strong and laptop-friendly, particularly in the student areas around Bahcelievler and Tunali Hilmi, and third-wave coffee has arrived properly in the last few years. Fixed-line internet is fast and cheap, and mobile data is good, though foreign SIM cards get throttled after 120 days unless the handset is registered, which is a well-known and expensive piece of Turkish bureaucracy worth planning around.

Getting Around Ankara

Ankara has a metro with four lines plus the older Ankaray light rail, and between them they cover the spine of the city from Kizilay outwards. Buses and shared dolmus minibuses fill everything else, and an Ankarakart smart card pays for all of it at roughly $0.40 a ride. The city is spread out and hilly, so walking works within a neighbourhood rather than between them. Taxis are metered, plentiful and cheap by European standards. The high-speed train to Istanbul takes about four and a half hours and to Konya under two, both from Ankara YHT station, and is much more pleasant than flying. Esenboga airport is 28km north with a frequent Havas bus. Traffic is heavy at rush hour and driving is not recommended for newcomers.

Visas for Ankara

Turkey grants most Western passports a 90-day stay in any 180-day period, with EU, UK and Canadian citizens entering visa-free and some nationalities needing an e-Visa bought online in advance. Turkey has no digital nomad visa in the usual sense, but it does have a short-term residence permit, the kisa donem ikamet, which is the standard route for anyone staying longer. It is applied for online through the e-ikamet system, requires proof of address, health insurance and funds, and has become noticeably harder to obtain in Istanbul in recent years. Ankara is generally reported as more straightforward. Turkey also has a well-known citizenship-by-investment property route. Anyone planning to stay should start the residence process well before the 90 days expire.

Best Time to Visit Ankara

Ankara sits on the Anatolian plateau at about 900 metres, which gives it a genuinely continental climate and four distinct seasons. Spring, April to early June, and autumn, September to October, are the best: warm days, cool nights, clear light and comfortable walking weather. Summer is hot and very dry, with highs in the low thirties, but the altitude keeps nights cool and the humidity is low, so it is far more bearable than the coast. Winter, December to February, is genuinely cold, regularly below freezing, with snow and grey skies, and air quality dips as heating ramps up. There is no tourist season to speak of, so accommodation prices are stable year-round, which is one quiet advantage of a city that does not live on visitors.

Pros and Cons of Ankara

Ankara is cheap, functional and largely undiscovered by remote workers, which is either its main appeal or its main problem depending on what you want. It has a real metro, excellent and inexpensive food, a serious university and technology sector, fast internet, and a central position that puts the whole of Anatolia within reach by good trains. It is far calmer and less crowded than Istanbul. Against that, it is not a beautiful city, the tourist sights amount to a strong museum and a hilltop citadel, and there is almost no international nomad community. English is limited outside the universities and technology parks. Winters are cold and the air quality suffers. Anyone arriving expecting Istanbul's drama will find a working capital instead.

Who Ankara Is For

Ankara suits remote workers who want to live in Turkey properly rather than pass through it, who are willing to learn some Turkish, and who value low costs and a functioning city over scenery and company. It works well for anyone building ties to the Turkish technology sector, for people who want a base for exploring Anatolia by train, and for those who find Istanbul overwhelming or too expensive. It is a reasonable choice for families, with safe neighbourhoods and good private schools. It is a poor fit for anyone who needs an English-speaking environment, an existing nomad crowd, beach access or nightlife on any scale. Those wanting Turkey with all of that should look at Istanbul, Izmir or the coast.

Frequently Asked Questions About Ankara

How much does it cost to live in Ankara as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Ankara is around $880 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Ankara?

Ankara rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is good for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Ankara safe for digital nomads?

Ankara scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Ankara a good city for digital nomads overall?

Ankara stands out for affordability and fast internet among remote workers. With a typical budget near $880 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
