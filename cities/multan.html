Setting Up as a Nomad in Multan

The city of saints, where blue-tiled Sufi shrines stand over five thousand years of settlement. What pulls nomads here is low costs, backed by food and culture. Budget around $420 a month. Air quality is the weak spot.

Best Neighborhoods in Multan

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Cantt

$$$
Ordered & Green

The quiet, ordered military district

The cantonment south of the old city, with wide roads, mature trees, low density and the best-maintained infrastructure in Multan. It has the reliable power and water that the rest of the city does not, along with the better restaurants and hotels, and it is the calmest place to stay here.

Best for: Anyone who wants reliable services and quiet
Pros
  • Best power and water supply in the city
  • Wide, tree-lined and genuinely quiet
  • Good restaurants and hotels nearby
Cons
  • Highest rents in Multan
  • Security checkpoints and restricted areas
  • Nothing within walking distance

Gulgasht Colony

$$
Residential & Modern

The middle-class north, near the university

A large planned residential district in the north of the city, close to Bahauddin Zakariya University and the Nishtar hospital complex, with a good supply of newer houses and flats, decent fibre coverage and the shops and bakeries that a settled middle-class area brings.

Best for: A monthly rental with fibre and a working generator
Pros
  • Better fibre coverage than the older city
  • Newer housing with proper wiring
  • University and hospital both close
Cons
  • A rickshaw is needed for the old city
  • Little of interest within the area itself
  • Load shedding still applies

The walled city and the fort mound

$
Ancient & Crowded

The shrines, the bazaars and the blue tilework

The historic core on and around the old fort mound, where the shrines of Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Bahauddin Zakariya stand above the bazaars, the Ghanta Ghar clock tower marks the centre, and the lanes of the walled city run below. It is the reason Multan is on this list.

Best for: A short stay focused on the shrines and the old city
Pros
  • The two great shrines within walking distance
  • The bazaars and the food are here
  • Cheapest rooms and meals in the city
Cons
  • Extremely congested and hard to reach by car
  • Almost no modern accommodation
  • Air quality is at its worst here

Model Town

$$
Settled & Affordable

Planned housing between the old city and the north

One of the older planned colonies, with a grid of streets, mature trees and a mix of houses and small apartment blocks at rents below Cantt and Gulgasht. It sits between the historic core and the newer northern districts and has most everyday shops within it.

Best for: Balancing cost against services
Pros
  • Rents below Cantt and Gulgasht
  • Everyday shops and markets within the colony
  • Reasonable access to both ends of the city
Cons
  • Older housing stock and variable wiring
  • Fibre coverage is patchy street to street
  • Little English spoken

Cost of Living in Multan

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Multan, so the $420 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 1000 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Multan runs about $420 a month for one person, which makes it one of the cheapest cities anywhere on this site. A furnished one-bedroom in Gulgasht or Model Town is $90 to $170, and older parts of the city are cheaper again. Food costs very little: a plate of nihari or a seer of Multani sohan halwa is a couple of dollars, and a full restaurant meal for two rarely passes $10. There is essentially no coworking market, so budget for home internet instead. Fibre or cable broadband is $10 to $18 a month. Rickshaws are under a dollar across the centre. Utilities are the variable, since summer cooling can add $40 to $70.

Where to Work in Multan

Multan has no meaningful coworking sector, so working here means a flat with a good connection. Fibre has reached Model Town, Gulgasht and Cantt through PTCL, Nayatel and local operators, delivering 20 to 100 Mbps for $10 to $18 a month, and it is the single most important thing to check before signing for a place. Mobile data from Jazz or Zong is a workable backup and cheap. Cafes exist around Bosan Road and the newer commercial strips but are social spaces rather than places to sit with a laptop. The real constraint is load shedding: scheduled power cuts are still common in summer, so a UPS or an inverter is essential rather than optional.

Getting Around Multan

Multan is flat and the old city around the shrines is walkable, though the traffic on the arterial roads is not. Autorickshaws and the Qingqi share-rickshaws cover the city for well under a dollar, and Careem and inDrive both operate. The Multan Metrobus runs a dedicated corridor across the city and costs a few cents. A motorcycle is what most residents use. The railway station puts you on the main line between Karachi and Lahore, with Lahore about four to five hours by fast train. Multan International Airport handles domestic flights plus Gulf routes to Dubai, Sharjah, Doha and Jeddah, which makes arriving from outside Pakistan reasonably simple.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Multan

Pakistan overhauled its visa system and most nationalities can now apply online through the NADRA visa portal, with tourist visas commonly issued for three months and business visas for longer. Processing is usually quick and a sponsor is no longer required for tourism from most countries. There is no digital nomad visa. Extensions are handled online but are not guaranteed. The practical constraint is not the visa but the travel advice: several governments advise against travel to parts of Pakistan, and southern Punjab is treated more cautiously than Lahore or Islamabad. Foreigners in some districts are subject to police registration and occasionally to a mandatory escort, which affects how freely you can move.

Best Time to Visit Multan

November to March is the only sensible window. Winter days are mild at 20C to 25C with cold nights, and December and January bring fog that regularly disrupts flights and road travel across Punjab. April to June is extreme: Multan is one of the hottest cities in Pakistan and 45C is a normal May afternoon, with dust storms on top. The monsoon reaches here weakly in July and August, adding humidity to the heat without much relief, though it can also flood. September and October are hot but improving. Urs festivals at the shrines of Bahauddin Zakariya and Shah Rukn-e-Alam are the events worth timing around, both in the cooler half of the year.

Pros and Cons of Multan

The case for Multan is history and price. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in South Asia, its Sufi shrines are extraordinary and the blue-tiled Multani kashi work on them is unlike anything else in the region, and the whole month costs less than a week in Western Europe. The mangoes are the best in the country. Food is superb and cheap. Against that, and it is a long list: no coworking, no foreign community, summer heat that makes four months unworkable, load shedding, poor air quality, limited English outside educated circles, and travel advisories that will complicate your insurance. Alcohol is effectively unavailable and nightlife does not exist.

Who Multan Is For

Multan suits an experienced traveller with a specific reason to be in southern Punjab: Sufi architecture, Pakistani history, family connections, or work in the region. It is not a place you drift into. If you have been to Pakistan before, know how the registration rules work in your district and have a flat with fibre and an inverter, the cost of living is remarkable and the city rewards attention. It does not suit a first trip to South Asia, anyone who needs a community of other remote workers, anyone travelling on insurance that excludes advisory areas, or anyone visiting between April and June. Lahore is the sane entry point to Pakistan for a newcomer.

Frequently Asked Questions About Multan

How much does it cost to live in Multan as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Multan is around $420 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Multan?

Multan rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Multan safe for digital nomads?

Multan scores 5 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is average for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Multan a good city for digital nomads overall?

Multan stands out for affordability and its food scene among remote workers. With a typical budget near $420 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
