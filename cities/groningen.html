Living in Groningen as a Digital Nomad

Student-powered bicycle kingdom, nightlife, innovation, and northern Dutch grit. Plan on roughly $2,400 a month. Its strengths are fast WiFi and safety, while the size of the nomad scene is where it scores lower.

Cost of Living in Groningen

Groningen is one of the cheaper places to base yourself in the Netherlands, mostly because it sits far from the Randstad and runs on a student economy. It is not cheap by global nomad standards, but for a Western European university city your euros stretch further here than in Amsterdam or Utrecht. Rents are usually quoted in euros, so figures below list the local price with an approximate US dollar equivalent at roughly 1.08 dollars per euro (check the current rate, as it moves).

  • Rent, one-bedroom in the centre: around EUR 1,000 to 1,150 per month (about USD 1,080 to 1,240). Furnished short-term rentals aimed at internationals sit at the top of that range.
  • Rent, one-bedroom in a cheaper area: around EUR 700 to 850 per month (about USD 760 to 920) outside the ring, in districts like Vinkhuizen or Paddepoel.
  • Groceries: roughly EUR 200 to 300 per month (about USD 215 to 325). Shopping at Lidl or the Vismarkt market (Tuesday, Friday, Saturday) keeps this low.
  • Eating out: a single course runs EUR 9 to 20 (about USD 10 to 22), and a mid-range three-course dinner lands near EUR 35 (about USD 38). A coffee is EUR 3 to 4.50.
  • Coworking hot desk: roughly EUR 42 per day (about USD 45), or EUR 95 to 260 per month (about USD 100 to 280) depending on whether you want a flex seat or a dedicated desk.
  • Local transport: close to nothing if you cycle. A secondhand bike costs EUR 80 to 150, and a bus ride within the city is a few euros.

A realistic solo total, renting your own place, falls somewhere between EUR 1,600 and 2,500 per month (about USD 1,730 to 2,700), with rent doing most of the moving. Sharing a room or a student-style flat pushes the low end well under that.

Where to Work

The dedicated coworking scene is small but real. Launch Cafe on Herestraat is the best-known independent option, right in the city centre with fast WiFi, good coffee, a genuine community, and even a small fablab with 3D printers and a laser cutter. For a more corporate flex setup, Spaces and Regus both operate here, with global all-access plans if you move between cities, and locations near the Zernike Campus and Europark as well as central spots.

Cafe-work culture is strong because this is a student town. Places like Koffiestation and the many independent coffee bars around the centre are used to people settling in with a laptop for a few hours. WiFi across the city is genuinely fast and reliable, and Dutch mobile data is excellent, so tethering is a solid backup. Just be aware that popular cafes get loud and busy around exam periods and lunchtime.

Getting Around

Groningen is arguably the most bike-friendly city on earth. Around 61 percent of all trips in the city are made by bicycle, the centre is compact and completely flat, and the road layout was deliberately redesigned decades ago to favour bikes over cars. Get a bike in your first week and you will barely think about transport again. You can cross the whole city in 15 to 20 minutes, and cyclists get priority infrastructure, including traffic lights that extend the green when it rains.

The centre is also very walkable, and city and regional buses (Qbuzz) cover anywhere a bike will not. For flights, the local airport is Groningen Eelde (GRQ), a small regional field about a 50-minute bus ride from Central Station with limited routes. In practice most nomads fly from Amsterdam Schiphol (AMS), which is reached by a direct hourly intercity train that takes roughly 2 hours and 10 to 20 minutes from Groningen Central Station.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

The Netherlands has no dedicated digital nomad visa. If you hold a passport that gets visa-free Schengen entry, you can stay up to 90 days within any 180-day period across the whole Schengen area, which covers most short and medium stays without paperwork.

For staying longer, US citizens have a well-worn route: the Dutch American Friendship Treaty (DAFT). You register as self-employed (a ZZP or a BV), invest and maintain at least EUR 4,500 in your business, and in return receive a residence permit valid for two years, renewable for a further five if you still meet the conditions. IND application fees are in the low hundreds of euros. You do not need to speak Dutch for the permit itself, though citizenship later would require the integration exam. Non-US, non-EU nationals generally face the much stricter self-employed residence permit, which demands you prove your business adds value to the Netherlands.

Immigration rules and fees change, so confirm the current requirements with the official IND (Immigration and Naturalisation Service) before you commit.

Best Time to Visit

Be honest with yourself about the weather. Groningen has a cool maritime climate with grey, wet, windy winters, and January averages only about 2.5 degrees Celsius (37 Fahrenheit). Rain is spread fairly evenly across the year, and the wind off the North Sea can be relentless from November to March. Summers are mild rather than hot, with July around 17.5 degrees Celsius (63 Fahrenheit) and long daylight hours.

The best window for remote work and general quality of life is mid-May to mid-September, when it is warmest, sunniest, and the terraces are full. If you dislike short dark days and horizontal drizzle, treat winter here as a working-indoors season rather than an exploring one.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Cheaper than the Randstad, with lower rent and everyday costs.
  • Pro: World-class cycling and a compact, flat, walkable centre.
  • Pro: Young, lively, and very international thanks to tens of thousands of students.
  • Pro: Excellent WiFi and easygoing cafe-work culture.
  • Con: Housing is tight and student-driven, so finding a good short-term rental takes effort.
  • Con: Grey, wet, windy weather for much of the year.
  • Con: Smaller dedicated nomad scene than big-name hubs, so you build your own network.
  • Con: Schiphol is a two-hour-plus train away, so international travel needs planning.

Who Groningen Is Best For

Groningen suits you if you want a genuine, affordable Dutch city with a young energy, you love cycling, and you value a walkable everyday life over a large expat-nomad crowd. It is a great fit for remote workers who enjoy student-town culture, cafe life, and easy access to the rest of the Netherlands and Germany. US citizens eyeing a longer stay have a clear legal path through DAFT, which makes it more practical here than in many European cities.

You should probably skip it if you need constant sunshine, a big built-in nomad community, or fast international flight connections from your doorstep. If a plug-and-play scene and warm weather matter most, a southern European hub will serve you better, and you can always visit Groningen for a summer stint instead.

Frequently Asked Questions About Groningen

How much does it cost to live in Groningen as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,400 a month in Groningen for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Groningen?

Groningen rates 9 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Groningen safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Groningen is 9/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Groningen a good city for digital nomads overall?

Groningen stands out for fast WiFi and safety among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,400 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
