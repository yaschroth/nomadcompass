Setting Up as a Nomad in Kamakura

A laid-back seaside temple town south of Tokyo, home to a giant bronze Buddha, Zen gardens, hydrangea trails, and surf beaches. Plan on roughly $1,600 a month. Its strengths are safety and cleanliness, while the size of the nomad scene is where it scores lower.

Cost of Living in Kamakura

Kamakura is more affordable than central Tokyo, though it is far from a budget destination. A single nomad should plan on roughly $1,400 to $1,900 a month, depending on how you handle rent. A small flat near the station commands a premium, while sharing or living a few stops out lowers the figure considerably. Groceries and eating at local spots are reasonable, and a rail pass makes the Tokyo commute predictable. These are editorial estimates rather than precise quotes, and prices swing with the yen and the season. Weekend crowds can nudge cafe and restaurant spending upward if you are not careful.

  • Rent (one-bed near the station): a clear majority of your budget
  • Groceries and home cooking: moderate, with fresh produce and seafood widely available
  • Eating out: friendly local set meals to pricier tourist-facing spots on Komachi-dori
  • Transport: an IC card or commuter pass for the frequent trains to Tokyo and Yokohama

Where to Work in Kamakura

Kamakura suits a calm, focused working rhythm rather than a buzzing coworking scene. Internet at flats and guesthouses is generally fast and reliable, so many nomads simply work from home. Independent cafes are scattered around the station, Komachi-dori and the Hase area, and several welcome laptop users during quieter hours, though seats fill fast at weekends. Dedicated coworking is limited compared with Tokyo, so some residents keep a membership in the city and travel in when they need a desk or meetings. For deep work, the temples, beaches and wooded hills nearby offer easy breaks, and Yokohama is a short train ride away for larger offices.

Getting Around Kamakura

Kamakura is compact and best explored on foot, by bicycle or on its charming local railways. The JR Yokosuka Line links the town to Tokyo in around an hour, while the vintage Enoden line trundles along the coast between Kamakura, Hase and Enoshima, passing the sea and back gardens on the way. Central sights, Komachi-dori and Tsurugaoka Hachimangu sit within easy walking distance of the main station. Buses reach the hillier temples and quieter corners. Cycling is pleasant along the flatter coastal stretches, though the town gets busy on fine weekends. Most nomads find they rarely need a car here.

Visas for Kamakura

Kamakura follows Japan's national immigration rules, so there is nothing specific to the town itself. Many nationalities can enter visa-free for short stays, typically up to ninety days, which suits a trial visit. In 2024 Japan introduced a digital nomad visa allowing eligible nationals from certain countries to stay for up to six months while working remotely for overseas clients, subject to income and insurance conditions. Longer stays generally require a work, spouse or student status. Rules change and eligibility varies by passport, so always confirm the current requirements with an official Japanese source or embassy before planning a move, rather than relying on summaries.

Best Time to Visit Kamakura

Kamakura has a humid subtropical climate with four distinct seasons, each with its own character. Spring brings cherry blossom and mild, comfortable days, making it a popular arrival window. June is famous for hydrangeas, especially at Hase-dera and Meigetsu-in, though it coincides with the rainy season and sticky air. Summer is hot and humid but lively, with surfers and beachgoers filling the coast. Autumn offers crisp weather and fine foliage around the temples, arguably the most pleasant stretch for working and exploring. Winter is cool, quiet and clear, ideal if you prefer fewer crowds. Weekends and holidays draw heavy day-tripper traffic year round.

Pros and Cons of Kamakura

Kamakura appeals to nomads who want calm, nature and history within reach of a world city, but it asks for some compromises in exchange. The pace is gentle, the setting beautiful and the sense of safety reassuring, yet the small size and limited English can feel isolating for anyone expecting a large, ready-made expat scene. Weigh the coastal quiet against the practical realities of remote-work infrastructure and language before committing to a longer stay.

  • Pros: relaxed coastal setting, easy Tokyo access, very safe and clean, rich temple culture, surf and beaches
  • Cons: limited English, small nomad community, sparse dedicated coworking, heavy weekend crowds, rents higher than rural Japan

Who Kamakura Is For

Kamakura suits the self-sufficient remote worker who values tranquillity, the outdoors and cultural depth over a busy social calendar. It is a natural fit for artists, writers, surfers and anyone drawn to Zen temples, coastal walks and a slower daily rhythm, all while staying close enough to Tokyo for meetings and city life. Those who already have their own network, or who happily work solo, will thrive here. It is a weaker match for nomads who need a large English-speaking community, nightlife or plentiful coworking on their doorstep. Come for the quiet, the sea and the history, and treat Tokyo as your backup city.
