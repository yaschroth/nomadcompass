Why Siem Reap Works for Remote Work

Explore the ancient temples of Angkor and a surprisingly vibrant nomad scene. A tropical climate and strong culture make Siem Reap an easy base, and at about $1,200 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep cleanliness in mind.

Siem Reap remains one of Southeast Asia's most affordable cities for remote workers. Prices are quoted in US dollars, which circulate freely alongside the Cambodian Riel (KHR). Here is a realistic monthly breakdown for a solo nomad:

  • Rent (central, Wat Bo or Old Market area): $300-500 for a furnished one-bedroom apartment with AC
  • Rent (quieter outskirts, e.g. Sala Kamroeuk): $150-280 for similar space
  • Groceries (market shopping): $60-100 per month buying local produce
  • Eating out (local Khmer meals): $2-5 per meal (8,000-20,000 KHR); Western dishes $7-15
  • Coffee: $2-4 at specialty cafes
  • Coworking hot-desk: $5-15 per day; $70-120 per month depending on space
  • Local transport (tuk-tuk or Grab rides): $1-3 per trip in town
  • SIM card with 100GB data: roughly $15/month
  • Total comfortable range: $700-1,200 per month including rent, food, coworking, and occasional activities

Beer at a local bar can cost as little as $0.50-1. A one-hour massage runs $8-10. If you cook at home and skip coworking memberships, you can live comfortably on $600-800 per month.

Where to Work

Siem Reap has a small but functional remote work infrastructure. The city is not Chiang Mai, but it is more than workable for a stay of a few weeks to several months.

Coworking Spaces

  • AngkorHUB offers 150 Mbps internet, free coffee, a rooftop terrace, and 24/7 access on premium plans. Day passes start around $7; monthly memberships around $89.
  • Format CoWork on Wat Bo Road is the most productivity-focused option, with 100 Mbps symmetric fiber and a backup generator so power cuts do not stop your work. Reviews consistently rate it highly for speed and reliability.
  • BioLAB Coffee and Office on Wat Bo Road blends specialty coffee with proper desk infrastructure including ergonomic seating, two balconies, and personal lockers. Rated 4.5 on Tripadvisor.
  • Footprint Cafes Hub in Sala Kamroeuk runs a social enterprise cafe downstairs with a quieter coworking floor above, plus occasional community events and video recording equipment.

Cafe Work Culture

Many cafes welcome laptop workers. The Little Red Fox Espresso in Kandal Village has reliably fast WiFi and is a local favorite among nomads. Common Grounds Cafe and Temple Coffee n Bakery along the riverside are other solid options with consistent connectivity.

WiFi Reality Check

Average fixed-line speeds reach around 55 Mbps, and dedicated coworking spaces perform well. Cafe WiFi is hit-or-miss: do not rely on it for video calls at peak tourist hours. Power outages happen, especially during rainy season, so coworking spaces with generators are worth the price if you have deadlines.

Getting Around

Siem Reap is a low-rise, spread-out city. The central area around Old Market, Pub Street, and Wat Bo is walkable if you do not mind heat. Beyond that, you will rely on ride-hailing or rentals.

  • Grab app is the most reliable option for tuk-tuks and cars, with fixed upfront pricing. A cross-town tuk-tuk ride costs $2-4.
  • Bicycles rent for $3-5 per day and are practical for the flat terrain in the dry season.
  • Motorbike rentals are available for $5-10 per day for those comfortable riding in mixed traffic.
  • Airport transfers: The international airport is about 10 km from the city center. Grab taxis cost $12-18; official airport taxis run $25-35. A newer airport bus service connects to the city for around $8.

There is no city bus network worth relying on. Tuk-tuks without the Grab app will quote tourist prices, so always negotiate or use the app.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Cambodia is visa-friendly for most Western passport holders. You have several options depending on how long you plan to stay.

  • E-visa (Tourist/T visa): Available online before arrival for $30. Valid for 30 days, single entry. Extendable once for another 30 days through a local agent (around $50 in fees), giving you a maximum 60-day tourist stay.
  • Visa on arrival: Available at land borders and airports for the same $30 fee and 30-day entry.
  • E-class (Ordinary) visa: The long-term option for nomads. Apply on arrival or at a consulate, then extend through local visa agents for 1, 3, 6, or 12 months at a time. Many long-term residents extend repeatedly without leaving the country.
  • Digital nomad visa: Cambodia does not offer a dedicated digital nomad visa as of 2025-2026.
  • Entry requirement: All air arrivals must complete the Cambodia e-Arrival (CeA) card online within 7 days before arrival. It is free and replaces the paper arrival card.

Always verify current visa rules with the official Cambodian e-visa portal or your nearest Cambodian embassy before traveling.

Best Time to Visit

Siem Reap has two distinct seasons and one shoulder period worth knowing about.

  • Dry season (November to early April): The most comfortable window for remote work. Temperatures stay around 24-32 degrees Celsius with low humidity and minimal rain. December through February is the coolest and most pleasant stretch. This is also peak tourist season, so accommodation prices are higher and popular spots are busier.
  • Rainy season (May to October): Afternoon downpours are common, humidity is high, and temperatures peak around 35 degrees Celsius in April-May. Power outages increase during heavy storms. That said, rain is usually short-lived, mornings are often clear, and prices drop significantly.
  • Sweet spot for nomads: October to November. Rain tapers off, the surrounding landscape stays lush, crowds have not yet returned, and prices are still low. This is arguably the best balance of livability and value.

Air quality worsens in the dry season from January through March due to regional agricultural burning. If you are sensitive to air pollution, check IQAir readings before committing to those months.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Very low cost of living, with a comfortable lifestyle achievable on $800-1,000 per month
  • Extraordinary cultural setting with Angkor Wat and dozens of temples within easy reach
  • Growing nomad community with regular meetups and social enterprise cafes
  • English is widely spoken in the tourist and expat zones
  • Excellent and cheap food scene, from Khmer street stalls to acclaimed restaurants
  • Easy, affordable visa options for long stays via the E-class extension route
  • Generally safe for solo travelers with low rates of violent crime

Cons

  • Tourism dependency means constant sales pressure in central areas; interactions can feel transactional
  • Air quality is poor during dry season burning periods (January through March)
  • Coworking infrastructure is small; options are limited compared to Phnom Penh or Chiang Mai
  • Rainy season brings power cuts that can disrupt work
  • The city is quiet and can feel repetitive after two to three months
  • Heat and humidity from April through September are intense and draining

Who Siem Reap Is Best For

Siem Reap works well for nomads who genuinely want to engage with Cambodian culture, Khmer history, and a slower pace of life. If you are on a tight budget, this is one of the most affordable places in Asia to spend a month or two without sacrificing comfort. It suits people who value access to world-class archaeological sites and who are happy with a smaller, community-feel city over a buzzing metropolis.

Photographers, writers, and solo travelers who want immersive cultural experiences alongside their remote work will find it rewarding. The nomad community is small but welcoming, and the social enterprise scene means you can do meaningful co-working alongside locally-rooted people.

Skip Siem Reap if you need a large tech or startup community, high-speed infrastructure without workarounds, or a city with genuine nightlife beyond the Pub Street tourist circuit. If you bore quickly of temples and need constant urban stimulation, you may exhaust what Siem Reap offers within a few weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions About Siem Reap

How much does it cost to live in Siem Reap as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Siem Reap is around $1,200 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Siem Reap?

With a WiFi score of 5/10, connectivity in Siem Reap is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Siem Reap safe for digital nomads?

Siem Reap scores 6 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is workable for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Siem Reap a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Siem Reap is worth a look if you value culture and low costs. Costs sit near $1,200 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.

Which area of Siem Reap is best for digital nomads?

Most nomads stay within a short ride of the center around Pub Street and the Old Market for cafes and nightlife, while quieter residential pockets like Wat Bo and Sala Kamreuk offer calmer, cheaper rooms nearby. The town is small and flat, so a bicycle or scooter covers it easily.

What visa do I need for Siem Reap, Cambodia?

Cambodia is known for being straightforward: many nationalities can get a tourist e-visa or visa on arrival, and there is a business visa class that can be extended for long stays, which nomads often use. Confirm the current visa types and extension process through official sources before you rely on a long stay.

How cheap is Siem Reap for digital nomads?

It is one of the most affordable bases in the region, with a comfortable budget around $1,200 a month. Rooms, local food, and getting around all cost very little, and US dollars are used alongside the riel, which keeps day-to-day spending simple for visitors.

What is Siem Reap like beyond Angkor Wat?

It is the gateway to the Angkor temples, but the town itself is laid-back and green with a small, friendly nomad and expat scene, a growing cafe culture, and a low cost of living. It is quieter than the region's big hubs, which suits nomads who want calm, cheap, and warm over a busy scene.
