Setting Up as a Nomad in Inverness

The capital of the Highlands, at the end of a loch everyone has heard of. Plan on roughly $1,550 a month. Its strengths are nature and English, while the size of the nomad scene is where it scores lower.

Best Neighborhoods in Inverness

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Inverness city centre

$$$
Central & Compact

The castle above the Ness and the streets below it

The middle of Inverness on both banks of the River Ness, with the red sandstone castle on its mound, the Victorian Market, the pedestrian High Street and the pubs along Church Street where the traditional music sessions happen. The Ness Islands, joined by footbridges, are a short walk upstream.

Best for: Anyone who wants everything in the Highlands' only town on foot
Pros
  • Everything central within a fifteen minute walk
  • The Ness Islands walk starts in the middle of town
  • The best pubs and music sessions in the Highlands
Cons
  • The most expensive rents in the Highlands
  • Very busy with coach tourists from May to September
  • Short-term lets have taken a lot of the housing stock

Crown and Hilton

$$$
Residential & Settled

The residential slopes east of the river

The residential districts climbing the hill east of the centre, holding much of Inverness's family housing along with the schools and the hospital at Raigmore. It is quiet, settled and within walking distance of the centre if you accept the gradient on the way home.

Best for: Longer stays and families who want a house and a garden
Pros
  • The best residential stock in Inverness
  • Schools and Raigmore hospital nearby
  • Views west over the town to the hills
Cons
  • Steep enough to matter on the walk back
  • Few shops or restaurants
  • Housing is tight, as everywhere in the Highlands

Merkinch and the canal

$$
Working & Affordable

The working end, where the Caledonian Canal meets the firth

The area northwest of the centre between the river and the Caledonian Canal, where Thomas Telford's canal completes its run from Fort William through the Great Glen and enters the Beauly Firth at the Clachnaharry sea lock. It is the most ordinary and most affordable part of the town.

Best for: Anyone who wants lower rents and the canal towpath
Pros
  • Cheapest housing in Inverness
  • The Caledonian Canal towpath runs from here to Fort William
  • The Merkinch nature reserve on the firth
Cons
  • Areas of genuine deprivation
  • Few services and little to walk to
  • Industrial in parts along the canal

Culloden and Balloch

$$
Suburban & Historic

The eastern suburbs, by the battlefield

The suburban villages east of Inverness, largely built since the 1970s as commuter housing, and immediately beside Culloden Moor, where the 1746 battle ended the Jacobite rising in about an hour and where the visitor centre and the marked field are among the better historic sites in Britain.

Best for: Families wanting newer housing, and anyone drawn to Culloden
Pros
  • Culloden battlefield and its visitor centre on the doorstep
  • Newer housing with gardens and parking
  • Clava Cairns, a Bronze Age burial site, is nearby
Cons
  • Six kilometres from the centre and car dependent
  • Suburban with little character
  • Bus service thins in the evening

Cost of Living in Inverness

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Inverness, so the $1,550 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 1000 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,550 a month. A one-bedroom runs roughly $700 to $1,100, and the market is tight because the Highlands have a genuine housing shortage made worse by short-term lets. Groceries cost a little more than the Scottish average, since everything comes up the A9. Eating out is moderate, with good local seafood, beef and game. Utilities are a serious cost given the winters, and rural Highland properties are often on oil or electric heating rather than gas, which is more expensive again. Council tax applies on top of rent.

Where to Work in Inverness

Connectivity is better than the Highlands' reputation suggests. Fibre reaches most of Inverness at good speeds and the Scottish Government's rural broadband programmes have extended coverage, though it thins quickly once you leave the city. Mobile coverage in Inverness is comprehensive and becomes patchy in the glens. Power is reliable, with occasional weather-related outages. There is a small coworking scene and a growing remote worker presence, since the Highlands have actively marketed themselves to people leaving cities. The time zone is GMT with British summer time. Anyone planning to work from a rural cottage should test the connection before committing, since coverage in the glens varies enormously and satellite is sometimes the only answer.

Getting Around Inverness

Inverness is small and walkable, laid out along the River Ness with the castle above it, and covering the town on foot takes twenty minutes. Buses cover the city and the region, with Stagecoach running the Highland network. A car is close to essential for anything beyond the town, since the glens and the coast are where the interest is. Inverness Airport has flights to London, Amsterdam and several United Kingdom cities. The railway runs south to Edinburgh in three and a half hours and the Kyle line west to Skye is among the great scenic routes.

Visas for Inverness

The United Kingdom requires most non-visa nationals to obtain an Electronic Travel Authorisation before arrival, and visa nationals need a standard visitor visa. Visitors may not work for a United Kingdom employer, though remote work for an overseas employer during a visit is permitted within limits. There is no digital nomad visa. Longer stays run through sponsored work visas, the Global Talent route or family categories. Scotland has argued for a distinct migration route to address rural depopulation, and it does not currently exist. Anyone working remotely for an overseas employer while visiting should read the current visitor rules carefully, since what is permitted is defined narrowly and has changed recently.

Best Time to Visit Inverness

May to September is the season, with July around seventeen degrees, long days and the Highlands at their most accessible. Late May and early June are ideal: the days are long, the hills are green and the midges have not yet reached full strength, which they do from late June through August and which is a genuine consideration rather than a joke. September and October give autumn colour and clear air. Winter from November to March is dark and wet, with the northern lights occasionally visible on clear nights. Anyone walking between June and August should carry midge repellent and consider a head net, which locals use without embarrassment and which visitors usually buy on day two.

Pros and Cons of Inverness

The case for Inverness is that it is the only real town in the Highlands and gives access to some of the best landscape in Europe: Loch Ness at the door, the Cairngorms an hour south, Skye and the west coast two hours away, and the North Coast 500 route starting here. It is safe, English-speaking and small enough to know quickly. The case against is the housing shortage, the weather, the midges, and a town that is genuinely quiet, with a limited job market outside tourism, healthcare and public services.

Who Inverness Is For

Inverness suits hillwalkers, climbers, cyclists and anyone whose priority is being close to the Highlands rather than being in a city. It suits people who already work remotely and want a British base with landscape at the door, and the Highlands actively want incomers. It suits whisky people, since Speyside is an hour east. It does not suit anyone who needs a city's amenities, anyone hoping to find work locally without one already arranged, or anyone who would be badly affected by a wet, dark winter. It also suits anyone drawn to Culloden, four miles east, where the 1746 battle ended the Jacobite rising and where the visitor centre is one of the better ones in Britain.
