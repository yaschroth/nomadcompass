Hiroshima for Digital Nomads: The Honest Take

City of peace with powerful history. For remote workers, Hiroshima leans on safety and fast WiFi, with a typical budget near $2,700 a month. The main trade-off is the size of the nomad scene.

Cost of Living in Hiroshima

Hiroshima is one of Japan's more affordable major cities, noticeably cheaper than Tokyo or Osaka, and the weak yen of the mid-2020s stretches a foreign income further than it did a decade ago. Prices below are approximate 2025-2026 figures, and the USD equivalents assume roughly 150 yen to the dollar, so they shift with the exchange rate.

  • Rent, central 1-bedroom (Naka Ward, near Hondori or Kamiyacho): around 60,000 to 90,000 yen ($400-600) per month.
  • Rent, cheaper area (outer wards like Asaminami or Nishi): around 40,000 to 60,000 yen ($265-400).
  • Utilities (electricity, gas, water, internet): roughly 18,000 to 25,000 yen ($120-165), with summer air conditioning pushing the top end.
  • Groceries for one person: around 40,000 to 55,000 yen ($265-365).
  • Eating out: a bowl of ramen or okonomiyaki runs 800 to 1,200 yen ($5-8), and a mid-range dinner for two lands around 5,000 to 7,000 yen ($33-47).
  • Coworking hot desk: monthly memberships commonly start near 15,000 to 30,000 yen ($100-200), with serviced providers charging more.
  • Local transport: streetcar rides are 190 to 240 yen ($1.30-1.60) a trip, and a realistic monthly transport budget is 5,000 to 8,000 yen ($33-53).

A single remote worker can live comfortably on roughly 180,000 to 280,000 yen ($1,200-1,850) per month, depending on rent and how often you eat out. Short-stay furnished apartments and hotels cost more per night than a long lease, so budget higher if you are staying only a few weeks.

Where to Work

Hiroshima's coworking scene is small but real, clustered around the central Hondori and Kamiyacho districts and near Hiroshima Station. Named options include WeBase Hiroshima (a hostel with work-friendly space), iiOffice Seeds near Hiroshima Station, port.cloud, and international operator Regus for serviced desks and private offices. Availability and day-pass policies change, so confirm current hours and drop-in rates before you rely on a specific space.

Cafe-work culture exists but is more reserved than in Bali or Lisbon. Chains like Doutor, Tully's, and Starbucks are laptop-friendly and have reliable WiFi, while many independent cafes prefer you keep sessions short at busy times. WiFi across Japan is fast and stable, though free public networks can be patchy, so most nomads buy a local SIM or eSIM and tether when needed.

Getting Around

Hiroshima runs Japan's longest and busiest streetcar network, the Hiroden trams, which cover most of the central grid and are the easiest way to move around. Buses fill in the gaps, and the compact city center is genuinely walkable, so you can often reach shops, food, and coworking on foot. Pick up an ICOCA IC card to tap on and off trams, buses, and JR trains without fumbling for cash. Note that the old PASPY card was retired in spring 2025 and replaced by a QR-based system called MOBIRY DAYS, so ICOCA is now the most reliable everyday card.

Hiroshima Airport (HIJ) sits well outside the city, so the practical link is the airport limousine bus, which runs to Hiroshima Station and the central bus center in roughly 50 minutes for about 1,370 yen ($9) each way. Times and fares can change, so check the current timetable when you book.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and most EU countries can enter Japan visa-free for stays of up to 90 days as a temporary visitor, which covers a lot of short remote stints. This status is for tourism and short business, not formal work, so treat it accordingly.

For longer stays, Japan launched a digital nomad visa in 2024 that allows a stay of up to six months. It requires a high annual income (around 10 million yen, roughly $67,000) from sources outside Japan, plus private health insurance covering your time in the country. It is not renewable in place, and you generally must spend six months outside Japan before reapplying. Rules and thresholds change, so confirm the current requirements with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs or a Japanese embassy before you plan around it.

Best Time to Visit

Hiroshima has four distinct seasons and high year-round humidity from its coastal setting. Summers (June to August) are hot and sticky, with August highs around 33 C (91 F) and peak humidity in July, which makes long workdays outdoors or in a poorly cooled apartment unpleasant. Winters are mild and fairly dry, with January lows near 2 C (36 F).

The best windows for a remote-work stay are spring (late March to mid-May), when cherry blossoms arrive and temperatures are comfortable, and autumn (late October to mid-November), when the foliage turns and the air is crisp. Both offer pleasant days for balancing work with getting outside.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Noticeably cheaper than Tokyo or Osaka, with the weak yen helping foreign incomes go further.
  • Pro: Excellent, easy public transport and a walkable, compact center.
  • Pro: Clean, safe, and calm, with strong food (okonomiyaki is a local institution) and easy day trips to Miyajima.
  • Con: The nomad and coworking scene is small, so expect fewer ready-made communities and events.
  • Con: The language barrier is real outside tourist zones, and English is less common than in Tokyo.
  • Con: Fewer direct international flight connections, so getting in and out often means routing through Tokyo, Osaka, or a connection.

Who Hiroshima Is Best For

Hiroshima suits remote workers who want an authentic, lower-cost slice of Japan without the crowds and expense of the big metros. It rewards independent, self-directed people who are comfortable with a smaller expat scene, happy to navigate some Japanese, and drawn to a clean, safe, walkable base with good food and easy access to the Seto Inland Sea. It also works well for a focused month or two of deep work between livelier hubs.

You should probably skip it if you rely on a large, active nomad community for your social life, need constant networking events, or want a wide range of cheap direct flights on your doorstep. In those cases Tokyo, Osaka, or Fukuoka will serve you better, with Hiroshima making more sense as a quieter change of pace than a long-term home base.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hiroshima

How much does it cost to live in Hiroshima as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Hiroshima is around $2,700 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Hiroshima?

With a WiFi score of 9/10, connectivity in Hiroshima is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Hiroshima safe for digital nomads?

Hiroshima scores 10 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Hiroshima a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Hiroshima is worth a look if you value safety and fast WiFi. Costs sit near $2,700 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
