Cost of Living in Rome

Rome is one of the more expensive cities in southern Europe, though it remains cheaper than London or Paris. Rent is the biggest variable. A one-bedroom apartment in central neighborhoods like Monti, Trastevere, or Prati runs EUR 1,200-1,700 per month (roughly USD 1,300-1,850). In outer neighborhoods like Pigneto, Ostiense, or Garbatella you can find the same space for EUR 800-1,100 (USD 870-1,200), still with good metro or tram access.

Groceries at a local supermarket (Carrefour, Conad, Esselunga) cost around EUR 200-280 per month if you cook regularly. Eating out at a neighborhood trattoria runs EUR 12-20 per person for a full lunch; dinner at a sit-down place is closer to EUR 25-40. Street food and pizza al taglio keep costs lower on the days you don't cook.

A coworking hot-desk membership lands between EUR 130-300 per month depending on the space and hours. Local public transport (metro, bus, tram) costs EUR 35-38 per month for an unlimited pass. Utilities for a typical apartment add EUR 100-150, and a local SIM with data runs around EUR 10-15 per month.

A realistic all-in monthly budget: EUR 1,800-2,500 (USD 1,950-2,700) living solo in a reasonable apartment with coworking access and a mix of cooking and eating out.

Where to Work

Rome has a solid coworking scene, particularly in the Ostiense district. Talent Garden Ostiense is the largest hub, with 5,000 square meters, fast fiber internet, event spaces, and a community of 500-plus members including startups and freelancers. Day passes run around EUR 20; monthly flex plans start at EUR 129. Coaster Coworking, near the Colosseum, is a smaller, well-reviewed space with reliable connections and views of the Lateran basilica. Impact Hub Roma, also in the Ostiense area, blends coworking with social innovation events and flexible plans from EUR 120 per month for 10 days.

Cafe culture in Rome is genuine but not built for laptop marathons. Italians take espresso standing at the bar, and most traditional cafes expect a quick turnover. A few places do tolerate laptops: Faro Specialty Coffee on Via Piave is one of the better known spots, open weekday mornings until 3pm, with decent WiFi and a quieter atmosphere. Caffè Letterario in Ostiense formally offers coworking and WiFi alongside its bookshop and event space. For serious calls or long work sessions, lean on coworking spaces rather than cafes.

WiFi in apartments can be fast (fiber is widely available) but getting a local provider set up on a short lease takes effort. Some furnished rentals include it; confirm before you book.

Getting Around

Rome's historic center is walkable if you are comfortable with cobblestones and hills. Many nomads base themselves in areas like Monti, Trastevere, or Prati and walk to most daily errands. Beyond the center, the metro is limited to two main lines (A and B) that intersect at Termini station. Trams and buses cover a wider network but traffic and unreliable schedules make them frustrating. A monthly transport pass costs around EUR 35-38 and covers all public transit.

Cycling is picking up with more bike lanes added in recent years, though you need patience navigating traffic and parked scooters. Ride-hailing (Free Now, Uber Black) works but costs more than standard taxi.

For the airport, Rome has two. Fiumicino (FCO) is the main international airport, roughly 30 kilometers from the city center. The Leonardo Express train runs direct to Termini in 32 minutes and costs EUR 14; regional trains are cheaper at EUR 8-9 but take a few minutes longer. Ciampino (CIA) is smaller and mostly used by budget carriers; shuttle buses to Termini take 35-45 minutes depending on traffic.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

If you hold a US, Canadian, UK, Australian, or New Zealand passport, you can enter Italy visa-free for up to 90 days within any 180-day window under standard Schengen rules. This does not reset by leaving and re-entering; the 90-day counter follows your passport across all Schengen countries.

Italy launched a formal Digital Nomad Visa in 2024, which is active in 2025 and 2026. It allows non-EU remote workers to live in Italy for 12 months, renewable indefinitely. Key requirements include proof of remote income of at least EUR 28,000 per year, a work contract or client agreement with entities outside Italy, health insurance covering at least EUR 30,000, and at least six months of professional experience in your field. You apply through an Italian consulate in your home country before arrival.

Always verify current requirements directly with the Italian consulate or official government sources before you apply, as details change.

Best Time to Visit

The best windows for remote work in Rome are April to mid-June and mid-September to November. Spring brings mild temperatures around 18-22C (64-72F), manageable crowds, and long daylight hours. Autumn offers similar comfort with fewer tourists than spring and a warm, golden feel to the city.

Avoid July and August if you care about productivity. Temperatures regularly hit 35-38C (95-100F), air conditioning in older buildings is inconsistent, many local cafes and restaurants close for August, and tourist crowds peak. It is genuinely uncomfortable and the city feels half-empty of Romans.

Winter (December to February) is mild by northern European standards, around 8-12C (46-54F), but some days are rainy and grey. The city is quieter and cheaper, which some remote workers prefer. Note that 2025 is a Jubilee year for the Catholic Church, meaning Rome sees significantly elevated visitor numbers throughout the year, not just in summer.

Pros and Cons

  • Extraordinary food culture, from quick pizza al taglio to sit-down Roman classics
  • Rich history and beauty woven into everyday life, not just tourist zones
  • Good flight connections across Europe and long-haul from Fiumicino
  • Growing coworking scene, especially in the Ostiense district
  • Warm, sociable atmosphere and a large expat and nomad community
  • Visa pathway exists with the Digital Nomad Visa for stays beyond 90 days
  • High summer heat makes outdoor time and productivity genuinely hard
  • Bureaucracy is slow: banking, SIM cards, and housing contracts can test your patience
  • Cafe culture does not default to laptop-friendly, unlike northern European cities
  • Traffic and noise are real in central areas, and scooter chaos is constant
  • Cost is higher than other southern European nomad destinations like Lisbon or Tbilisi
  • Tourist crowds are intense in spring and autumn in the historic center

Who Rome Is Best For

Rome works best for nomads who genuinely want to live in a city rather than pass through it. If you value food, culture, history, and a place where everyday life has texture and depth, Rome rewards slow stays. It suits people on the Italy Digital Nomad Visa, those already holding EU residency, or those with passports that make multi-entry Schengen stays feasible. Remote workers in design, writing, media, or social innovation will find community through Talent Garden and Impact Hub.

Rome is a harder fit if you are strictly budget-focused, need constant reliable cafe WiFi, or want the streamlined expat infrastructure of somewhere like Lisbon or Barcelona. It is also demanding in high summer. If you run on tight deadlines and need zero friction in your workday setup, a smaller Italian city like Bologna or Catania may serve you better as a first Italy base.

Frequently Asked Questions About Rome

How much does it cost to live in Rome as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $3,000 a month in Rome for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Rome?

Rome rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Rome safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Rome is 6/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Rome a good city for digital nomads overall?

Rome stands out for food and culture among remote workers. With a typical budget near $3,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
