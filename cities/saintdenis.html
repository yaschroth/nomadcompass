Setting Up as a Nomad in Saint-Denis

A French department in the Indian Ocean built around one of the world's most active volcanoes. For remote workers, Saint-Denis leans on nature and climate, with a typical budget near $1,800 a month. The main trade-off is the language barrier.

Best Neighborhoods in Saint-Denis

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Saint-Denis centre

$$$
Creole & Urban

Creole mansions along the Rue de Paris

The centre of the prefecture, with the Rue de Paris and its nineteenth century Creole mansions running up from the Barachois seafront to the botanical garden, and the prefecture building, the covered market and the shops of the Rue Marechal Leclerc all within a few blocks.

Best for: Anyone who wants a walkable French city with everything on foot
Pros
  • The finest Creole architecture on the island
  • Genuinely walkable, with shops, market and administration central
  • The Barachois seafront for the evening
Cons
  • Parking is difficult and traffic heavy at peak times
  • No swimming beach in the city itself
  • Rents high for the island

Sainte-Clotilde

$$
Student & Practical

The university and hospital quarter east of the centre

The large eastern district of Saint-Denis holding the university campus, the main hospital, the retail parks and the road out to Roland Garros airport. It is where a good share of the city's population lives and it is cheaper than the historic centre.

Best for: Longer stays that want value, the campus or the hospital
Pros
  • Cheaper than the centre for more space
  • University campus, hospital and retail parks all here
  • Close to the airport
Cons
  • Aircraft noise on the approach paths
  • Suburban and short on character
  • Traffic into the centre is heavy every morning

La Montagne

$$$
Elevated & Residential

The green terrace on the slope above the city

A residential district on the mountainside directly above Saint-Denis, several hundred metres up, holding houses with views over the city and the sea and a noticeably cooler climate. The road up is a series of switchbacks and the air changes as you climb.

Best for: Anyone who wants cooler air and a view over the city
Pros
  • Several degrees cooler than the coast
  • Views over the whole city and the Indian Ocean
  • Trails into the interior start close by
Cons
  • The switchback road down is slow in traffic
  • Nothing walkable in the way of shops
  • Cloud settles on the slope in the afternoon

Saint-Paul

$$$
Historic & West Coast

The old capital on the west coast

The island's first settlement and its capital until 1738, about twenty five kilometres west along the coast, with a large seafront market on Fridays and Saturdays and the marine cemetery where the pirate Olivier Levasseur is said to be buried.

Best for: Anyone who wants the sunnier west coast and its beaches
Pros
  • Drier and sunnier than the north coast
  • One of the best markets on the island
  • Close to the lagoon beaches at Boucan Canot and Saint-Gilles
Cons
  • Twenty five kilometres from the prefecture and its offices
  • The coast road congests badly at peak hours
  • Hotter than Saint-Denis in summer

Cost of Living in Saint-Denis

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Saint-Denis, so the $1,800 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 951 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,800 a month as a solo remote worker in Saint-Denis, which is expensive for the Indian Ocean and reflects a French department where much of the food arrives from Europe. A one-bedroom flat runs roughly $700 to $1,100. Groceries cost noticeably more than mainland France and the price of living here has driven serious protest movements, including the gilets jaunes actions that were more sustained on Reunion than almost anywhere. The euro is the currency. This is our own estimate rather than measured data. Anyone comparing with Mauritius nearby should note that Reunion costs considerably more and offers European healthcare and schooling in exchange.

Where to Work in Saint-Denis

Reunion has European infrastructure: fibre reaches most of the coastal ring at good speeds, mobile coverage is comprehensive and French plans roam here without charge, which matters for anyone based in Europe. Power is reliable. There is a small coworking scene in Saint-Denis and Saint-Pierre. French is the language of everything, with Reunion Creole spoken among locals, and English is limited. The island sits three hours ahead of Paris in the northern winter, which makes a French morning into a Reunionese afternoon. Anyone working with mainland France should note that the offset flips with European daylight saving, which changes the overlap by an hour twice a year.

Getting Around Saint-Denis

Saint-Denis sits on the north coast and its centre, with the Creole townhouses along the Rue de Paris and the Barachois seafront, is walkable. A coastal road rings the island and the new coastal highway west of the capital is one of the most expensive roads ever built in France, running partly on a viaduct over the sea because of rockfalls. Buses cover the ring. The interior, with its three cirques and the volcano, is reached by mountain roads and on foot. Roland Garros airport has direct flights to Paris.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Saint-Denis

Reunion is an overseas department of France and part of the European Union while sitting outside the Schengen area, which is the detail that matters: a Schengen visa does not admit you and time here does not count against a Schengen allowance. European Union citizens have full freedom of movement and can live and work here with no permit. Most other Western nationalities receive ninety days visa-free. Longer stays for non-EU citizens require a French long-stay visa applied for before travel. Anyone travelling on to Mauritius, forty minutes away by air, should note that is a separate country with its own entry requirements.

Best Time to Visit Saint-Denis

May to November is the dry, cooler season and the practical window, with daytime temperatures around twenty-five on the coast and the hiking trails in the cirques at their best. December to April is the wet season and the cyclone season, and Reunion sits directly in the Indian Ocean cyclone belt; the island holds several world records for rainfall in a single day. Piton de la Fournaise, one of the most active volcanoes on earth, erupts most years, and eruptions are announced and viewable from designated points. Mafate, the cirque reachable only on foot or by helicopter, is walkable year-round but the trails are genuinely hard after heavy rain.

Pros and Cons of Saint-Denis

Reunion is one of the best hiking destinations anywhere: three collapsed volcanic cirques with villages reachable only on foot, over a thousand kilometres of marked trail, and an active volcano that erupts most years. It has European infrastructure, French healthcare and no visa question for Europeans. Against that, it is expensive, French is essential, the cost of living is politically contentious, and shark attacks have led to a swimming ban on much of the west coast that has been in place for over a decade. The island's Creole culture, mixing African, Indian, Chinese and European elements, is also more visible in daily life here than in most French departments.

Who Saint-Denis Is For

Saint-Denis suits a French speaker, ideally a European Union citizen, whose main interest is hiking or volcanology, since Reunion is genuinely world class at both. Anyone who runs will know it as the home of the Grand Raid, one of the hardest ultramarathons anywhere. It is a poor fit for anyone without French and anyone expecting a beach holiday, since the swimming restrictions on the west coast are real and the island's case is the interior rather than the shore. It also suits anyone drawn to the Indian Ocean's volcanic geology, since Piton de la Fournaise is among the most studied and most accessible active volcanoes anywhere.
