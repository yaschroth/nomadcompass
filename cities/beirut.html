Cost of Living in Beirut

Beirut operates almost entirely on cash, and most transactions happen in US dollars rather than the Lebanese pound (LBP). Budget carefully, because ATM access and card payments remain unreliable due to the ongoing banking crisis.

A typical monthly breakdown for a solo remote worker:

  • Rent, central neighborhoods (Achrafieh, Hamra, Mar Mikhael): $600-900/month for a furnished one-bedroom
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Bourj Hammoud, Dora, outer suburbs): $350-550/month
  • Groceries: $150-220/month from local markets and supermarkets
  • Eating out: $5-10 for a casual local meal, $20-40 at a mid-range restaurant
  • Coworking hot-desk: $100-175/month at established spaces
  • Generator subscription: $50-100/month (a near-mandatory extra because state electricity is limited to a few hours daily)
  • Local transport (Uber/shared taxi): $40-80/month depending on how often you move around
  • Total monthly range: roughly $1,200-1,800 for a comfortable setup in a central area

One important caveat: generator fees and cash logistics add friction that other cities do not have. Carry USD in small denominations at all times.

Where to Work

Beirut has a genuine coworking ecosystem that emerged partly because home electricity is so unreliable. Dedicated coworking spaces are your most dependable option for consistent power and fast internet.

  • Antwork operates in Hamra, Beirut Souks, and Saifi Village. It is the most established network, with multiple locations, fast fiber, an outdoor garden, and an active community of freelancers and startup founders.
  • The Olive Grove in Hamra is a smaller, creative-focused space in a traditional Beiruti house near the American University of Beirut campus.
  • Beirut Digital District in Bachoura is the city's tech hub with 80+ Mbps fiber and 24/7 access, better suited to longer stays.
  • Fabrika in Achrafieh serves designers, journalists, and researchers with specialist facilities including a photo studio and library.

Cafe work is popular in Hamra and Achrafieh. Barzakh in Hamra and Caffeine Coffee in Achrafieh-Sassine both have reliable WiFi and a work-friendly atmosphere. However, cafe power access is inconsistent, so carry a portable battery if you rely on laptop-only setups.

The real WiFi situation is honest: 4G mobile data at 30+ Mbps is often more reliable than fixed connections at smaller cafes. Many nomads use a local SIM as a backup. Fiber connections at dedicated coworking spaces are the exception, not the rule, in residential buildings.

Getting Around

Beirut has no metro or formal bus system worth relying on. You get around by a combination of shared minibuses (service taxis), Uber, Careem, and walking.

  • Shared service taxis (sherut): The cheapest option, running fixed routes for roughly $0.50-1 per ride, but routes can be confusing for newcomers.
  • Uber and Careem: Both operate in Beirut, with typical city rides costing $3-8. More transparent than street taxis.
  • Walking: Central neighborhoods like Hamra, Achrafieh, Gemmayzeh, and Mar Mikhael are walkable between each other, though cracked pavements and parked cars on sidewalks make it slow.
  • Airport transfers: Rafic Hariri International Airport is about 9 km south of the city center. Official airport taxis charge a fixed $25-30. Uber also operates from the airport. There is no train or cheap shuttle; the cheapest option is a shared minibus for under $2, though it requires a connection and is not practical with luggage.

Renting a car is possible but central Beirut parking is chaotic. Most remote workers stay in walkable central neighborhoods and rely on apps for longer trips.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of more than 80 countries, including most EU nations, the UK, the US, Canada, and Australia, receive a free visa on arrival at Beirut Rafic Hariri International Airport valid for one month. You can extend this at the General Security directorate for up to two additional months, giving a maximum of three months on a single visit.

Gulf Cooperation Council citizens (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Oman) and Jordanian nationals can stay up to three months on arrival without a fee.

Lebanon does not currently offer a dedicated digital nomad visa. Longer-term stays require a residency permit, which involves a Lebanese sponsor and bureaucratic complexity. In practice, most nomads cycle in and out, using the one-to-three-month visitor allowance.

Important restriction: If your passport contains Israeli stamps, visas, or entry/exit seals, you will be refused entry. Confirm entry requirements with your nearest Lebanese embassy before you travel.

Always verify current visa conditions with official Lebanese General Security sources before booking.

Best Time to Visit

Beirut has a Mediterranean climate: hot, humid summers and mild, rainy winters.

  • Spring (April to June): The best window for remote work. Temperatures range from 20-28°C (68-82°F), rainfall drops off sharply by May, and the city is lively without peak-summer heat and humidity. This is widely considered the optimal visiting period.
  • Autumn (September to October): A close second. Temperatures cool from summer peaks, humidity drops, and the city returns to full pace after August holidays.
  • Summer (July to August): Hot and very humid, with temperatures around 30-33°C (86-91°F). Power cuts are more disruptive when everyone is running air conditioning. Peak season means higher prices and crowded beach clubs.
  • Winter (December to February): Mild at 12-17°C (54-63°F) but with significant rainfall and grey skies for stretches. Fewer tourists, lower accommodation prices.

For a balance of good weather, city energy, and productive working conditions, May through mid-June and September through October are the sweet spots.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • One of the Arab world's most cosmopolitan and creatively energetic cities
  • Excellent food scene at every price point, from street food to internationally recognized restaurants
  • Strong English proficiency across most urban neighborhoods
  • Low cost of living compared to Western cities, with good value in rent and restaurants
  • Vibrant nightlife and cultural life that punches above the city's size
  • Warm, genuinely hospitable local culture toward visitors
  • Real coworking infrastructure built specifically to work around the electricity problem

Cons

  • Serious and ongoing security concerns: the US State Department maintains a Level 4 "Do Not Travel" advisory as of 2026
  • Daily electricity cuts require a generator subscription, adding cost and planning overhead
  • Banking and ATM access is severely limited; you must manage large amounts of physical cash
  • Political and security situation can shift quickly and unpredictably
  • No public transport system worth relying on; getting around requires apps or private taxis
  • Air pollution and waste management infrastructure are chronically strained
  • Israeli entry stamps are an immediate barrier for anyone who has visited Israel

Who Beirut Is Best For

Beirut suits experienced travelers who have already dealt with infrastructure-challenged destinations and want a culturally rich, affordable base in the Middle East. If you are drawn to creative cities, good food, Arabic-speaking markets, and a lively Mediterranean social scene, Beirut can be genuinely rewarding as a short-to-medium stay of one to three months.

It works best for people with location flexibility who can monitor the security situation and leave quickly if it deteriorates. Writers, designers, journalists, and anyone working in the Arab world or regional media will find a genuine professional community here.

Skip Beirut if you need complete predictability in your infrastructure, if daily power-cut management sounds exhausting, or if the current security advisories from your government give you pause. The city rewards resilience and adaptability, and it asks a lot of both.

Frequently Asked Questions About Beirut

How much does it cost to live in Beirut as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,600 a month in Beirut for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Beirut?

Beirut rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Beirut safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Beirut is 3/10, patchy for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Beirut a good city for digital nomads overall?

Beirut stands out for nightlife and food among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,600 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
