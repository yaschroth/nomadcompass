Setting Up as a Nomad in Siwa

A Berber oasis 50km from Libya, with salt lakes, mudbrick ruins and no hurry. Siwa pairs low costs with nature and a desert climate, all for about $550 a month. If anything, nightlife lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Siwa

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Siwa Town and Shali

$
Central & Ancient

Around the dissolving mudbrick fortress in the centre

The centre of the oasis, built around Shali, the mudbrick citadel that a rare three-day rain largely dissolved in 1926 and which still stands over the town as an eroded mass. The market, the few restaurants, the bike rentals and the guides are all in the square below it.

Best for: Anyone who wants the market, the guides and the fortress within a short walk
Pros
  • Shali, the market and the town square all together
  • Bicycle rental for the whole oasis
  • The only connectivity and services in Siwa
Cons
  • Very limited accommodation and restaurants
  • Donkey carts and traffic through the square
  • Conservative, so dress and behaviour matter

Aghurmi and the Oracle

$$
Historic & Palm-shaded

The old village on the rock where Alexander came

A settlement a few kilometres east built on and around the rock holding the Temple of the Oracle, where Alexander the Great came in 331 BC to be confirmed as a son of Amun. Palm gardens, springs and the remains of the older village surround it.

Best for: Stays that want the oracle temple and the palm gardens on the doorstep
Pros
  • The Temple of the Oracle and Cleopatra's spring nearby
  • Dense palm gardens and irrigation channels
  • Quiet and green by oasis standards
Cons
  • A bicycle or vehicle is needed for the town
  • Very few places to stay
  • No shops or services

Fatnas and the Salt Lakes

$$
Lakeside & Scenic

The island in the salt lake, west of town

A palm-covered spit reaching into Birket Siwa, the salt lake west of the town, known for the sunset and for the water dense enough to float in. The salt pans nearby are brilliant white and blue, and there are a handful of ecolodges along the shore.

Best for: Stays that want the salt lakes and the sunsets rather than the town
Pros
  • The salt lakes, the pans and the floating pools
  • The best sunsets in the oasis
  • Ecolodges built in the local salt and mudbrick technique
Cons
  • Ten to fifteen minutes from town by bicycle
  • No shops or services at all
  • Mosquitoes near the water at dusk

Dakrour and the Southern Gardens

$
Garden & Traditional

Below the mountain, where the sand baths are

The area south of the town around Gebel Dakrour, a hill known for a traditional sand-burial treatment for joint pain that people still travel for in summer, surrounded by olive and date gardens. Quiet, rural and a short ride from the centre.

Best for: Quiet stays among the date and olive gardens
Pros
  • Olive and date gardens and the springs among them
  • Very quiet and cheap
  • A short bicycle ride from town
Cons
  • Nothing here but gardens
  • No services or connectivity
  • Very hot in summer

Great Sand Sea Edge

$$
Desert & Absolute

Where the palms stop and the dunes begin

The southwestern edge of the oasis where the cultivation ends abruptly and the Great Sand Sea runs unbroken for hundreds of kilometres. A few desert camps operate out here. Travel beyond the edge requires military permission arranged through a licensed operator.

Best for: Short stays in a desert camp rather than a base of any kind
Pros
  • The dunes and the night sky, both exceptional
  • Desert camps and dune trips from the edge
  • Complete silence
Cons
  • Permits and a licensed operator are required to go beyond
  • No fixed accommodation or services
  • Extreme temperatures in summer

Cost of Living in Siwa

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Siwa, so the $550 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 831 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget roughly $550 a month as a solo remote worker in Siwa, which makes it about as cheap as anywhere habitable. A room or small house in the town runs about $150 to $280, and the eco-lodges built from local kershef mud-brick are more but often negotiable for long stays. Groceries land around $100 to $150; Siwa grows its own dates and olives in enormous quantity and both are excellent and nearly free, while anything trucked in from the coast costs more. A local meal is $2 to $4, a lodge dinner $8 to $14. There is very little to spend money on, which is either the appeal or the difficulty depending on temperament.

Where to Work in Siwa

Siwa has no coworking space and no expectation of one. Work happens in your accommodation. Connectivity is the honest obstacle: this is an oasis 550 kilometres from Cairo and 300 from the Mediterranean coast, and while mobile 4G reaches the town and fixed connections exist in the better lodges, speeds are modest and reliability is not guaranteed. Anyone whose work depends on video calls should treat Siwa as a place for asynchronous or offline-heavy work rather than a normal base, and should test any specific accommodation before committing. Power interruptions occur. A local SIM with a large data allowance is the standard arrangement and generally the most reliable option.

Getting Around Siwa

Siwa town is small and the standard local transport is a bicycle or a donkey cart, both of which are genuinely the normal way people move around the oasis. Bicycles can be rented for a couple of dollars a day and cover the town, the springs and the nearer ruins comfortably. For the salt lakes, the Great Sand Sea and Cleopatra's spring, a bicycle or a hired 4x4 works depending on distance. Getting to Siwa is the commitment: buses run from Alexandria in about eight hours and from Cairo in ten to twelve, and there is no airport or railway. Marsa Matruh on the coast is the nearest town of any size, about four hours north.

Visas for Siwa

Egypt grants most Western passports a 30-day visa on arrival at the airport or through an e-Visa obtained online in advance, and this can be renewed in-country at a passport office. There is no digital nomad visa. Long stays are handled through repeated renewals, which is well established but bureaucratic and generally done in Cairo or Alexandria rather than in Siwa. One important local consideration: Siwa sits close to the Libyan border in a military-sensitive area, and travel into the desert beyond the oasis requires permits arranged through local operators. Foreigners should register as required and follow local guidance, and should check their own government's travel advice for the western desert region before going.

Best Time to Visit Siwa

October to April is the season in Siwa. Winter days are warm and clear, around 20 to 25 degrees, and the nights are genuinely cold in the desert, dropping close to freezing in December and January, so warm clothing is needed despite the setting. This is when desert trips, the salt lakes and the sand sea are at their best. Summer, May to September, is extreme: temperatures regularly above 40 degrees and often much higher, which makes outdoor activity impractical between mid-morning and evening. The date harvest in October is the oasis's main annual event and a good time to visit. The Siyaha festival, a local reconciliation gathering, falls around the October full moon.

Pros and Cons of Siwa

Siwa is one of the most striking places in Egypt: an oasis of 300,000 date palms in the western desert, with a ruined mud-brick fortress town, salt lakes you float in, cold and hot springs, and the Great Sand Sea beginning at the edge of the cultivation. It has a distinct Amazigh culture and language quite separate from the rest of Egypt, and costs are extraordinarily low. Against that, it is genuinely remote: ten hours from Cairo, no airport, limited healthcare, and internet that will not support a call-heavy job. It is conservative and travellers should dress and behave accordingly. Its proximity to the Libyan border means checking travel advice is a necessity, not a formality.

Who Siwa Is For

Siwa suits remote workers who want somewhere genuinely unlike anywhere else, whose work is asynchronous and light on bandwidth, and who are content with very little in the way of amenities or company. It is a strong choice for writers, researchers and photographers, for anyone interested in Amazigh culture or desert landscape, and for a deliberate period of low-cost focus rather than a permanent base. It is a poor fit for almost everyone else: anyone needing reliable internet, healthcare, a social scene, easy travel, or a liberal environment will find it difficult. Dahab in Sinai and Alexandria on the coast are the practical Egyptian alternatives with far more infrastructure.

Frequently Asked Questions About Siwa

How much does it cost to live in Siwa as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Siwa is around $550 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Siwa?

Siwa rates 4 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is on the limited side for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Siwa safe for digital nomads?

Siwa scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Siwa a good city for digital nomads overall?

Siwa stands out for affordability and nature and the outdoors among remote workers. With a typical budget near $550 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
