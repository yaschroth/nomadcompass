Nusa Penida for Digital Nomads: The Honest Take

Bali's wild island, dramatic cliffs, manta rays, and Instagram famous spots. Nusa Penida pairs nature with low costs and a tropical climate, all for about $1,500 a month. If anything, nightlife lags.

Cost of Living in Nusa Penida

Nusa Penida is one of the most affordable island destinations in Southeast Asia for remote workers. Monthly costs come in well below Bali's main hubs, though you are trading convenience for that price difference.

  • Rent (central Ped area): IDR 7,500,000-12,000,000 ($460-740) per month for a basic studio or one-bedroom bungalow. Villas with sea views and a kitchen run IDR 14,000,000-20,000,000 ($860-1,230).
  • Rent (cheaper outlying areas like Suana or Sakti): IDR 3,500,000-6,000,000 ($215-370) per month for a homestay or simple private room with bathroom.
  • Groceries: IDR 500,000-900,000 ($30-55) per month if you shop at local markets and warung stalls. The selection of imported goods is limited, so expect to cook simply.
  • Eating out: A warung meal costs IDR 20,000-40,000 ($1.25-2.50). Mid-range restaurants average IDR 80,000-150,000 ($5-9) per meal. Budget IDR 1,500,000-2,500,000 ($90-155) per month eating out most meals.
  • Coworking or cafe work: No formal coworking membership fees exist; you spend IDR 40,000-80,000 ($2.50-5) per session at work-friendly cafes.
  • Local transport: Scooter rental runs IDR 1,500,000-2,500,000 ($90-155) per month, which is essentially mandatory.
  • Total monthly range: A frugal setup runs IDR 8,000,000-11,000,000 ($490-675). A comfortable mid-range budget lands at IDR 14,000,000-22,000,000 ($860-1,350).

Where to Work

There are no formal coworking spaces with membership plans on Nusa Penida as of 2025-2026. Remote work happens in a handful of cafe-style spots, and the culture is laid-back rather than productivity-focused.

Penida Colada

Located in Ped near the harbour, this beachside restaurant is the most popular work spot on the island. WiFi scores consistently high (reviewers rate it 9/10), the food is solid, and unlimited water refills make long sessions practical. Seating is not ergonomic, so bring your own setup awareness. Gets lively in the evenings.

Secret Penida

About one kilometre outside Ped, this restaurant has a distinct aesthetic with swings, greenery, and pink walls. WiFi is rated 9/10, food quality is high, and pricing is very reasonable. No air conditioning but ceiling fans keep it tolerable. Comfortable seating with backrests makes this one of the better extended work options.

Sunny Cafe Penida

Located in Ped Village, this is the closest thing to a dedicated coworking setup on the island, with dedicated work desks and ergonomic seating. It occasionally hosts workshops for freelancers. Go early before it fills with tourists.

The real WiFi situation

WiFi on the island has improved significantly over the past few years. Many cafes and restaurants now have workable connections, and Starlink has reached some guesthouses. That said, speeds vary and outages happen. A local SIM card (Telkomsel or XL Axiata) with a solid 4G data plan is a necessary backup. Telkomsel has the best coverage on the island. For calls that require rock-solid connection, plan around potential drops.

Getting Around

You need a scooter to live on Nusa Penida. The island is larger than most visitors expect, with major areas spread across rough terrain. Walking between neighbourhoods is not realistic, and ride-hailing apps like Gojek do not operate reliably here.

Renting a scooter costs IDR 70,000-100,000 ($4.50-6) per day, with discounts for weekly or monthly rentals. The roads range from recently paved to heavily potholed, with blind corners, steep descents, and occasional sand patches that catch riders off guard. If you are not confident on a motorbike, hire a driver instead. Driver day rates run IDR 400,000-600,000 ($25-37).

To reach Nusa Penida from Bali, take a fast boat from Sanur Harbour. The crossing takes around 40-45 minutes and costs IDR 150,000-250,000 ($9-15) each way depending on the operator. Boats run throughout the day, with most departures in the morning. From Ngurah Rai International Airport in Bali, it is roughly 30-60 minutes by taxi or ride-share to Sanur, depending on traffic. Budget IDR 100,000-200,000 ($6-12) for that leg. You can also bring your scooter over via the Roro car ferry from Padang Bai for around IDR 120,000 ($7.50) per scooter with two passengers; the crossing takes about 90 minutes.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of most Western countries, including the US, UK, EU nations, Australia, and Canada, receive a Visa on Arrival (VOA) valid for 30 days upon entry to Indonesia. This is extendable once for an additional 30 days, giving you up to 60 days total. The VOA costs IDR 500,000 ($30) and can be paid on arrival or processed online in advance.

For longer stays without the VOA, Indonesia's Tourist Single-Entry Visitor Visa (C1) is available through Indonesian embassies and gives you 60 days initially, extendable twice for a maximum of 180 days in-country.

Indonesia launched a Remote Worker Visa (E33G) for digital nomads, which allows stays of up to five years and offers income tax exemptions on earnings from foreign employers. In practice, uptake has been limited due to documentation requirements and the need to apply before arrival. The more popular path among long-term nomads is the B211A Social-Cultural visa combined with extensions managed by a local visa agent.

Always verify current visa rules with the official Indonesian immigration authority or a licensed visa agent before your trip, as policies change.

Best Time to Visit

The dry season runs from April through October, with the peak of comfort landing in May, June, and September. Temperatures sit at 29-33 degrees Celsius year-round with high humidity, but dry-season days feel significantly more pleasant with lower rainfall and cleaner skies. Visibility underwater for snorkelling and diving is best during this window.

July and August are peak tourist months. Viewpoints and popular beaches get crowded between 10am and 3pm as day-trippers arrive from Bali. If you are working remotely and can arrange your schedule flexibly, this is manageable since mornings and late afternoons stay quiet.

The wet season runs November through March. Rain typically falls in heavy, short bursts rather than all-day downpours, so mornings can still be productive. January and February see the most rainfall. Prices drop, crowds thin, and the landscape turns lush green. The tradeoff is rougher boat crossings, occasional WiFi outages during storms, and reduced beach and dive conditions. For remote work focused on productivity, the dry season shoulder months of May-June and September-October give you the best combination of weather, manageable crowds, and stable conditions.

Pros and Cons

  • Very low cost of living compared to Bali's main areas
  • Stunning natural scenery with dramatic cliffs, crystal-clear water, and world-class snorkelling and diving
  • Slower, quieter pace than Canggu or Seminyak
  • Small but growing community of long-term visitors and remote workers
  • Fresh, affordable local food at warungs throughout the island
  • No traffic jams and a genuinely off-the-beaten-track feel
  • No formal coworking spaces with reliable infrastructure or dedicated desks
  • WiFi can be inconsistent and power outages occur during storms
  • You must ride a scooter to get anywhere; roads can be hazardous
  • Limited healthcare on the island; serious medical issues require evacuation to Bali
  • Small nomad community compared to Bali; networking opportunities are limited
  • Limited grocery options for imported goods, specialty foods, or health products
  • Boat-dependent access to Bali means weather delays can disrupt travel plans

Who Nusa Penida Is Best For

Nusa Penida suits remote workers who prioritise nature and low costs over infrastructure. If your work runs on async communication, your schedule is flexible, and you do not need to be on video calls for six hours a day with ironclad connectivity, this island rewards you with stunning surroundings and a pace of life that is hard to find in more developed nomad hubs.

Divers and snorkellers will find it particularly compelling since Nusa Penida sits next to some of the best dive sites in all of Indonesia, including manta ray cleaning stations and mola-mola (ocean sunfish) sightings from July to October.

Nusa Penida is a harder fit if you rely on fast, uninterrupted internet for live calls, client video meetings, or latency-sensitive work. It also is not a great choice if you thrive on a large expat community, coworking events, or easy access to urban amenities. Nomads who need a visa run or a change of scene from Bali can do a productive stint of two to four weeks here, though settling in for months requires genuine patience with the limitations of island infrastructure.

Frequently Asked Questions About Nusa Penida

How much does it cost to live in Nusa Penida as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Nusa Penida is around $1,500 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Nusa Penida?

With a WiFi score of 3/10, connectivity in Nusa Penida is patchy for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Nusa Penida safe for digital nomads?

Nusa Penida scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Nusa Penida a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Nusa Penida is worth a look if you value nature and low costs. Costs sit near $1,500 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
