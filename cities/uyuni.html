Setting Up as a Nomad in Uyuni

The gateway to the Salar de Uyuni, the world's largest salt flat, a blinding white expanse that mirrors the sky after rain. For remote workers, Uyuni leans on nature and low costs, with a typical budget near $700 a month. The main trade-off is the size of the nomad scene.

Uyuni Town Center

Busy, practical, traveller-focused frontier town

The walkable hub around Avenida Ferroviaria

The compact heart of town, clustered around Avenida Ferroviaria and the clock tower, where nearly every hostel, tour agency, restaurant, and the bus terminal sit within a few blocks. It is dusty and functional but convenient, and it is where you book tours and refuel between jeep departures.

Best for: First-time visitors who want everything within walking distance
Pros
  • Everything walkable, from tours to food to buses
  • Easy to compare and book tour operators
  • Cheapest concentration of hostels and cafes
Cons
  • Dusty, cold, and short on charm
  • Quiet and empty once tours depart at dawn

Train Cemetery Edge

$
Eerie, industrial, open and windswept

Rusting locomotives on the windswept fringe

The southwestern edge of town, a short walk or ride from the center, where dozens of rusting antique locomotives sit abandoned on the salt-plain fringe. There is little accommodation out here, but it is an iconic, hauntingly photogenic first or last stop on most salar tours.

Best for: Photographers and travellers chasing the classic Uyuni shot
Pros
  • One of Uyuni's most photographed sights
  • Free and open to wander
  • Usually the opening stop on jeep tours
Cons
  • No real accommodation or services nearby
  • Exposed, cold, and very windy

Colchani

$
Rustic salt-country outpost with craft stalls

The salt-processing village at the salar's edge

A small village about 20 kilometres north of Uyuni, right where the road meets the salt flat. It is the traditional salt-harvesting and processing community, with artisan salt stalls, souvenir stands, and the first salt hotels. Most tours pause here before rolling out onto the white.

Best for: Travellers wanting the salt-village and salar gateway experience
Pros
  • Right at the entrance to the salar
  • Salt crafts, souvenirs, and salt-block buildings
  • Closer to the flat than town for sunrise
Cons
  • Very few services beyond tourism
  • Basic, cold, and limited lodging

Tahua (salt flat village, 95km across the salar)

A remote village on the northern shore of the Salar de Uyuni, at the foot of the Tunupa volcano, roughly a couple of hours from town. It offers salt hotels and simple stays for travellers who want to sleep beside the flat, well away from the frontier bustle of Uyuni.

Best for: Those wanting to overnight on the salar away from town

Cost of Living in Uyuni

Uyuni is genuinely cheap, though most travellers only stay a few nights, so think of it as a short-stay budget rather than a monthly base. A realistic solo monthly range runs roughly $600 to $1,100 depending on how much you rely on tours and heated rooms. Basic guesthouses and hostels are inexpensive, meals in local comedores cost only a few dollars, and the town itself has little to spend money on. The biggest line item is the multi-day 4x4 salt-flat and lagoon tour, which is a one-off splurge rather than a recurring cost. Heating, hot water, and warmer private rooms carry a premium here because of the harsh altiplano climate.

  • Hostel dorm or basic private room: roughly $8 to $30 per night
  • Local set lunch (almuerzo): around $2 to $5
  • Multi-day Salar de Uyuni 4x4 tour: roughly $130 to $250 all-in
  • Entel or Tigo SIM with data: a few dollars to top up

Where to Work in Uyuni

Be honest with yourself before planning to work here: Uyuni is a windswept frontier tourist town, not a digital-nomad base, and connectivity reflects that. Wifi in hostels and cafes is basic, often slow, and can drop out entirely, especially during tour season or bad weather. There is no true coworking space. If you need to stay online, buy a local Entel or Tigo SIM at the terminal or a phone shop and use mobile data as your primary connection, keeping expectations low for video calls or large uploads. A handful of laptop-friendly cafes and pizzerias on and around Avenida Ferroviaria will let you nurse a coffee while you work. Download anything important before you arrive, and treat Uyuni as a place to disconnect between deadlines rather than power through them.

  • Buy an Entel or Tigo SIM and rely on mobile data over cafe wifi
  • Expect slow speeds and intermittent drops, especially in peak season
  • Work offline where possible and sync when the connection is stable
  • Cafes and pizzerias near Avenida Ferroviaria are the most laptop-tolerant spots

Getting Around Uyuni

The town itself is small, flat, and easily walkable, with most hostels, tour agencies, and restaurants clustered within a few blocks of Avenida Ferroviaria and the clock tower. For the salt flat and the coloured altiplano lagoons you go by 4x4 tour jeep, which is the whole point of coming here. Taxis and minibuses cover short local hops, while long-distance buses connect Uyuni to Oruro, La Paz, Potosi, and the Argentine border at Villazon. The historic train line also runs to Oruro and Villazon, a slower but memorable ride. The small Joya Andina airport (UYU) has flights to La Paz. At 3,650m the altitude is real, so take your first day slowly.

  • The town center is walkable in ten to fifteen minutes end to end
  • 4x4 jeep tours are the only practical way to reach the salar and lagoons
  • Buses and trains link Oruro, La Paz, Potosi, and Villazon
  • Flights from Joya Andina airport (UYU) reach La Paz
  • Acclimatize, hydrate, and rest on arrival to manage the altitude

Visas for Uyuni

Uyuni follows Bolivia's national entry rules, so there is nothing town-specific to arrange. Many nationalities, including most Europeans, can enter as tourists without a visa in advance and are commonly granted stays of up to 90 days per calendar year, sometimes issued in shorter increments that you extend at a migration office. Some nationalities, notably United States citizens, face a reciprocity visa and fee with additional document requirements. Rules, fees, and day allowances change, so always confirm your own nationality's current status with an official Bolivian source or consulate before travelling. Keep your passport, entry stamp, and any tour paperwork handy, since remote checkpoints on multi-day lagoon tours near the Chilean and Argentine borders occasionally ask to see documents.

  • Many visitors get up to 90 days per year, often stamped in shorter blocks
  • Some nationalities pay a reciprocity fee and provide extra documents
  • Carry your passport on cross-border lagoon tours near Chile and Argentina
  • This is editorial guidance, not legal advice, so verify officially

Best Time to Visit Uyuni

Uyuni has two very different seasons, and which you choose depends on the experience you want. The dry season, roughly May to October, gives you solid, drivable salt, wide-open blue skies, and reliable access to the distant lagoons and geysers, at the cost of bitterly cold nights that can drop well below freezing. The wet season, roughly December to March, brings a thin sheen of water that turns the salar into the famous mirror reflecting the sky, but rain also floods and closes parts of the flat and can make the far lagoon routes impassable. Whichever you pick, days can be sunny while nights are brutally cold, so pack serious layers and prepare for strong altiplano wind and intense high-altitude sun.

  • May to October: firm salt, clear skies, easy tour access, very cold nights
  • December to March: the mirror effect, but flooding can close routes
  • Shoulder months can mix both looks with less predictable conditions
  • Pack heavy layers, sun protection, and wind-proof gear year-round

Pros and Cons of Uyuni

Uyuni delivers one of the most surreal landscapes on the planet, and that is precisely why people come. The upside is enormous natural spectacle at a very low cost: the endless white salt flat, the mirror after rain, red and green lagoons, flamingos, geysers, and the eerie Train Cemetery at the edge of town. The downside is everything else. This is a cold, dusty, functional frontier town with basic infrastructure, slow wifi, little nightlife, simple food, and a small local scene that empties out around the tour cycle. Altitude affects many visitors on arrival. Come for a short, awe-inspiring stop, manage your expectations about comfort and connectivity, and you will leave delighted rather than disappointed.

  • Pro: a genuinely once-in-a-lifetime landscape at bargain prices
  • Pro: clean, dry high-altitude air and vast, empty horizons
  • Con: harsh cold, wind, basic wifi, and limited comforts
  • Con: minimal nightlife, simple food, and a tiny transient community

Who Uyuni Is For

Uyuni is for the traveller chasing a landscape, not a lifestyle. It rewards photographers, adventure seekers, and curious wanderers who want to stand in the middle of the world's largest salt flat and watch the horizon dissolve into sky. It fits neatly into an overland route through Bolivia, Chile, or Argentina as a two-to-four-night highlight. It is not for anyone who needs reliable internet, a comfortable work rhythm, warmth, or a social scene, and it is a poor choice as a long-term nomad base. If you can embrace the cold, the altitude, and the rough edges for a few unforgettable days, Uyuni will give you one of the most extraordinary experiences in South America.

  • Great for photographers, overlanders, and bucket-list adventurers
  • Ideal as a short two-to-four-night stop on a longer regional route
  • Poor fit for remote workers needing stable wifi and daily comfort
  • Best for those who can handle cold, altitude, and basic infrastructure

Frequently Asked Questions About Uyuni

How much does it cost to live in Uyuni as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Uyuni is around $700 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Uyuni?

Uyuni rates 4 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is on the limited side for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Uyuni safe for digital nomads?

Uyuni scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Uyuni a good city for digital nomads overall?

Uyuni stands out for nature and the outdoors and affordability among remote workers. With a typical budget near $700 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
