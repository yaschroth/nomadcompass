Setting Up as a Nomad in Kilkenny

A medieval city of black limestone, with a castle at one end and a cathedral at the other. What pulls nomads here is English, backed by safety and culture. Budget around $1,650 a month. The size of the nomad scene is the weak spot.

Best Neighborhoods in Kilkenny

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

The Medieval Mile

$$$
Medieval & Central

Castle at one end, cathedral at the other

The historic spine of Kilkenny running from the castle up through the High Street and Parliament Street to St Canice's Cathedral, past the Tholsel, Rothe House and the slipways, the narrow lanes that connect the parallel streets. Almost everything worth seeing in the city is on this route.

Best for: Anyone who wants the whole city within a twenty minute walk
Pros
  • Castle, cathedral, round tower and medieval houses on one route
  • The best pubs and restaurants are along it
  • Genuinely walkable and largely unspoiled
Cons
  • The most expensive rents in the town
  • Busy with visitors and stag parties at weekends
  • Very limited housing stock in the historic core

The castle and the Parade

$$$
Grand & Green

The Butler seat and its parkland

The southern end of the Medieval Mile around Kilkenny Castle, held by the Butler family from 1391 until 1967 when they sold it to the city for fifty pounds, with fifty acres of parkland behind it and the Design Centre in the former stables opposite, which is where the craft tradition is most visible.

Best for: Anyone who wants the parkland and the craft workshops
Pros
  • Fifty acres of castle parkland open to the public
  • The Design Centre and the craft workshops opposite
  • The Parade is the town's main open space
Cons
  • Very little residential stock
  • Busiest part of the town for visitors
  • Parking is difficult and expensive

The Nore riverside

$$
Riverside & Quiet

The walk along the river below the castle

The riverside path along the Nore running south from the castle, past the weir and out towards the countryside, with the Lacken Walk on the far bank and the Woodstock estate further downstream. It is the town's main green corridor and the walk most residents use.

Best for: Anyone who wants the river path and quieter housing
Pros
  • A genuinely good riverside walk from the middle of the town
  • Quieter than the Medieval Mile a few streets away
  • The Nore holds otters and kingfishers
Cons
  • Limited shops and restaurants
  • The path floods after heavy rain
  • Housing here is mostly older and small

Bennettsbridge and the Nore valley

$$
Rural & Craft

The craft villages downstream, about eight kilometres south

The village on the Nore south of Kilkenny where Nicholas Mosse Pottery and several other craft workshops operate, and beyond it Thomastown and the twelfth century Cistercian ruin at Jerpoint Abbey, which has the finest carved cloister in Ireland. The valley is the county's most attractive countryside.

Best for: Anyone drawn to the craft workshops or wanting a village base
Pros
  • Working craft potteries and studios rather than shops
  • Jerpoint Abbey and its carved cloister nearby
  • Cheaper housing and a genuine village
Cons
  • Eight kilometres from Kilkenny with limited bus service
  • Car dependent
  • Very quiet in the evening

Cost of Living in Kilkenny

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Kilkenny, so the $1,650 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 951 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,650 a month. A one-bedroom runs roughly $800 to $1,200, and Ireland's housing shortage is the defining economic fact here as everywhere in the country, with supply tight and rents having risen sharply over the past decade. Groceries are expensive by European standards. Eating out is expensive, though Kilkenny has an unusually good restaurant scene for a town of its size. Utilities are moderate and heating runs through a mild damp winter. Local property charges apply to owners rather than renters, unlike the British system. Anyone comparing with Dublin should note that rents here run well under half the capital's, which is the main reason people have moved out along the railway line.

Where to Work in Kilkenny

Connectivity is good. Fibre reaches most of Kilkenny at reasonable speeds and mobile coverage is comprehensive, with reliable power. Ireland's National Broadband Plan has extended fibre well into rural areas around the town. There is coworking, and the government has funded a network of remote working hubs across the country as a deliberate policy of moving jobs out of Dublin, several of which are within reach. The time zone is GMT with Irish standard time, which serves Europe in the afternoon and North America comfortably in the evening. Anyone considering one of the state-funded remote working hubs should look at the Connected Hubs network, which lists them nationally and is genuinely well organised.

Getting Around Kilkenny

Kilkenny is small and entirely walkable, with the castle at one end of the Medieval Mile and St Canice's Cathedral at the other, and the whole route takes twenty minutes. Buses cover the town and the region. The railway runs to Dublin in about ninety minutes and to Waterford in forty. Dublin Airport is roughly two hours by road or by direct bus. A car helps for reaching the countryside, and the Nore valley, Jerpoint Abbey and the Wicklow mountains are all within reach. Anyone walking the Medieval Mile should pick up the trail map, since the route links the castle, the cathedral and a dozen buildings that are easy to walk past unnoticed.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Kilkenny

Ireland is a member of the European Union but not of the Schengen area, so it operates its own visa system: European Union and EEA citizens may live and work here with no permit, and most Western nationalities enter visa-free for ninety days. Ireland is not in Schengen, so time here does not count against a Schengen allowance, which is a genuinely useful feature for anyone managing that limit. There is no digital nomad visa. Longer stays require an employment permit or one of the other immigration categories. Anyone using Ireland to pause a Schengen clock should keep the entry and exit evidence, since proving where you were is your responsibility rather than the border's.

Best Time to Visit Kilkenny

May to September is the season, with July around nineteen degrees and long evenings. Ireland's weather is unreliable in every month, so the difference is daylight and probability rather than certainty. The Cat Laughs comedy festival in June and the Kilkenny Arts Festival in August are the two events that fill the town and are both genuinely good. Winter from November to February is mild, wet and dark, rarely freezing but persistently grey. Spring comes early and the countryside is green in every month, which is the compensation. Anyone visiting during the arts festival in August should book early, since the town's accommodation fills and prices in that week bear no relation to the rest of the year.

Pros and Cons of Kilkenny

The case for Kilkenny is a medieval city that survived: black limestone buildings, a castle held by the Butler family for six centuries, a thirteenth century cathedral with a round tower you can still climb, and slipways and lanes that follow their original lines. It has a genuine arts and craft scene and better food and pubs than a town of twenty seven thousand should support. The case against is Irish costs, a housing market under real strain, and a town small enough that the choice in most things runs out quickly.

Who Kilkenny Is For

Kilkenny suits anyone who wants an Irish base outside Dublin with real character and good rail links, particularly people working remotely for European or American employers. It suits craftspeople, since the Kilkenny design tradition is genuine and the workshops are working ones. It suits anyone who takes pubs seriously. It does not suit anyone on a tight budget, anyone who needs a city, or anyone arriving without accommodation arranged, because Ireland's rental market is difficult in a way that surprises people from elsewhere in Europe. It also suits hurling supporters, since Kilkenny is the most successful county in the sport's history and Nowlan Park on a championship day is a genuine spectacle.

Frequently Asked Questions About Kilkenny

How much does it cost to live in Kilkenny as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Kilkenny is around

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Kilkenny?

Kilkenny rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is good for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Kilkenny safe for digital nomads?

Kilkenny scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Kilkenny a good city for digital nomads overall?
