Setting Up as a Nomad in Dhaka

One of the world's most densely packed megacities, chaotic and warm-hearted, with rickshaw art, river ferries and startlingly low costs. Plan on roughly $700 a month. Its strengths are low costs and food, while air quality is where it scores lower.

Best Neighborhoods in Dhaka

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Gulshan

$$$$
Upmarket & International

Embassies, lake views and the city's serviced apartments

Dhaka's diplomatic and business quarter, built on a numbered road grid around Gulshan lake, with embassies, corporate towers, supermarkets and the best hotels. It has the widest streets, the most reliable power and the most English spoken anywhere in the city. Almost every foreign resident starts here.

Best for: Remote workers who want reliable power, fast internet and international-standard services above all else.
Pros
  • Most dependable electricity and internet in Dhaka
  • Serviced apartments, supermarkets and clinics geared to foreigners
  • Lakeside walking paths and wider roads
Cons
  • By far the most expensive rents in the country
  • Gridlock on the Gulshan avenues most of the day
  • Feels like a bubble, detached from the rest of Dhaka

Banani

$$$
Lively & Central

Rooftop restaurants and offices on road 11

Immediately north of Gulshan and busier at street level, Banani stacks cafes, rooftop restaurants and small offices into mid-rise buildings around road 11. Younger professionals and returning Bangladeshi expats live here. Rents sit slightly under Gulshan while keeping the same shops and services within reach.

Best for: Nomads who want Gulshan's convenience with more street life and slightly lower rent.
Pros
  • Best cafe and restaurant density in north Dhaka
  • Cheaper than Gulshan while sharing its amenities
  • Walking distance to Gulshan offices and the airport road
Cons
  • Very noisy, constant horns on road 11
  • Traffic and construction dust are relentless
  • Small flats, little green space

Dhanmondi

$$
Cultural & Value

Lake-side older Dhaka, universities, bookshops and coaching centres

A settled residential area in the west built around the Dhanmondi lake, full of universities, cultural centres, bookshops and long-standing family apartment blocks. It has the most walkable lakefront in Dhaka and a strong cafe culture aimed at students. Rents are well below the Gulshan side.

Best for: Long stayers who want cultural life, lake walks and a much lower rent than the diplomatic zone.
Pros
  • Clearly cheaper than Gulshan or Banani
  • Lakeside paths, cafes and cultural venues
  • Deep local character and good street food
Cons
  • Power cuts are more frequent than in Gulshan
  • Severe congestion on Mirpur Road
  • Waterlogging during heavy monsoon rain

Bashundhara Residential Area

$$
Planned & Modern

Planned blocks, new apartments and a big student population

A large master-planned district on the eastern edge, laid out in lettered blocks with wide roads, newer apartment buildings and North South University at its heart. It is calmer and cleaner than inner Dhaka, with the country's biggest shopping mall nearby, and rent per square metre is reasonable.

Best for: Nomads who want a modern, spacious flat and calmer streets, and do not mind being out of the centre.
Pros
  • Newer buildings with lifts and backup generators
  • Wider, quieter, better-planned roads
  • Good value space compared with Gulshan
Cons
  • Long, unpredictable commute into central Dhaka
  • Still under construction in many blocks, so dust and noise
  • Low-lying land, some blocks flood in heavy rain

Uttara

$$
Suburban & Quiet

Sector-numbered suburb by the airport with metro access

A self-contained northern suburb organised into numbered sectors, with parks, lakes, schools and its own restaurants and malls. The Dhaka metro terminates here, which makes trips south far more predictable than by road. It is the quietest option for anyone happy to live away from the business districts.

Best for: Budget-minded long stayers and families who value quiet, space and metro access over central location.
Pros
  • Metro line makes journeys south predictable
  • Noticeably cheaper and quieter than Gulshan
  • Parks, lakes and a self-contained set of services
Cons
  • Far from Gulshan and the CBD if you travel by road
  • Aircraft noise on approach paths near the airport
  • Nightlife and international options are thin

Cost of Living in Dhaka

A single nomad can live in Dhaka on roughly $700 per month, and that figure buys a comfortable rather than a bare existence. A furnished one-bedroom flat on a monthly basis in Gulshan, Banani or Dhanmondi runs $300 to $500, while comparable space in Mohammadpur or Uttara drops to $180 to $300. Local meals cost $1 to $3, a plate of biryani at a neighbourhood institution is around $2.50, and restaurant dining in the diplomatic zone climbs to $10 to $20 per head. A Grameenphone or Robi SIM with 30GB to 50GB costs $4 to $8 monthly. Coworking desks run $60 to $120. Utilities including electricity, an inverter or generator share and a fibre line total $40 to $80, higher in summer when air conditioning runs constantly. Transport rarely exceeds $40. The value is genuine, though comfort here is bought in air conditioning, filtered air and backup power rather than in square metres.

Where to Work in Dhaka

Base yourself in Gulshan, Banani or Dhanmondi; these hold the embassies, the reliable fibre and most of the cafe culture. Moar and North End Coffee Roasters have branches across Gulshan and Dhanmondi and are the default laptop cafes, with Crimson Cup and Cafe Mango also tolerating long sessions. For dedicated space, Impact Hub Dhaka and Hubdhaka in Gulshan offer day passes and monthly desks, and Bangladesh has a growing cluster of startup incubators around Gulshan Avenue that rent desks. Fibre to residential and office buildings commonly delivers 30 Mbps to 100 Mbps and is decent by regional standards, but load shedding remains a real constraint, so confirm any building has an IPS, inverter or generator before signing. Mobile 4G is widely usable as backup. Cafes get loud in the evening, power sockets are scarce in older venues, and the government has previously throttled mobile internet during unrest, so a VPN and a data buffer are sensible.

Getting Around Dhaka

Dhaka has no comprehensive metro, but Line 6 of the Dhaka Metro Rail now runs elevated from Uttara North through Mirpur, Farmgate and Motijheel, and it is transformative for that corridor; fares are roughly $0.20 to $1.00. Everything else is road, and Dhaka has some of the worst congestion on earth, with average speeds in the single digits during peak hours. Pathao and Uber are the workhorses: a Pathao motorcycle ride across town costs $1 to $3 and is far faster than a car, while a car booking is $3 to $8. CNG auto-rickshaws cost $1.50 to $4 after the inevitable haggling. Local buses are cheap at under $0.30 but crowded and hard to parse without Bangla. Walkability is poor because footpaths are broken, occupied or missing. Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport sits north of the city; allow ninety minutes from Gulshan by car despite the short distance. No scooter or car ownership is needed.

Visas for Dhaka

Bangladesh has no digital nomad visa and none is under serious discussion. Most Western nationals, including US, UK, EU, Canadian and Australian passport holders, are eligible for visa on arrival at Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport for up to 30 days at a fee of around $51, but this is discretionary rather than guaranteed: immigration officers can and do refuse it, and they typically expect proof of onward travel, a hotel booking and sufficient cash. The safer route is applying in advance at a Bangladeshi mission for a tourist visa, which is commonly issued for 30 to 90 days and sometimes with multiple entries and validity up to a year. Extensions are handled by the Department of Immigration and Passports in Agargaon, are bureaucratic and are usually granted only for short periods. Overstays attract fines and departure delays. Working remotely on a tourist visa is not formally provided for; the on-the-ground reality is that laptop work goes unremarked.

Best Time to Visit Dhaka

The comfortable window is November to February, when daytime highs settle around 25C to 29C, humidity drops and evenings turn genuinely cool in December and January. That is the season for travel and for the winter wedding and cultural calendar. March and April heat up quickly, and by April and May temperatures reach the high thirties with brutal humidity and pre-monsoon nor'wester storms. The southwest monsoon runs roughly June to September, delivering heavy, sustained rain, waterlogged streets and serious traffic paralysis, though it is also the greenest and cheapest stretch. October is a transitional month and increasingly pleasant late on. The offsetting problem with the cool season is air quality: Dhaka regularly ranks among the world's most polluted cities from December through March, with brick kiln smoke and dust pushing AQI past 200. Key dates include Pohela Boishakh in mid-April, Durga Puja in autumn and the two Eid holidays, which empty the city.

Pros and Cons of Dhaka

The case for Dhaka is cost, energy and access. Very few capitals let a remote worker live centrally, eat well and hire help on $700 a month, and the country is genuinely cheap once rent is settled. English is widely spoken in the professional and educated classes, hospitality toward foreigners is warm and often disarming, the food culture is underrated, and Dhaka is a serious base for exploring Sylhet's tea hills, the Sundarbans and Cox's Bazar. The startup and NGO scene means there are actual peers. Against that: the traffic is genuinely among the world's worst and eats hours daily, the air quality in winter is a real health cost rather than an inconvenience, footpaths and public space are poor, and load shedding still interrupts work. Political unrest and hartals can shut the city and disrupt internet. Solo women face persistent staring and harassment, alcohol is restricted, serious medical care means flying to Bangkok, and there is close to no nomad infrastructure.

Who Dhaka Is For

Dhaka suits the nomad with a purpose beyond arbitrage: development sector workers, researchers, journalists, textile and supply chain people, and anyone with family, business or a genuine curiosity about South Asia at full volume. It rewards travellers who have already done India or Pakistan, who are comfortable negotiating chaos, and who value cost and human warmth over infrastructure. Founders on a tight runway can extend it dramatically here. It is a poor fit for first-time nomads, for anyone with asthma, allergies or cardiovascular issues given the winter air, and for people who need walkable streets, reliable power, an established coworking community or an easy social scene of other remote workers. Anyone whose priority list includes nightlife, beaches within reach, or predictable calendars will be frustrated. Solo women should weigh the harassment reality honestly. Those wanting cheap Asia without the friction should look at Chiang Mai, Da Nang or Kathmandu instead.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dhaka

How much does it cost to live in Dhaka as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Dhaka is around $700 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Dhaka?

Dhaka rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Dhaka safe for digital nomads?

Dhaka scores 5 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is average for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Dhaka a good city for digital nomads overall?

Dhaka stands out for affordability and its food scene among remote workers. With a typical budget near $700 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
