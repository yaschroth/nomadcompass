A Remote Worker's Guide to Oslo

Where fjords meet cutting-edge architecture in the world's greenest capital. Plan on roughly $5,000 a month. Its strengths are fast WiFi and nature, while cost is where it scores lower.

Cost of Living in Oslo

Oslo is one of the most expensive cities in the world, so budget carefully before committing to a stay. All figures are in Norwegian krone (NOK) with approximate USD equivalents at roughly 10.5 NOK to 1 USD.

  • Rent, central (Frogner, Sentrum, Grunerlokka): 18,000-25,000 NOK/month ($1,700-2,400)
  • Rent, cheaper area (Grunerlokka east, Toyen, Sagene): 12,000-16,000 NOK/month ($1,140-1,520)
  • Groceries: 4,000-6,000 NOK/month ($380-570); cooking at home is essential for budget control
  • Eating out: a sit-down lunch costs 180-250 NOK ($17-24), dinner easily 350-600 NOK ($33-57) per person
  • Coworking hot-desk: 2,000-3,500 NOK/month ($190-330); day passes run 250-500 NOK ($24-47)
  • Local transport (Ruter 30-day pass, Zone 1): about 728 NOK/month ($69)
  • Total realistic range: 30,000-45,000 NOK/month ($2,850-4,300) depending on housing choice and lifestyle

Alcohol, restaurants, and leisure activities add up fast. Many nomads reduce costs by cooking at home, sharing apartments, and using the excellent public transit instead of ride-shares.

Where to Work

Oslo has a solid coworking scene anchored by a few major players. Mesh (two locations, Nationaltheatret and Youngstorget) is the largest startup hub in the Nordics, with a buzzing community feel and flexible hot-desk memberships. Rebel at Universitetsgata 2 occupies a converted 1960s office block with podcast studios and its own microbrewery, though it sits at the pricier end. Factory Toyen in Kjolberggata is a converted textile factory with over 6,000 square metres of space, a strong creative-tech community, and a more relaxed vibe. Spaces runs several polished locations around the city including Nydalen and Kvadraturen.

Cafe-working culture exists but is more limited than in southern European cities. Norwegians tend to work silently and some cafes quietly discourage lingering for hours. Fuglen (Universitetsgata 2) and Godt Brod Grunerlokka (Thorvald Meyers gate 49) are among the most laptop-tolerant spots with reliable WiFi. For serious output days, a coworking membership is the better call.

WiFi quality is generally excellent. Oslo ranks among the top European cities for internet speed, with most coworking spaces and cafes delivering 50-200 Mbps. Mobile data on a local SIM (Telia, Telenor, ice.no) is fast and affordable, so connectivity is rarely an issue anywhere in the city.

Getting Around

Oslo is compact and very walkable in its core. The public transport network (run by Ruter) covers the metro (T-bane), trams, buses, and ferries on one integrated ticket system. A 30-day pass in Zone 1 costs around 728 NOK ($69) and covers almost everything you will need as a visitor. Download the Ruter app to buy tickets before boarding; inspectors do check.

Cycling is popular year-round for locals, and the city operates a dock bike-share scheme (Oslo Bysykkel) with an affordable season pass. The city centre is hilly in places, but most areas around Frogner, Grunerlokka, and Sentrum are flat enough.

Getting to and from Oslo Gardermoen Airport (OSL): the Flytoget airport express train runs every 10 minutes and takes 19-22 minutes to Oslo Central Station, costing 268 NOK ($25) one way. The slower Vy regional train takes about 25 minutes and costs roughly half the price. Buses are cheaper still but take 45 minutes or more depending on traffic.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Norway is a Schengen member. Citizens of the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and most of the EU can enter visa-free and stay up to 90 days within any 180-day period. There is no option to extend a tourist stay beyond the 90-day Schengen limit from inside the zone.

Norway does have an independent contractor or freelancer visa for those who want to stay longer. It requires documented remote income equivalent to at least 360,000 NOK per year (around $34,000) from foreign clients, plus proof of accommodation and health insurance. It grants a two-year residence permit. Applications are processed through the Norwegian Directorate of Immigration (UDI).

Note that ETIAS, the Schengen travel authorisation system for visa-exempt visitors (including US, UK, and Australian passport holders), is expected to launch in late 2026. Always verify current requirements at udi.no and the official Norwegian foreign ministry site before traveling.

Best Time to Visit

For remote work combined with quality of life, May through September is the sweet spot. June and early July offer long daylight hours (up to 19 hours of daylight around the solstice), warm temperatures of 18-22 degrees Celsius (64-72 F), and a city that genuinely comes alive with outdoor life, rooftop terraces, and fjord swimming.

May and September are arguably better for focused work: fewer crowds, lower prices at accommodation, and milder weather without the tourist surge. Autumn brings reliable cozy cafe culture as the leaves turn.

Winter (November through March) is cold, dark (as few as 6 hours of daylight), and can feel isolating if you are working alone. That said, infrastructure never shuts down, heating is excellent, and the city is genuinely beautiful under snow. Come in winter only if you are prepared for the darkness and have a social plan.

Pros and Cons

  • Extremely safe: Oslo consistently ranks among the safest cities globally
  • World-class infrastructure and internet
  • English spoken fluently: almost no language barrier in day-to-day life
  • Outstanding nature access: forests and fjord swimming are 20 minutes from the centre
  • Clean, organised, efficient city with an excellent transit network
  • Strong startup and tech community for networking
  • Extremely expensive: one of the highest cost-of-living cities in Europe
  • Social culture can feel reserved: making local friends takes time
  • Dark winters with limited daylight from November to February
  • Limited nightlife variety compared to Berlin or Amsterdam
  • Alcohol is expensive and restricted in hours and where it can be purchased
  • Short Schengen window with limited long-stay options for most passports

Who Oslo Is Best For

Oslo suits nomads who earn well in their home currency and prioritise safety, cleanliness, and access to nature. If you want to combine city productivity with weekend hiking and fjord kayaking without any logistical friction, few cities match it. Freelancers in tech, design, or finance with strong incomes will find it comfortable and inspiring. The startup community is tight-knit and welcoming to foreign professionals.

Skip Oslo if you are on a strict budget, dependent on a vibrant social scene to stay motivated, or planning a longer stay without the income required for the freelancer visa. It is also a tough sell if you struggle with seasonal darkness or come from a warm climate and plan to visit in winter.

Frequently Asked Questions About Oslo

How much does it cost to live in Oslo as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $5,000 a month in Oslo for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Oslo?

Oslo rates 9 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Oslo safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Oslo is 8/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Oslo a good city for digital nomads overall?

Oslo stands out for fast WiFi and nature among remote workers. With a typical budget near $5,000 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
