Where to Work

Stavanger has a small but functional coworking scene, shaped by its oil-and-gas corporate culture more than a freelance community.

Innovation Dock on the waterfront is the most well-known space: 3,500 m2 of desks, meeting rooms, and sea views, with 1 Gbps symmetrical fibre. It attracts startups and energy-sector companies, so the community tilts corporate rather than nomadic. Innoasis in the city centre is Norway's smart-city innovation hub; flex desk membership runs $420/month and the space is open and professional. Spaces has a commercial location in the city centre with the typical international coworking formula.

Cafe work is possible but not the norm. Coffeeberry in the centre is the clearest laptop-friendly option, with free WiFi and power outlets. Cafe Sting, perched by the Valberg tower, is atmospheric and a local favourite, though it is more social than work-focused.

Mobile data (4G/5G) is excellent and consistent across the city centre and suburbs. Fixed broadband in apartments is typically 100-500 Mbps. Coworking WiFi at Innovation Dock reaches 1 Gbps. You will not have connectivity problems in Stavanger.

Getting Around

The city centre is compact and walkable. The historic wooden-house district (Gamle Stavanger), the harbour, the main shopping street, and most restaurants are within 15 minutes on foot of each other. You do not need a car to live and work in the centre.

Buses are operated by Kolumbus and cover the greater city well. A monthly bus pass costs roughly $68. The network connects Stavanger to Sandnes and the broader Jæren region. Trams do not exist here; buses and ferries are the public transport backbone.

Getting to and from the airport: Stavanger Airport Sola is 14 km southwest of the city. The Flybussen airport express departs every 20 minutes and takes around 25-30 minutes to the city centre; a single ticket costs about $19 online. The cheaper public bus route (bus 42 toward Sandnes, then X30) takes about 65 minutes and costs the standard fare. Taxis run $53 or more ($47+), higher on evenings and weekends.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Norway is part of the Schengen Area. Most Western passport holders (US, UK, Canada, Australia, EU) can enter visa-free and stay up to 90 days within any 180-day period. There is no general tourist extension available; once your 90 days are up, you must leave the Schengen zone.

Norway does not offer a mainstream "digital nomad visa" of the type you find in Portugal or Estonia. However, a self-employed worker permit exists for non-EU nationals who have a sole proprietorship abroad and at least one Norwegian client; it allows stays of up to two years, extendable to six, with a path to permanent residency. The income threshold is roughly $37,920 per year gross. This is a niche route and requires application from outside Norway in most cases.

UK nationals post-Brexit are subject to the same 90/180 Schengen rule. EU/EEA citizens can live and work freely.

Always verify current rules with the Norwegian Directorate of Immigration (udi.no) before making plans.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit

For remote work combined with quality of life, June through August is the clear sweet spot. Temperatures sit in the 15-20 degrees C range (59-68 F), days are extremely long (near-midnight dusk in June), and outdoor activities are accessible. June is typically the driest month. Crowds arrive in July, but Stavanger does not feel overwhelmed the way tourist-heavy cities do.

September and early October are a strong second choice: the summer crowds thin out, the landscape shifts to autumn colours, and the weather stays mild enough for day trips to the Lysefjord and Preikestolen.

Winter (November through February) is dark, wet, and rainy more than snowy. Temperatures hover just above freezing, and the short daylight hours can affect productivity and mood. It is manageable, but you need to prepare mentally for a grey and damp few months. The upside: accommodation is cheaper and the city feels quieter.

Spring (March to May) brings gradually improving light and temperatures, but rain persists and the hiking season has not yet opened fully. It is a reasonable time to visit if you prioritise lower prices and fewer tourists.

Pros and Cons

  • Excellent internet and infrastructure throughout the city
  • Very safe, with low crime and a relaxed, orderly environment
  • Outstanding nature access: Preikestolen, Kjeragbolten, and Lysefjord are nearby
  • High English fluency among locals; you will rarely struggle to communicate
  • Clean, well-maintained city with good air quality and a compact, walkable centre
  • Strong food scene, including Michelin-starred restaurants and a genuine seafood market culture
  • Very high cost of living; one of the most expensive cities in Europe
  • Small international nomad community; you will mostly meet oil-industry expats rather than other remote workers
  • No official digital nomad visa; the 90-day Schengen cap applies to most non-EU passports
  • Dark, rainy winters that can drag on from October through March
  • Limited coworking variety; options are functional but few, and skew corporate
  • Nightlife is modest for a city of its size; most venues close early by European standards

Who Stavanger Is Best For

Stavanger suits remote workers who prioritise safety, clean infrastructure, and extraordinary weekend hiking over cost or a buzzing nomad scene. If you want to spend your evenings in a fjord kayak or your weekends hiking to Preikestolen, and you can handle Norwegian prices without flinching, Stavanger delivers a genuinely high quality of life. EU and EEA passport holders with no Schengen restriction get the most value here since they can stay as long as they want. It also works well as a short anchor point: fly in, work solidly for 6-8 weeks, explore the region, then move on before the 90-day clock runs out.

Skip Stavanger if you are watching your budget closely, if you need a large community of fellow nomads for networking or social life, or if you are a non-EU passport holder wanting to stay more than 90 days without pursuing a formal work permit. It is also a poor fit if you need a warm climate or dislike grey, rainy weather for months at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions About Stavanger

How much does it cost to live in Stavanger as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,850 a month in Stavanger for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Stavanger?

Stavanger rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Stavanger safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Stavanger is 9/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Stavanger a good city for digital nomads overall?

Stavanger stands out for nature and safety among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,850 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
