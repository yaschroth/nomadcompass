What Ubud Offers Digital Nomads

Bali's spiritual heart, rice terraces, temples, and wellness retreats. For remote workers, Ubud leans on nature and a large nomad community, with a typical budget near $1,800 a month. The main trade-off is nightlife.

Cost of Living in Ubud

Ubud sits at a comfortable middle ground: noticeably cheaper than Canggu or Seminyak, but pricier than rural Bali towns. Your monthly spend depends heavily on how much you lean into the tourist-facing cafes and health food scene versus eating local.

  • Rent (central Ubud, studio): IDR 7,000,000–12,000,000/month (USD 430–740). Expect a clean room with AC, a kitchenette, and usually a small garden or rice-field view.
  • Rent (quieter areas: Penestanan, Nyuh Kuning, Sayan): IDR 5,000,000–9,000,000/month (USD 305–555). These neighborhoods are 10–20 minutes from the center by scooter and often get you more space for less.
  • Groceries (self-catering, local market staples): IDR 700,000–1,200,000/month (USD 43–74). Cooking with local ingredients from Pasar Ubud is the cheapest route; imported goods at Bintang Supermarket add up fast.
  • Eating out (warung, local): IDR 20,000–40,000 per meal (USD 1.25–2.50). A full plate of nasi goreng or nasi campur at a family warung rarely exceeds this.
  • Eating out (cafes, health food, expat dining): IDR 80,000–200,000 per meal (USD 5–12). Ubud has no shortage of smoothie bowls and avocado toast; budget accordingly if that is your daily routine.
  • Coworking hot-desk (monthly): IDR 1,800,000–3,500,000/month (USD 110–215). Day passes run IDR 130,000–220,000 (USD 8–14).
  • Local transport (Grab/Gojek, scooter rental): IDR 400,000–800,000/month (USD 25–49) for a scooter rental, or roughly IDR 25,000–80,000 per Grab ride.
  • Total realistic monthly range: USD 1,000–1,600 covers a comfortable nomad life with decent accommodation, daily cafe work, and regular eating out. Budget travelers who cook and ride a scooter can get under USD 800; anyone who leans into Ubud's wellness and fine-dining scene can easily spend USD 2,000+.

Where to Work

Ubud punches above its size when it comes to remote-work infrastructure, though it is not as wired as Canggu.

Coworking Spaces

Outpost Ubud (Penestanan area) is the most established option: air-conditioned zones, standing desks, 24-hour access, a rooftop yoga studio, and a coliving component if you want community built in. Monthly hot-desk passes run around USD 150–200. Setter Ubud (Jl. Raya Sanggingan) is the newer, more design-forward space: 200 Mbps fiber, ergonomic chairs, a cafe on-site, and dedicated focus areas. Both spaces host events and attract a mix of long-term residents and short-term visitors.

Cafe Work Culture

Ubud has a strong tradition of laptop-friendly cafes, and many double as light coworking spots. Seniman Coffee Studio on Jl. Sriwedari has consistently reliable WiFi and excellent in-house roasted coffee. Zest Ubud on Jl. Goutama Selatan is spacious, has plentiful power outlets, and draws a steady nomad crowd. Most cafes expect you to keep ordering; a coffee every 90 minutes is the unspoken norm at busy spots.

The Real WiFi Situation

Dedicated coworking spaces typically offer 50–200 Mbps and are video-call reliable. Cafes average 10–40 Mbps, which handles most tasks but can degrade during the lunch rush. Mobile data (Telkomsel or XL Axiata) is cheap and fast enough to be a reliable backup: a 50 GB SIM card costs around IDR 100,000 (USD 6). Power outages are rare but do happen during heavy rain; a coworking space with a generator is worth the premium if your work is time-sensitive.

Getting Around

Ubud's center is compact but the surrounding area sprawls across narrow roads through rice fields and jungle. Walking works for the few blocks around Jl. Monkey Forest and Jl. Raya Ubud, but the sidewalks are broken or nonexistent in most directions, and the hills add up quickly. For anything beyond the immediate center, you need transport.

  • Scooter rental: The default choice for most nomads. Rentals run IDR 60,000–100,000/day (USD 4–6) or IDR 400,000–700,000/month. An international driving permit technically required, though rarely checked. Be honest with yourself about your confidence level on narrow, hilly roads with no shoulders.
  • Grab / Gojek: Available and reliable within Ubud. Most rides in town cost IDR 15,000–40,000 (USD 1–2.50). These apps also work for food delivery.
  • Bluebird taxi: Metered, honest, useful for longer trips or if you prefer not to use an app.
  • Airport transfer: Ngurah Rai International Airport (DPS) is roughly 35–40 km south and takes 75–100 minutes depending on traffic. A Grab from Ubud to the airport costs about USD 12–18. Pre-arranged private transfers (booked through your accommodation or a local driver) run USD 20–35 and are worth it for early flights when Grab can be slow to dispatch. The official airport taxi queue charges 2–3 times more; if you order a Grab, walk a short distance outside the airport grounds first.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Most visitors arrive on a Visa on Arrival (VOA / e-VOA), available to passport holders from over 90 countries including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and the EU. The VOA costs IDR 500,000 (approximately USD 30) at the airport, grants 30 days of stay, and can be extended once at a local immigration office for another 30 days (IDR 500,000, in-person visit required with biometrics since mid-2025). Total maximum stay on this path: 60 days.

For longer stays, Indonesia introduced the E33G Remote Worker KITAS (the closest thing to a digital nomad visa), launched April 2024. It allows one year of residence, renewable once, for people employed full-time by a company outside Indonesia. Requirements include proof of employment (not freelance), a minimum annual income of USD 60,000, and a minimum bank balance of USD 2,000 over the preceding 3 months. Official fees total around USD 315; using a visa agent adds USD 300–600. The E33G does not permit working for Indonesian employers or clients.

Many nomads cycle between the VOA and visa runs to neighboring countries, or switch to a Social/Cultural Visa (B211A) for 60 days extendable to 180 days. Always verify current requirements with official Indonesian immigration sources before your arrival, as policies change.

Best Time to Visit

Ubud sits at 600–900 meters elevation in central Bali, which makes it noticeably cooler and more humid than the coast year-round. Daytime temperatures stay around 26–30°C; nights drop to 20–23°C.

Dry season (May through October) is the sweet spot for remote work: lower humidity, reliable sunshine, and far fewer power interruptions from heavy storms. May, June, and September are particularly good: crowds and prices are slightly lower than peak July and August, and the weather is close to ideal. October is a strong shoulder month.

Wet season (November through March) brings daily rain, usually in short tropical bursts in the late afternoon or evening rather than all-day grey skies. January is the rainiest month. The landscape turns intensely green, rice fields are at their most photogenic, and Ubud's indoor ecosystem of studios, galleries, and spas keeps you productive and occupied. Connectivity holds up in modern coworking spaces. It is a legitimate choice if you can tolerate humidity and occasional flooding on lower roads.

Avoid high season (late July and August) if crowd-sensitivity or budget matters to you: Monkey Forest Road becomes a tourist thoroughfare, accommodation prices spike 30–50%, and coworking spaces fill up.

Pros and Cons

  • Exceptional food scene at every price point, from IDR 25,000 warungs to internationally acclaimed fine dining
  • Strong nomad and expat community with established coworking options and frequent events
  • Genuinely beautiful natural setting: rice terraces, jungle, and rivers within walking distance of the center
  • Good infrastructure for wellness: yoga studios, spas, and health food options are abundant and affordable by Western standards
  • Cooler than coastal Bali, which matters if you find tropical heat difficult to work in
  • Rich cultural life: Balinese Hindu ceremonies, traditional dance performances, and art markets are woven into daily life rather than staged for tourists
  • No beach: the nearest coast (Sanur, Canggu) is 45–90 minutes away depending on traffic
  • Poor walkability beyond the center; you need a scooter or app-ride for almost everything
  • Can feel saturated with wellness tourism: much of the commercial center caters to a retreat crowd, which some nomads find grating
  • Traffic on the main roads worsens during high season and is genuinely bad around Jl. Raya Ubud in the morning
  • WiFi quality in cafes is inconsistent; a coworking membership pays for itself fast if your work needs steady bandwidth
  • Visa path for long stays is complex and involves in-person immigration visits; the E33G rules out freelancers entirely

Who Ubud Is Best For

Ubud rewards nomads who want a slower pace without sacrificing good food, community, or culture. It suits you well if you do your best work in a quiet, leafy environment rather than a buzzy beach town. Writers, designers, coaches, and anyone whose work benefits from stepping away from the internet for a few hours will find the rhythm here easier to maintain than in Canggu's party-adjacent energy.

It is also a good fit for nomads in the 30-plus bracket who are done optimizing for nightlife and more interested in morning yoga, good coffee, and an honest Indonesian meal that does not cost USD 20.

Think twice if you need 24-hour urban amenity access, a beach within walking distance, or the kind of fast-paced coworking scene that produces spontaneous collaborations. Ubud's community is real but more scattered and intentional than Canggu's. And if you are a freelancer hoping to stay long-term legally, the E33G visa closes that door; you will be running a visa cycle every 30–60 days, which gets old.

Frequently Asked Questions About Ubud

How much does it cost to live in Ubud as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Ubud is around $1,800 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Ubud?

With a WiFi score of 5/10, connectivity in Ubud is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Ubud safe for digital nomads?

Ubud scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Ubud a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Ubud is worth a look if you value nature and a large nomad community. Costs sit near $1,800 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
