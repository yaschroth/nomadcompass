Cost of Living in Lima

Lima is one of South America's more affordable capital cities, though costs vary sharply by neighborhood. Budget around $1,000 to $1,800 USD per month for a comfortable nomad lifestyle.

  • Rent (central Miraflores or San Isidro): S/2,500 to S/4,500 ($650 to $1,200) for a furnished one-bedroom. Barranco tends toward the lower end of that range.
  • Rent (cheaper areas like Jesus Maria or Pueblo Libre): S/1,800 to S/2,800 ($480 to $750) for a decent furnished apartment.
  • Groceries: S/600 to S/900 ($160 to $240) per month shopping at Wong or Metro supermarkets. Local markets (mercados) cut that figure further.
  • Eating out: A solid lunch menu (menu del dia) runs S/12 to S/20 ($3 to $5). Mid-range restaurants cost S/50 to S/100 ($13 to $26) per person. Upscale cevicherias add up fast.
  • Coworking hot-desk: S/400 to S/900 ($105 to $240) per month depending on the space. Day passes typically cost S/30 to S/50 ($8 to $13).
  • Local transport: Metropolitano BRT fares are S/3.20 per trip. Uber or Cabify rides across Miraflores run S/10 to S/20. Budget S/150 to S/300 ($40 to $80) monthly.
  • Total realistic range: $900 to $1,500 USD per month covers rent, food, coworking, and transport with room for a social life.

Where to Work

Lima's coworking scene has matured quickly. You will find well-designed spaces at every price point in Miraflores, Barranco, and San Isidro.

Comunal Coworking is the most recognized local chain with locations in Miraflores and Barranco, offering reliable fiber, meeting rooms, and a genuine community of freelancers and designers. The Workshop, located next to the Pucllana archaeological site in Miraflores, is a strong pick for a professional atmosphere. International names like WeWork and Spaces are also present in San Isidro for those who need that setup.

Cafe culture for laptop work is genuinely good. Puku Puku has multiple locations across Miraflores and San Isidro, with solid Wi-Fi and specialty Peruvian coffee. Tostaduría Bisetti in Barranco is quieter and excellent for focused mornings.

The real WiFi situation is better than you might expect. Home fiber connections and coworking spaces consistently deliver speeds that handle video calls without trouble. Mobile data on Claro or Entel is fast and cheap if you need a backup. The main caveat is that some smaller cafes limit seating time during busy hours, so ordering regularly is the understood etiquette.

Getting Around

Lima is a massive, sprawling city and traffic is genuinely bad, especially in the mornings and early evenings. Within Miraflores and Barranco you can walk most errands, and the clifftop Malecon provides a pleasant route between the two neighborhoods. Beyond that, you will rely on apps or buses.

Uber and Cabify are reliable and affordable. Most in-district rides cost S/8 to S/18. InDrive lets you negotiate fares and can be cheaper for longer trips. Avoid unmarked street taxis; app-based options are far safer.

The Metropolitano BRT (rapid bus) runs a north to south corridor at S/3.20 a trip and is useful for getting between districts. Lima's Metro (Line 1) connects southern and central districts but does not serve Miraflores or Barranco directly.

From Jorge Chavez International Airport to Miraflores, an Uber costs around S/40 to S/55 (roughly $11 to $15) and takes 30 to 60 minutes depending on traffic. The Airport Express Lima bus is a cheaper option at S/15, running directly to Miraflores. Expect the journey to feel longer than the map suggests.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of the US, Canada, the UK, EU countries, and Australia enter Peru visa-free and receive a tourist stamp allowing up to 90 days on arrival, with a maximum of 183 days within any 365-day period. Unlike some countries in the region, Peru does not generally allow most nationalities to extend a tourist stay from within the country, so a border run is the typical workaround for longer stays.

Peru passed a digital nomad visa law in 2023, but as of late 2025 the visa has not yet been made available to applicants. The updated government documentation published in September 2025 made no reference to it being active. Check the official Superintendencia Nacional de Migraciones website (migraciones.gob.pe) to confirm current status before you arrive.

Best Time to Visit

Lima's climate is mild year-round but the two seasons feel very different. From December through April, you get clear skies, warm temperatures of 26 to 30 degrees Celsius (79 to 86 F), and a lively beach atmosphere along the Malecon. This is summer, hotel prices are higher, and the city buzzes.

From May through November, a coastal fog called garua settles in. You will rarely see rain, but the sky stays a flat gray for weeks. Temperatures hover between 15 and 22 degrees Celsius (59 to 72 F). The city is quieter, cheaper, and many nomads find this the more productive window precisely because there is less temptation to be outdoors.

For remote work, both windows are workable. If you care about sunshine and exploring on weekends, January through March is the sweet spot. If you want lower costs and fewer crowds, June through September delivers that, even if the gray sky takes some adjustment.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: One of the world's great food cities, with an extraordinary range from market lunches to internationally acclaimed restaurants
  • Pro: Affordable cost of living relative to the quality of infrastructure and amenities available
  • Pro: Fast, reliable internet in coworking spaces and most cafes
  • Pro: Active nomad and expat community concentrated in Miraflores and Barranco
  • Pro: Good base for exploring Peru's interior on long weekends
  • Con: Gray, sunless winters from May to November that wear on mood over time
  • Con: Traffic is severe; cross-city commutes can eat 45 to 90 minutes each way
  • Con: Air quality and pollution are noticeable, particularly in central and northern districts
  • Con: Petty theft is a real concern outside the main nomad neighborhoods; staying alert matters
  • Con: No digital nomad visa yet, which limits official long-stay options

Who Lima Is Best For

Lima works particularly well for nomads who care about food culture, want affordable urban living with solid infrastructure, and are comfortable in a large Latin American city. If you spend your evenings exploring restaurants, checking out independent galleries in Barranco, and using weekends as a launchpad for trips to Cusco, Arequipa, or the Amazon, Lima delivers consistently. The cost-to-quality ratio for food especially is hard to beat anywhere in South America.

It suits people on a mid-range budget who want a real city with neighborhood life, not just a tourist bubble. English is limited outside the main nomad areas, so some Spanish goes a long way.

Lima is a harder fit if you need reliable sunshine for your mental health, hate traffic, or want a walkable city where you can cover ground on foot. It can also feel impersonal and chaotic if you are based outside Miraflores, Barranco, or San Isidro. If a quiet mountain town or beach village sounds more appealing than a sprawling metropolis of 11 million, consider Cusco or Huanchaco instead.

Frequently Asked Questions About Lima

How much does it cost to live in Lima as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,800 a month in Lima for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Lima?

Lima rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Lima safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Lima is 4/10, patchy for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Lima a good city for digital nomads overall?

Lima stands out for food and easy visas among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,800 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
