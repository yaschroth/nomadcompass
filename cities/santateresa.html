Setting Up as a Nomad in Santa Teresa

A boho surf town on the Nicoya Peninsula, a single sandy road of yoga studios, beach clubs, and world-class waves. What pulls nomads here is nature, backed by clean air and climate. Budget around $1,400 a month. The size of the nomad scene is the weak spot.

Playa Carmen

$$
Busy, convenient, central

The central crossroads

Playa Carmen is the beating heart of the area, the crossroads where the main coastal road meets the beach access. It concentrates supermarkets, restaurants, surf shops, banks and beach breaks, making it the most convenient and connected spot for a car-free nomad who wants everything close.

Best for: First-timers and nomads who want everything within walking distance
Pros
  • Most central and walkable
  • Best access to shops, banks and restaurants
  • Consistent, beginner-friendly surf breaks
Cons
  • Busiest and dustiest part of town
  • Less peaceful than the outer areas

Santa Teresa Beach

$$$
Trendy, social, upscale

The main strip

Santa Teresa beach is the long, sunset-facing main strip running north from Playa Carmen, lined with beach clubs, yoga studios, boutique hotels, cafes and villas. It is the postcard heart of the scene, buzzing at golden hour, and home to much of the nomad and wellness community along its dusty spine.

Best for: Surf-and-wellness nomads who want the full scene and social energy
Pros
  • Iconic surf and sunsets
  • Best concentration of cafes, coworking and yoga
  • Strong nomad and wellness community
Cons
  • Priciest area with high demand
  • Crowded and lively in high season

Playa Hermosa

$$
Relaxed, spacious, laid-back

Quieter surf to the north

Just north of the main strip, Playa Hermosa is a broad, mellow beach with a calmer, more spacious feel and excellent surf. It suits nomads who want the ocean and community within easy reach but prefer a quieter, less congested base away from the busiest central bustle.

Best for: Surfers and nomads wanting a quieter beach with room to breathe
Pros

Malpais

$$

The quiet fishing-village side

South of Playa Carmen, Malpais keeps more of its original fishing-village character: quieter, more rustic and rugged, with rocky coves, tide pools and a slower pace. It draws those seeking calm and nature over nightlife, and sits closest to the Cabo Blanco nature reserve at the peninsula's tip.

Cost of Living in Santa Teresa

Let us be honest: Santa Teresa is no longer a cheap Costa Rican beach town. Prices have climbed sharply over the past decade as the town has gone upscale, and a comfortable nomad month here typically lands between $1,200 and $2,200, sometimes more in high season or in a nicer villa. Rent is the biggest swing, with a modest studio or shared jungle house at the low end and a stylish one-bedroom near the beach at the top. Imported goods, restaurant meals and beach-club days add up quickly, so budget accordingly if you want the full lifestyle.

  • Rent: roughly $650 to $1,400 for a studio or one-bedroom, higher near the beach.
  • Coworking day pass: around $12 to $20; monthly memberships bring the rate down.
  • A casual soda (local eatery) meal: $7 to $12; a beach-club dinner: $20 to $40.
  • Groceries: pricier than mainland Costa Rica because everything trucks in over the rough road.

Where to Work in Santa Teresa

Santa Teresa has grown a genuine remote-work scene, not just a couple of cafes with wifi. You will find a handful of dedicated coworking spaces plus plenty of laptop-friendly cafes strung along the main road, and a real, welcoming nomad community that makes it easy to find collaborators, surf partners and friends. Wifi is decent by beach-town standards and fine for most calls and everyday work, but it can be variable, especially in the wet season or during a power blip, so treat a strong connection as a bonus rather than a guarantee. Bring a backup local SIM with a solid data plan so you are never fully offline before an important call.

  • Use coworking spaces for reliable air-conditioned focus and fast, stable wifi.
  • Cafes work well for lighter tasks and a change of scene.
  • Carry a Kolbi or Claro SIM as a hotspot backup for critical meetings.
  • Schedule big video calls for mornings, when connections tend to be most stable.

Getting Around Santa Teresa

Santa Teresa is essentially one long, partly unpaved coastal road, and life happens along it. Most people get around on foot, by bicycle, or by scooter and ATV, which are the true local transport and easy to rent by the day or month. The road is famously dusty in the dry season and muddy in the wet, so expect a workout for your laundry either way. For heavier loads or wet nights, 4x4 taxis are plentiful. Getting here from San Jose is a half-day journey in either direction: either drive and take the ferry from Puntarenas, or fly the short hop to Tambor airstrip and transfer in by road.

  • Rent an ATV or scooter for the most freedom; helmets and caution are essential on the dusty road.
  • 4x4 taxis are reliable but add up over a month.
  • Ferry from Puntarenas plus a drive is the classic route from San Jose.
  • Flying into Tambor saves time but costs more.

Visas for Santa Teresa

Most nomads arrive on Costa Rica's standard tourist entry, commonly granted for up to 90 days for many nationalities, which is enough for a long surf-and-work season. Plenty of longer-term visitors do border runs to reset that window, though this is informal and increasingly scrutinized, so do not treat it as a guaranteed strategy. For a more stable base, Costa Rica offers a dedicated digital-nomad (rentista-style) visa aimed at remote workers who meet a set monthly-income threshold, which can grant a longer legal stay and some practical perks. Rules, income requirements and processing change over time, so this is editorial context only and not legal advice. Confirm the current requirements with official Costa Rican sources or an immigration professional before you commit.

Best Time to Visit Santa Teresa

The dry season, roughly December to April, is the classic window and the busiest: reliably sunny days, gorgeous sunsets, prime beach time and consistent, well-groomed surf. It is also when prices peak and the town fills up, so book accommodation early. The green or wet season, running May to November, brings lush jungle, quieter beaches, noticeably lower prices and its own kind of magic, with mornings often clear and rain arriving in warm afternoon bursts. Surf stays fun year-round. If you want guaranteed sun and social energy, come in the dry months; if you prefer calm, value and a moody green landscape, the wet season rewards you.

  • December to April: sunniest, busiest, priciest, prime surf and beach.
  • May to November: lush, quieter, cheaper, with afternoon rain.
  • Shoulder months like May and November often balance value and decent weather.

Pros and Cons of Santa Teresa

Santa Teresa delivers a rare combination: world-class surf, a deep yoga-and-wellness culture, stunning sunsets, a healthy food scene and a real, growing nomad community, all in a relaxed jungle-meets-beach setting near the Cabo Blanco reserve and Montezuma. The trade-offs are real too. It is not cheap, the access road is rough and dusty, wifi can be variable, and it gets genuinely busy in high season. This is a place you choose for lifestyle first and convenience second. If the surf, community and scenery matter more to you than low costs and smooth logistics, the pros comfortably outweigh the cons.

  • Pros: excellent surf, strong wellness and nomad community, beautiful beaches and sunsets, good food and English.
  • Cons: high cost for Costa Rica, rough dusty road, variable wifi, busy and pricier in high season.

Who Santa Teresa Is For

Santa Teresa suits the active surf-and-wellness nomad who can afford it and values lifestyle over budget. If your ideal day mixes a dawn surf, a yoga class, focused work from a good coworking space and a sunset beer on the sand, you will feel right at home here, and the community will fold you in fast. It is a weaker fit for penny-pinching travelers, anyone who needs flawless infrastructure and rock-solid wifi for high-stakes work, or those who dislike dust, heat and rough roads. Come with a healthy budget, a taste for the ocean and a flexible attitude toward logistics, and Santa Teresa can be one of the most rewarding bases in Central America.
