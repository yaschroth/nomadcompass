Madrid is not the bargain it was five years ago. Rents have climbed hard since 2022, and central one-bedroom flats now sit near the top of the Spanish market. Here is a realistic monthly budget for one person (USD figures use roughly 1.08 to the euro, so treat them as approximate).

  • Rent, one-bedroom in a central district (Centro, Salamanca, Chamberi, Malasana): $1,350 to $1,800 ($1,300 to $1,730). Furnished flats add roughly $110 to $230.
  • Rent, one-bedroom in a cheaper outer district (Usera, Vallecas, Villaverde, Carabanchel): $910 to $1,250 ($865 to $1,190).
  • Groceries: $230 to $340 ($215 to $325) if you cook most meals. Shopping at Mercadona or Lidl keeps you near the lower end.
  • Eating out: the weekday menu del dia (a fixed lunch with starter, main, dessert or coffee, and a drink) runs $14 to $20 ($13 to $19). A tapas dinner with drinks lands around $17 to $28 per person.
  • Coworking hot desk: $110 to $280 ($108 to $270) per month, or roughly $17 to $28 for a day pass.
  • Local transport: the monthly Zone A pass (abono) is $37 ($35), held flat by regional subsidies through 2026.

A single remote worker renting centrally should budget around $2,150 to $2,950 ($2,050 to $2,810) all in. Living in an outer district and cooking more can pull that under $1,700 ($1,620). Sharing a flat, which is very common, changes the math entirely.

Where to Work

Madrid has a deep coworking scene. Established names include Utopicus, Impact Hub, Spaces, WeWork, Talent Garden, and The Shed, spread across the center and business districts. For true short-stay flexibility, Nomad Coworking offers plans from a few days upward with 24/7 access on monthly memberships. Most spaces bundle fast WiFi, meeting rooms, printers, and free coffee.

Cafe-work culture exists but is softer than in some nomad hubs. Spanish cafe owners generally do not mind you nursing a coffee for an hour or two, though camping out all afternoon with a laptop is less welcome in busy central spots. WiFi in cafes is common but uneven, so keep a phone hotspot as backup. Home internet is genuinely strong: Spain has some of the best fiber coverage in Europe, and most flats come with fast, reliable connections.

Getting Around

The Madrid Metro is extensive, clean, and one of the easiest big-city systems in Europe to use. Single rides start around $1.50, and the $37 monthly abono covers Metro and buses across Zone A. If you are 26 or under, the Abono Joven costs about $11 per month for the entire region, airport included, which is one of the best transport deals in Europe.

Central Madrid is very walkable. Neighborhoods like Centro, Malasana, Chueca, and La Latina flow into each other on foot, and you can cross much of the core in half an hour. You will rarely need a car.

Getting to and from Adolfo Suarez Madrid-Barajas (MAD) is simple. Metro Line 8 connects the airport (stations at T1-T2-T3 and T4) to the city in roughly 15 to 30 minutes. There is a $3.50 airport supplement on single Metro tickets, but it is waived if you use a 30-day abono, an annual pass, or a tourist ticket. Airport buses and taxis are also available, with taxis running a fixed flat fare to and from the center.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

If you hold a US, UK, Canadian, Australian, or similar passport, you can enter Spain visa-free as a tourist under the Schengen 90/180 rule: up to 90 days within any rolling 180-day period across the whole Schengen Area. That cap covers time spent in other Schengen countries too, not just Spain.

To stay longer, Spain offers a digital nomad visa for non-EU citizens who work remotely for companies or clients outside Spain. As of 2026 the income threshold is around $3,250 net per month (roughly $38,700 per year) for a single applicant, with more required for accompanying family. You also need private health insurance valid in Spain. Applying from a consulate abroad typically grants up to one year, while applying from inside Spain can grant a residence authorization valid for up to three years, both renewable if you keep qualifying. EU and EEA citizens do not need any of this and can live and work in Spain freely.

Immigration rules and income figures change, so confirm the current requirements with an official source such as Spain's consulate or the Ministry of Foreign Affairs before you commit.

Best Time to Visit

Madrid has a real continental climate: hot, dry summers and genuinely cold winters. July and August regularly push past 35 C (95 F), the city empties out as locals leave, and midday becomes hard to work through without air conditioning. Winter nights drop near freezing, though days are often crisp and sunny.

The best windows for remote work are spring (April to June) and autumn (September to early November), when temperatures are mild, terraces are pleasant, and the city is fully alive. If you can only come in summer, make sure your flat and coworking space have solid air conditioning.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: excellent, cheap public transport and a highly walkable center.
  • Pro: fast, reliable internet across the city and a mature coworking scene.
  • Pro: outstanding food, nightlife, and a clear path to legal long-term residency via the nomad visa.
  • Pro: safe, well connected to the rest of Europe, and welcoming to newcomers.
  • Con: rents have risen sharply and central flats are no longer cheap by Spanish standards.
  • Con: brutal summer heat and a genuinely cold winter make timing matter.
  • Con: the late local schedule (dinner at 9pm or later) can clash with early-morning client calls.
  • Con: less English than in some hubs, so a little Spanish goes a long way.

Who Madrid Is Best For

Madrid suits remote workers who want a real European capital rather than a beach town: big-city energy, deep culture, world-class food, and easy travel across Spain and Europe. It works well if you value walkability, nightlife, and a large expat and nomad community, and if your income comfortably clears the visa thresholds should you want to stay.

You should probably skip it if your budget is tight and you were expecting the low rents of a few years ago, if you can only visit in peak summer and hate heat, or if you want a quiet, slow, small-town pace. In those cases a smaller Spanish city like Valencia or Granada may fit better.

Frequently Asked Questions About Madrid

How much does it cost to live in Madrid as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $3,200 a month in Madrid for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Madrid?

Madrid rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Madrid safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Madrid is 7/10, good for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Madrid a good city for digital nomads overall?

Madrid stands out for nightlife and culture among remote workers. With a typical budget near $3,200 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
