What Florence Offers Digital Nomads

Experience renaissance masterpieces, Tuscan hills, and artisan gelato. A mediterranean climate and strong culture make Florence an easy base, and at about $2,800 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep cost in mind.

Florence sits at the expensive end of Italian cities for remote workers, largely because its compact historic center is one of the most visited urban areas in Europe. Budget honestly and you can make it work; go in unprepared and the tourist-zone pricing will catch you off guard.

  • Rent, central (Santa Croce, Duomo, San Giovanni): $1,250-$1,700/month (~$1,200-1,650) for a one-bedroom
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Rifredi, Campo di Marte, Gavinana): $850-$1,100/month (~$820-1,050) for a one-bedroom
  • Groceries: $230-$340/month (~$220-330) shopping at Esselunga or Lidl
  • Eating out: $14-$20 for a trattoria lunch, $28-$51 for dinner; budget $280-$460/month if you eat out regularly
  • Coworking hot desk: $230-$400/month (~$220-385); day passes run $17-$28
  • Local transport (bus/tram): $40-$46/month for a monthly pass; the city center is walkable so many nomads skip it entirely
  • Total realistic range: $2,050-$2,950/month (~$2,000-2,860) depending on how central you live and how often you eat out

A local on a local salary lives on roughly $1,500/month. As a remote worker paying market rent and eating at restaurants a few times a week, plan for the higher end of the range above.

Where to Work

Florence has a handful of solid coworking options and a thriving specialty coffee scene, though cafe culture here leans more toward standing at the bar than camping with a laptop for hours.

Coworking Spaces

Nana Bianca (near the train station) is the most active community space, mixing startups with remote workers and running regular events. Impact Hub Firenze in the Rifredi neighborhood suits heads-down work, with dedicated desks and fiber internet. Ruby Dante Workspaces near Piazza della Liberta offers polished shared offices from around $360/month with flexible day-pass options from $23. The Social Hub Florence (formerly The Student Hotel) combines a cafe, coworking, and event space near the river and works well for shorter stays.

Cafe Work Culture

The Italian bar tradition means most locals drink their espresso standing and leave in five minutes. That said, Ditta Artigianale, a specialty micro-roastery with several locations, is explicitly laptop-friendly with long tables, fast WiFi, and no pressure on time. La Menagere in the center also has generous space. Avoid tourist-facing cafes around the Duomo for work; they are crowded, expensive, and the WiFi is patchy.

WiFi Reality

Coworking spaces have reliable fiber connections. Many apartment buildings still run on ADSL rather than fiber, so check before signing a lease. For backup, a local SIM (TIM or Iliad) with a data plan runs around $11-$17/month and covers most of the city well.

Getting Around

Florence's historic center is compact enough to walk almost everywhere you need to go day-to-day. Most attractions, coworking spaces, and restaurants within the walls are 20-30 minutes on foot from each other.

The city's bus network (ATAF/Autolinee Toscane) covers the wider city reasonably well, and a monthly pass costs around $40-$46. The T1, T2, and T3 tram lines connect key neighborhoods and run frequently. Cycling is popular but cobblestones make it harder than it looks; rental e-bikes exist but are mainly tourist-focused.

Airport: Florence's Amerigo Vespucci Airport (FLR) is about 5 km from the center. The T2 tram runs directly from the airport to the main train station (Santa Maria Novella) every 5-10 minutes, costs $1.50, and takes roughly 20 minutes. Taxis from the airport charge a fixed $25 for the historic center. Pisa airport (PSA, 80 km away) serves more budget carriers and connects via a direct train to Florence SMN.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

As part of the Schengen Area, Italy allows most Western passport holders (US, UK, Canada, Australia) to stay visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. This covers a short-term stay but not long-term remote work.

Italy introduced a formal Digital Nomad Visa (Visto per Nomadi Digitali) that allows non-EU remote workers to live in Italy for 12 months, renewable. Key requirements include a minimum annual income of around $31,900, proof of remote employment or freelance clients based outside Italy, a university degree or professional qualification, health insurance, and at least six months of remote work experience. Processing typically takes one to three months and must be done at an Italian consulate in your home country before arrival. The application fee is $130.

EU and EEA citizens can live and work in Italy without restrictions. Always verify current requirements with the official Italian consulate or Ministry of Foreign Affairs website before applying.

Best Time to Visit

For remote work, the sweet spots are March through May and September through November. Spring brings mild temperatures (15-22C), manageable crowds, and long evenings. Autumn is arguably the best window: cooler air, harvest produce, and the city returns to locals after the summer exodus.

Summer (June through August) is genuinely uncomfortable for work. Temperatures regularly hit 35C or above, humidity is high, and the historic center fills with tour groups. Many local shops and restaurants outside the tourist belt close for two or more weeks in August as Florentines head to the coast.

Winter (December through February) is the least crowded and cheapest time to visit. Temperatures drop to 3-10C, it rains regularly, and some smaller venues reduce hours. If you prioritize focus, low costs, and an authentic local pace, winter works well and the city is genuinely beautiful in the quiet months.

Pros and Cons

  • Exceptional cultural environment: world-class museums, architecture, and food within walking distance of any apartment
  • Compact and walkable with no need for a car in day-to-day life
  • Strong specialty coffee and food scene for daily quality of life
  • Good flight connections via both FLR and PSA airports
  • Mild seasons in spring and autumn that are genuinely pleasant for outdoor work breaks
  • Established expat and student community making it easier to meet English speakers
  • Expensive relative to other Italian cities, especially rent in central areas
  • Heavy tourist congestion from April to October; some streets feel like theme parks
  • Brutal summer heat with limited air conditioning in older buildings
  • Pickpocketing is a real risk on buses and in crowded markets; Florence ranks high in Italy for petty theft
  • Nightlife is limited compared to Rome or Milan; the city quiets down early
  • Integrating socially with locals takes time; the community can feel inward-facing outside of international student circles

Who Florence Is Best For

Florence rewards people who want to live inside one of the great art cities of the world and are willing to pay for it. If your idea of a productive day involves a morning espresso at a century-old bar, focused work in a good coworking space, a long trattoria lunch, and an evening walk past the Arno, Florence can feel almost unreasonably good.

It suits designers, writers, and creative professionals who draw energy from visual and cultural immersion. History lovers, architecture enthusiasts, and food-focused nomads will find the everyday environment deeply satisfying. It also works well for people who want a base to explore Tuscany, given the day-trip options to Siena, Lucca, and Chianti.

Skip Florence if you need a buzzing nightlife scene, a large active nomad meetup community, or a tight budget. It is not the place if you want to grind in a neutral, distraction-free environment; the city constantly competes for your attention. Nomads who do best here are those who treat that competition as the point.

Frequently Asked Questions About Florence

How much does it cost to live in Florence as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Florence is around $2,800 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Florence?

With a WiFi score of 6/10, connectivity in Florence is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Florence safe for digital nomads?

Florence scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Florence a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Florence is worth a look if you value culture and food. Costs sit near $2,800 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
