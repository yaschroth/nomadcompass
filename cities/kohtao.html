Setting Up as a Nomad in Koh Tao

The world's busiest dive-certification island, small enough to circle by scooter and stacked with beach cafes that tolerate all-day laptops. Koh Tao pairs nature with climate, all for about $1,000 a month. If anything, the culture scene lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Koh Tao

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Sairee Beach

$$$
Beachfront & Social

The island's main strip of bars, dive shops and cafes

A long sandy beach on the west coast backed by one narrow walking lane packed with dive schools, smoothie bars, laundries and beach clubs. Most first-time visitors and short-stay divers base here. Several cafes with reliable fibre make it the default daytime work spot on the island.

Best for: Nomads who want people to talk to, dive courses on the doorstep and no need for a scooter.
Pros
  • Everything walkable without a scooter
  • Best choice of laptop-friendly cafes
  • Easy to meet other travellers within a day
Cons
  • Bass from beach bars runs late most nights
  • The busiest and priciest stretch of the island
  • Beach lane floods and gets muddy in heavy rain

Mae Haad

$$
Practical & Central

The pier village where the ferries and the ATMs are

The island's practical hub, built around the ferry pier. Streets hold the banks, pharmacies, hardware shops, the 7-Elevens and the cheapest local Thai kitchens. Long-stay residents choose it for convenience rather than looks, and it is a ten minute walk south along the coast path to Sairee.

Best for: Longer-stay workers who value banks, groceries and ferry access over a sea view.
Pros
  • Best supply of everyday shops and services
  • Cheapest local food on the island
  • Quick ferry access for visa runs
Cons
  • Traffic and ferry crowds around arrival times
  • Very little actual swimming beach
  • Feels more functional than scenic

Chalok Baan Kao

$$
Quiet & Residential

A calmer bay village in the south of the island

A horseshoe bay at the southern end with a handful of restaurants, two dive centres and mostly low-rise bungalows on the hillsides above. It is quiet by 10pm and popular with people staying a month or more. Monthly bungalows here run cheaper than anything near Sairee.

Best for: Nomads on a monthly stay who want sleep, space and lower rent.
Pros
  • Noticeably cheaper monthly rentals
  • Quiet enough for early calls
  • Still has cafes, a gym and dive shops
Cons
  • A scooter is basically required
  • The bay is shallow and rocky at low tide
  • Slower nightlife and fewer people around

Tanote Bay

$$$
Secluded & Scenic

An east-coast snorkelling cove with a handful of resorts

A small granite-boulder bay on the east side reached by a steep road across the island's middle. There are only a few resorts, one or two restaurants and clear water straight off the sand. Internet is resort-dependent, so it suits people whose work is mostly offline or async.

Best for: Solo workers who want near total quiet and swim-out snorkelling.
Pros
  • Some of the clearest water on the island
  • Almost no crowds outside day trips
  • Genuinely dark, quiet nights
Cons
  • Steep access road is dangerous on a scooter in rain
  • Very limited food and shop choice
  • Wi-Fi quality varies a lot by resort

Hin Wong Bay

$$
Remote & Rustic

The island's most isolated liveable bay

A rocky northeast bay with a couple of small resorts, a dive site offshore and effectively nothing else. The dirt access track is rough and there are no shops, so residents ride into Mae Haad for supplies. Best treated as a retreat rather than a base with everyday errands.

Best for: Writers and deep-work types who want to disappear for a few weeks.
Pros
  • Extremely quiet, close to zero nightlife
  • Excellent shore snorkelling and diving
  • Lowest-key stretch of the island
Cons
  • Access track is rough and often impassable in rain
  • No shops, restaurants limited to resorts
  • Patchy internet and mobile signal

Cost of Living in Koh Tao

About $1000 a month is realistic on Koh Tao if the accommodation is monthly rather than nightly. A furnished studio or small bungalow with air conditioning in Mae Haad, Sairee or Chalok Baan Kao rents for $250 to $450 monthly off-season, climbing to $500 or more from December to March; long-stay negotiation is standard. Thai food at local shops costs $2 to $4 a plate, while Western restaurants on Sairee run $8 to $15, so a mixed diet lands at $300 to $400. An AIS or True tourist SIM with generous data is $10 to $18 a month. Formal coworking day passes are around $8 to $12, or roughly $90 to $150 monthly where offered. Electricity is the sting: island rates and constant air conditioning produce $50 to $120 bills. Scooter hire is $5 to $8 daily. Verdict: excellent value if diving is the point, otherwise mainland Thailand is cheaper.

Where to Work in Koh Tao

Base yourself in Mae Haad if ferries, banks and the widest cafe choice matter, Sairee for the social and dive-school density, or Chalok Baan Kao for quiet and better sleep. Reliable laptop-friendly cafes cluster in Mae Haad and along the back road behind Sairee: Zest Coffee Lounge and Cafe del Sol are long-standing fixtures, alongside a rotating set of air-conditioned specialty roasters that come and go each season, so it pays to check current openings rather than trust old lists. Dedicated coworking has never taken firm root here; most remote workers rely on cafes plus a good room connection. Fibre reached the island years ago and 50 Mbps to 200 Mbps is common, but island bandwidth degrades in peak season, power cuts happen during storms, and the November to December monsoon can sever service for hours. A 5G phone plan as backup is close to essential.

Getting Around Koh Tao

Koh Tao has no airport and no public bus network. Arrival is by ferry into Mae Haad pier: Lomprayah and Seatran catamarans from Chumphon on the mainland cost roughly $18 to $28 and take under two hours, while services from Koh Phangan and Koh Samui run $12 to $22. Combined bus-boat or flight-boat tickets from Bangkok, Surat Thani or Samui airport are sold everywhere. On the island, songthaew pickups charge about $3 per person on shared routes and $8 to $15 for private hires at night, which adds up fast. Sairee and Mae Haad are walkable, but the road between beaches is steep and unlit. A scooter at $5 to $8 a day is effectively essential for anything beyond one beach, though the hills, gravel and rental damage scams make it the single biggest risk on the island. Helmets and photographic evidence at pickup are non-negotiable.

Visas for Koh Tao

Most Western passport holders enter Thailand under the visa exemption scheme, which since mid-2024 grants 60 days on arrival, extendable once at an immigration office for a further 30 days at a fee of 1900 baht, roughly $55. The nearest immigration office for Koh Tao is on Koh Samui, so extensions require a ferry trip. Since 2024 Thailand has offered the Destination Thailand Visa, or DTV, a five-year multiple-entry visa aimed squarely at remote workers and long-stay soft-power visitors such as divers and Muay Thai students. It permits 180 days per entry, extendable once inside Thailand by another 180 days, costs around 10,000 baht or about $290 and requires proof of roughly 500,000 baht, near $14,000, in savings plus supporting documents such as a remote employment contract. Thailand also requires a digital arrival card. Overstaying carries fines and blacklisting, so it is not worth risking.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit Koh Tao

Koh Tao sits in the Gulf of Thailand and runs on a different calendar from Phuket or Chiang Mai. February to April is the prime window: calm seas, visibility often above 20 metres, hot days near 33C and reliable ferries. May to August stays good, with short afternoon showers, whale shark sightings peaking around March to May and again in September, and fewer crowds. September and October bring increasing rain and choppier crossings. The worst window is late October through December, when the northeast monsoon delivers heavy sustained rain, rough seas, occasional cancelled ferries and power interruptions; some businesses close for a few weeks. January is a transitional month, improving steadily. Festivals worth timing include Songkran in mid-April, Loy Krathong in November, the Koh Tao Festival and the neighbouring Koh Phangan Full Moon parties, which draw the island's crowds every lunar month.

Pros and Cons of Koh Tao

Koh Tao's strengths are specific and strong: it is one of the cheapest and best places on earth to learn or teach scuba diving, the water is warm year round, the community of divers, instructors and long-stay remote workers is genuinely tight, monthly rents are low, Thai food is excellent and inexpensive, and the island is small enough to feel like a village within a week. Free diving, bouldering and hiking add variety. The drawbacks accumulate, though: the island is tiny and can feel claustrophobic after two months, medical facilities are basic with serious cases evacuated to Koh Samui or Bangkok, scooter accidents are common and roads are steep and poorly surfaced, rental scams and inflated damage claims are a recurring complaint, waste management and water quality lag behind visitor numbers, electricity is expensive, the monsoon can strand people, and formal coworking infrastructure remains thin.

Who Koh Tao Is For

Koh Tao fits the diver first and the nomad second: instructors, divemaster trainees, underwater photographers and remote workers who want to be in the water every morning before a European-facing afternoon of work. It also suits low-budget solo travellers who value a small, friendly, easy-to-join community, freelancers on the Destination Thailand Visa planning a long soft-power stay, and anyone who prefers a village rhythm to a city. Skip it if the work needs enterprise-grade uptime, if a large formal coworking scene with events and networking matters, or if family-sized apartments, hospitals and international schools are requirements. It is also a poor fit for anyone who dislikes scooters, gets restless on small islands, or needs a varied nightlife and restaurant scene beyond beach bars. Those seeking cheaper city living with the same visa should compare Chiang Mai.
