Setting Up as a Nomad in Tokyo

Ultra-modern efficiency meets ancient tradition in the world's largest city. A humid subtropical climate and strong safety make Tokyo an easy base, and at about $1,920 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep cost in mind.

Tokyo has a reputation for being punishingly expensive, but the weak yen has quietly changed the math. With the rate sitting near 155 yen to the dollar (and at times weaker), your foreign income stretches further than it did a decade ago. The city is not cheap, but for many remote workers it now costs less than a mid-tier European capital. A realistic monthly breakdown for one person:

  • Rent, central wards (Shibuya, Minato, Shinjuku): a small studio (1R or 1K) runs $820 to $1,070. Furnished short-term or serviced apartments cost noticeably more.
  • Rent, cheaper wards (Adachi, Nerima, Edogawa, Katsushika) or a 10 minute walk from a station: $440 to $630 for a comparable studio.
  • Groceries: $220 to $345 if you cook at home. Supermarkets and konbini are everywhere.
  • Eating out: cheap and excellent. A ramen bowl or teishoku set is $5 to $7.50, a konbini lunch under $4.50. Budget $190 to $380 monthly depending on how often you cook.
  • Coworking hot desk: $95 to $125 for a monthly flex membership, or roughly $19 for a day pass.
  • Local transport: $50 to $76 using a Suica for daily trips.

All in, a frugal setup in an outer ward lands around $1,140 to $1,390 per month. A comfortable central lifestyle runs $1,770 to $2,210. Utilities, a phone plan, and health cover sit on top of that.

Where to Work

Tokyo has a deep coworking scene. WeWork operates a dozen-plus locations, with polished spaces above Shibuya's Center Gai and across Roppongi and Marunouchi. Smaller independents are worth seeking out: .andwork Shibuya draws an international creative crowd, CASE Shinjuku near Takadanobaba is affordable and unpretentious, and DIAGONAL RUN TOKYO caters to visiting professionals. Most hot desks include lockers, free coffee, and member events.

Cafe-work culture is real but has unwritten rules. Chains like Starbucks, Doutor, and Komeda are laptop-friendly, but at small independent cafes camping for hours is frowned upon, so read the room. On connectivity: Tokyo's mobile and fixed internet is fast and dependable. Free public WiFi (station networks, Starbucks, konbini) is patchy and often needs a clunky sign-in, so most nomads run an eSIM or a pocket WiFi device for a reliable always-on connection.

Getting Around

Tokyo's rail network is the best on earth, and you will rely on it daily. Get a Suica or Pasmo IC card (regular plastic Suica sales resumed in 2025, or add one to your phone) and tap through every gate. The two cards are interchangeable and work on JR lines, the Tokyo Metro, Toei subway, buses, and private railways. The JR Yamanote Line loop connects most major hubs, and individual rides typically cost $1 to $2. The city is genuinely walkable within neighborhoods, and remarkably safe on foot at any hour.

Tokyo has two airports. Haneda (HND) is close in and easy: 30 to 45 minutes to central Tokyo by monorail or Keikyu line. Narita (NRT) is far out; take the Keisei Skyliner (around $16, roughly 45 minutes to Ueno) or the Narita Express. If you can choose your flights, Haneda saves you real time and money.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Citizens of the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and most EU countries get 90 days visa-free as a temporary visitor, with no advance application. That covers a lot of remote workers, though technically you should not be doing local income-earning work on this status. In 2024 Japan launched a digital nomad visa under "Designated Activities," valid for up to six months for remote workers employed by companies outside Japan. It requires proof of about $63,080 in annual income plus private health insurance covering up to $63,080 in treatment. It cannot be renewed or extended: you must leave and spend six months outside Japan before reapplying. Stacking back-to-back visa-free entries to live here long-term is risky, as immigration officers can refuse entry. Always confirm current rules with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs or your nearest Japanese embassy before you travel.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit

Tokyo has four distinct seasons and they matter. Summer (June to September) is hot and brutally humid, with a rainy stretch in June and typhoon risk into autumn. Spring (late March to April) brings cherry blossom and mild, pleasant weather, but it is peak tourist season with higher prices. Autumn (October to November) is arguably the best window for remote work: comfortable temperatures, low humidity, clear skies, and fewer crowds. Winter is cold but dry and sunny, and it is the cheapest and quietest time to settle in.

Pros and Cons

  • Pros: world-class public transport, extremely safe, superb and affordable food, fast reliable internet, deep coworking options, and a weak yen that makes it cheaper than expected.
  • Pros: clean, efficient, endlessly interesting, with excellent healthcare and near-zero petty crime.
  • Cons: a real language barrier outside tourist zones, apartments that are genuinely tiny by Western standards, and a stubborn cash culture where some places still refuse cards.
  • Cons: finding a long-term apartment as a foreigner is bureaucratic, summers are oppressive, and central rents remain high.

Who Tokyo Is Best For

Tokyo suits remote workers who value structure, safety, and quality over a party-hostel social scene: people who want a productive base, great food, and a city that simply works. It rewards the curious, and it fits solo nomads and couples on stable, foreign-currency income.

Skip it if you need a low monthly budget, crave a tight built-in nomad community, or want somewhere you can coast on English alone. Those chasing rock-bottom costs or an instant social circle will find Southeast Asia an easier landing.

Frequently Asked Questions About Tokyo

How much does it cost to live in Tokyo as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Tokyo is around $1,920 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Tokyo?

With a WiFi score of 9/10, connectivity in Tokyo is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Tokyo safe for digital nomads?

Tokyo scores 10 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Tokyo a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Tokyo is worth a look if you value safety and food. Costs sit near $1,920 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
