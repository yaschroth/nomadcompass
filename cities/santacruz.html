Living in Santa Cruz as a Digital Nomad

Bolivia's economic engine with tropical lowland vibes awaits. What pulls nomads here is low costs, backed by easy visas and climate. Budget around $1,200 a month. The language barrier is the weak spot.

Cost of Living in Santa Cruz

Santa Cruz is one of South America's more affordable mid-size cities, though prices have crept up as the expat and nomad community has grown. Here is a realistic monthly breakdown for a solo remote worker in 2025-2026.

  • Rent, central neighborhoods (Equipetrol, Casco Viejo): BOB 2,800-4,800 (~$400-690 USD) for a furnished one-bedroom
  • Rent, outer or residential areas (Plan 3000, Barrio Urbarí): BOB 1,400-2,400 (~$200-345 USD) for similar space, less finished
  • Groceries (cooking most meals): BOB 700-1,200 (~$100-175 USD)
  • Eating out (mix of local spots and mid-range restaurants): BOB 700-1,750 (~$100-250 USD); street empanadas run BOB 3-5, a sit-down meal BOB 50-100, and a proper restaurant BOB 150-200 with drinks
  • Coworking hot-desk: BOB 700-1,200 (~$100-175 USD) per month at most spaces
  • Local transport (app taxis, trufis): BOB 200-500 (~$30-70 USD) depending on how much you rely on InDriver versus shared trufis
  • Internet and utilities: BOB 350-560 (~$50-80 USD) combined

Total realistic range: $600-1,100 USD per month for a comfortable nomad lifestyle, or $500-700 if you go fully local. The currency is the boliviano (BOB); roughly 7 BOB to 1 USD at mid-2025 exchange rates.

Where to Work

Santa Cruz has a small but functional coworking scene concentrated in the Equipetrol business district. Hub40 (Manzana40 Plaza Empresarial, Equipetrol) is the most established, running on floors 9-10 of a tower with 200+ Mbps fiber and a large community of local startups. MAKE Cowork, also in Equipetrol, is more intimate and known for its friendly atmosphere and fast connections. Central43 in Barrio Urbarí is a quieter option at a lower price point, useful if you want fewer distractions. GoWork, another Equipetrol space, gets positive marks for energy and decor.

Cafe work is viable but patchy. Typica Cafe is widely cited as the best place to open a laptop, with good espresso and reliable WiFi. Alto Tostado has a dedicated cowork nook upstairs and outdoor seating. Avoid Starbucks-equivalent chains for serious work; they get loud and the WiFi degrades fast.

The broader WiFi situation is honest: fiber connections in coworking spaces and newer apartments are solid (50-200 Mbps is common), but public WiFi in malls and cafes can be inconsistent. Buy a local SIM from Tigo or Entel for 4G backup; data is cheap at around BOB 50-80 for a monthly plan with several gigabytes.

Getting Around

Santa Cruz is built in concentric rings radiating from the Casco Viejo (historic center). The city is not walkable at scale: the rings expand far, and the heat for most of the year makes walking impractical for anything beyond a few blocks. Most nomads rely on a combination of InDriver (the dominant rideshare app, where you name your price), standard app-booked taxis, and trufis (shared minibuses that follow fixed ring routes). Trufis cost around BOB 2-3 per ride but are cramped and pickpocketing does happen; keep your phone out of sight.

Taxis and InDriver rides within the central area typically run BOB 20-40. Uber also operates in Santa Cruz. Renting a bicycle is an option in the dry season; a few bike lanes exist around the inner rings.

Viru Viru International Airport sits about 15 km north of the center. An InDriver ride costs roughly BOB 60-100 (around $9-15 USD) and takes 20-40 minutes depending on traffic. There is no rail link. If you fly into Bogota, Lima, or Sao Paulo, direct connections to Viru Viru are easy to find.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

As of December 2025, US passport holders no longer need a tourist visa and can enter Bolivia visa-free for up to 90 days. EU, UK, Canadian, Australian, and New Zealand passport holders have also been in the visa-free category for years, also receiving 90 days on entry.

One requirement applies to everyone: you must complete the free SIGEMIG online pre-registration before flying in. It takes about 10 minutes and is mandatory at immigration.

You can extend your stay once at a SIGEMIG office in Santa Cruz, La Paz, or Cochabamba, bringing your passport, entry stamp, and approximately BOB 250-300 ($35-45 USD). This grants another 90 days, for a maximum of 180 days per calendar year. Bolivia does not currently offer a dedicated digital nomad or remote worker visa; the tourist stay is the only option for most visitors. Always confirm current entry rules with official sources before traveling, as policies can change.

Best Time to Visit

Santa Cruz has a tropical lowland climate with two distinct seasons. The dry season runs from May through October: temperatures sit between 22 and 32 degrees Celsius (72-90 F), humidity drops, and days are sunny. June and July are the coolest months and the most comfortable for being outside or staying focused at a desk without fighting sweat and stickiness. This is the clearest window for remote work.

The wet season from November through April brings daily afternoon thunderstorms, heavy rain bursts, and humidity that makes sitting in a non-air-conditioned space difficult. Mornings are usually fine and the city functions normally, but the heat and wet combination during January and February is exhausting. If you visit in the wet season, budget for an apartment or coworking space with air conditioning, which is common in central areas.

Be aware that during July, a cold front called the surazo can blow in from Argentina, dropping temperatures sharply for a few days. It passes quickly but the city grinds to a partial halt.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Very low cost of living compared to most Latin American nomad hubs
  • Warm, social local culture; Santa Cruz has a reputation for being one of Bolivia's more outward-facing, business-minded cities
  • Good food scene by regional standards, with a mix of traditional Bolivian cuisine and international options
  • Faster growth in coworking infrastructure than La Paz
  • No altitude sickness: Santa Cruz sits at only 416 m (1,365 ft), making it an easy adjustment from sea level
  • Direct flights to most major South American hubs

Cons

  • Small, nascent nomad community; English is rarely spoken outside hotels and some coworking spaces
  • Heat and humidity from November through April can be draining
  • Walkability is poor; you will be taxi-dependent
  • Safety requires standard precautions: petty theft in markets and trufis, with some areas of Plan 3000 best avoided at night
  • No digital nomad visa; the 90-day tourist allowance is the ceiling without a restart
  • Banking and international payments can be cumbersome; bring cash or rely on ATMs with fees

Who Santa Cruz Is Best For

Santa Cruz suits nomads who want to get off the well-worn Latin America circuit without sacrificing urban comfort. If you are comfortable working in Spanish, enjoy a city with real local character over a tourist overlay, and want to keep costs well below $1,000 a month, Santa Cruz delivers. It works especially well for people who have already done Medellin, Lima, or Buenos Aires and want something less saturated. The dry season months from May through September are the sweet spot.

It is a harder sell if you depend on a strong English-speaking network, struggle with heat and humidity, or need a large expat community to feel grounded. Travelers who want mountain scenery and high-altitude culture are better served by La Paz or Sucre. And anyone who needs seamless digital banking should research Bolivian ATM and card acceptance carefully before committing to a longer stay.

Frequently Asked Questions About Santa Cruz

How much does it cost to live in Santa Cruz as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,200 a month in Santa Cruz for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Santa Cruz?

Santa Cruz rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Santa Cruz safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Santa Cruz is 5/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Santa Cruz a good city for digital nomads overall?

Santa Cruz stands out for low costs and easy visas among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,200 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
