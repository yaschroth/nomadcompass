Setting Up as a Nomad in Turin

Discover industrial elegance, coffee culture, chocolate, and Alpine views. Turin pairs food with culture and a humid subtropical climate, all for about $2,500 a month. If anything, air quality lags.

Cost of Living in Turin

Turin is one of the most affordable major cities in northern Italy, undercutting Milan and Rome while still offering a genuinely urban, culturally rich lifestyle. As of 2025-2026, a solo remote worker can live comfortably for less than you might expect.

  • Rent, central (Centro, Crocetta, Vanchiglia): €800-1,100/month (~$870-1,200 USD) for a one-bedroom apartment
  • Rent, cheaper areas (San Salvario, Barriera di Milano, Mirafiori): €600-750/month (~$650-820 USD)
  • Groceries: €200-280/month (~$220-310 USD) shopping at Esselunga or local markets
  • Eating out: A sit-down lunch at a trattoria runs €12-18; dinner €20-35 with wine
  • Coworking hot-desk: €100-200/month (~$110-220 USD) depending on the space
  • Local transport (monthly pass): €38/month (~$42 USD) covering buses, trams, and metro
  • Total realistic range: €1,400-2,200/month (~$1,530-2,400 USD) for a comfortable solo lifestyle

Food is a genuine strong point here. A glass of local Barolo at a bar costs €4-6. Aperitivo culture means many bars serve free snacks with your drink from around 6 pm, which effectively replaces dinner for a couple of euros.

Where to Work

Turin has a solid and growing coworking scene, anchored by a handful of well-established spaces. Toolbox Coworking (Via Agostino da Montefeltro) is the flagship, with over 600 members, fast symmetric WiFi around 200 Mbps, large desks, private phone booths, and a strong community of tech freelancers and makers. It covers 10,000 square metres across multiple floors. OGR Tech powered by Talent Garden (Corso Castelfidardo 22) sits inside a converted 19th-century railway workshop and is the most architecturally striking option, aimed at startups and innovators. Copernico Torino Garibaldi (Corso Valdocco 2) is the most corporate-feeling of the three, good if you need meeting rooms and a polished address.

Cafe work culture in Turin is more relaxed than in Milan but less nomad-oriented than Lisbon or Barcelona. Traditional cafes generally do not encourage laptop use, especially during peak hours. However, a wave of newer, younger spots in San Salvario and Vanchiglia are explicitly welcoming. Caffè Platti and MagazziniOz both have a reputation for tolerating long stays. WiFi quality in cafes is inconsistent, so treat coworking spaces as your primary option if you have calls or deadlines.

Getting Around

Turin's public transport network is run by GTT and covers buses, trams, and a single metro line (Line 1) running east to west through the city centre. The metro is modern, clean, and driverless. A monthly pass at €38 covers all modes and is your best investment if you stay longer than a week.

The city is highly walkable in the centre. The historic grid layout with long arcaded streets (portici) makes walking comfortable even in rain or heat. Cycling infrastructure is expanding, and bike-share schemes operate across the city.

Turin Airport (TRN, also called Caselle) is 16 km north. The easiest option is the regional train via the Turin Metropolitan Railway Service from Torino Porta Susa, taking 34 minutes and costing €3.70. Alternatively, the Arriva Italia bus connects Porta Nuova and Porta Susa stations to the airport in 45-50 minutes for €7.50. Taxis from the airport to the centre cost around €40.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Italy is a Schengen member. Citizens of the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and most other western countries can enter visa-free and stay up to 90 days within any 180-day period. There is no visa-free option beyond that for pure stays.

Italy launched a Digital Nomad Visa (Type D) in April 2024, now fully operational. It is valid for 12 months and renewable indefinitely. Requirements include proof of highly skilled remote work or freelance income, a minimum annual income of €28,000, health insurance, and a clean criminal record. The application fee is €116 and processing typically takes 1-3 months. You apply through the Italian consulate in your home country before arriving. Once in Italy with the visa, you register for a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno).

Always verify current requirements with the official Italian consulate or the Portale Immigrazione before applying, as rules can change.

Best Time to Visit

The sweet spot for remote work in Turin is April through June and September through October. Temperatures are mild (14-22°C), the city is active without being overwhelmed by tourists, and the Alps backdrop on clear days is genuinely beautiful.

July and August are hot and humid, with temperatures occasionally reaching 35°C. The city empties as locals leave for the coast or mountains, which means quieter streets and cheaper accommodation but also some businesses and cafes closing for weeks at a time. Winter (December through February) is cold and foggy, with temperatures often below 5°C and occasional snow. It is not unpleasant if you like that atmosphere, but the fog can be relentless in January. Spring thunderstorms in May are common but typically brief.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Genuinely affordable for a northern Italian city with high quality of life
  • Pro: Outstanding food and wine culture rooted in Piedmontese tradition
  • Pro: Walkable, well-connected by public transport, easy airport access
  • Pro: Less tourist-saturated than Rome, Florence, or Venice
  • Pro: Strong coworking infrastructure anchored by Toolbox and OGR Tech
  • Pro: Close to the Alps and skiing in winter, lakes and hiking in summer
  • Con: Air quality is poor in winter due to the Po Valley inversion layer
  • Con: English is limited outside the city centre and universities
  • Con: Cafe laptop culture is not as developed as in other nomad hubs
  • Con: The international nomad community is small compared to Lisbon or Berlin
  • Con: Hot and muggy in peak summer with some seasonal business closures

Who Turin Is Best For

Turin rewards remote workers who want a genuine Italian city experience without the tourist noise of Rome or Florence. If you are drawn to serious food and wine culture, Baroque architecture, access to mountains, and a lower cost of living than Milan, Turin delivers on all fronts. It is well-suited to people who can manage some Italian or are willing to learn, and who prefer depth over a packed social nomad scene.

You may want to look elsewhere if you need a large English-speaking community, constant networking events with other nomads, or warm weather year-round. It is also not the best fit if air quality is a serious health concern for you, particularly in winter months when the Po Valley smog can be persistent.

Frequently Asked Questions About Turin

How much does it cost to live in Turin as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Turin is around $2,500 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Turin?

With a WiFi score of 7/10, connectivity in Turin is good for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Turin safe for digital nomads?

Turin scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Turin a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Turin is worth a look if you value food and culture. Costs sit near $2,500 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
