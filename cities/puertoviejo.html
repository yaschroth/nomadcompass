Setting Up as a Nomad in Puerto Viejo

Caribbean Costa Rica: Afro-Caribbean food, surf breaks and jungle that reaches the sand. A tropical rainforest climate and strong nature make Puerto Viejo an easy base, and at about $1,700 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep cost in mind.

Best Neighborhoods in Puerto Viejo

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Puerto Viejo Town

$$$
Central & Sociable

The main strip, reggae bars and the bike rental shops

The village itself, a few streets between the road and the sea with the bars, restaurants, supermarkets and bicycle rentals that make the place function. It is where the nightlife is, where most of the coworking happens, and where you can live without a vehicle.

Best for: Anyone who wants the bars, the cafes and the bike shops on the doorstep
Pros
  • Everything walkable, no vehicle needed
  • The bars, live music and the social scene
  • Best connectivity and the coworking spaces
Cons
  • Loud on the strip at night
  • Highest rents in the area
  • Theft from accommodation is a known problem

Playa Cocles

$$$
Surf & Relaxed

Two kilometres south, the surf beach and quieter cabinas

The long beach immediately south of town, with the area's most consistent surf, a scattering of cabinas and small restaurants set back under the trees, and a bike path running into the village. It is calmer than the strip while keeping it within five minutes by bicycle.

Best for: Stays that want the surf beach and the town a short ride away
Pros
  • The area's best surf directly there
  • Quieter than town but connected by the bike path
  • Jungle backing onto the beach
Cons
  • Rip currents make it unsuitable for casual swimming
  • Fewer shops and restaurants
  • Beach theft is common

Punta Uva

$$$$
Secluded & Tropical

The calmest water on the coast, seven kilometres south

A pair of sheltered coves either side of a rocky point, where the reef makes the water calm and clear enough to swim and snorkel properly, backed by rainforest with sloths and howler monkeys in the trees. Accommodation is scattered houses and small lodges.

Best for: Stays that want swimmable water and rainforest rather than a village
Pros
  • The calmest, clearest water on this coast
  • Rainforest and wildlife at the door
  • Genuinely quiet
Cons
  • A bicycle or car is essential
  • Twenty minutes by bike into town
  • Very few shops and slower internet

Manzanillo

$$
Village & Wild

The end of the road, at the edge of the wildlife refuge

The last village on the coast road, thirteen kilometres south, where the Gandoca-Manzanillo refuge begins and the coastal trail runs on through rainforest to empty beaches. It is a small Afro-Caribbean fishing village with a few sodas and cabinas and nothing else at all.

Best for: Stays where the wildlife refuge matters more than any amenity
Pros
  • The refuge and its coastal trail from the village
  • Genuine Afro-Caribbean village life
  • The emptiest beaches on this coast
Cons
  • Thirty minutes to Puerto Viejo
  • Almost no services and unreliable internet
  • The bus is infrequent

Playa Negra and Cahuita Road

$$
Quiet & Affordable

North of the village, black sand and lower rents

The black-sand beach immediately north of town and the road running on towards Cahuita, with houses and cabinas set among the trees at rents noticeably below the southern beaches. It is a five-minute walk or ride into the village and considerably quieter.

Best for: Longer stays wanting lower rents within walking distance of town
Pros
  • Rents below the southern beaches
  • Five minutes on foot or by bike into the village
  • Quiet black-sand beach
Cons
  • Black sand gets very hot and is less appealing
  • Fewer restaurants nearby
  • The road to Cahuita carries steady traffic

Cost of Living in Puerto Viejo

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Puerto Viejo, so the $1,700 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 1000 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget roughly $1,700 a month as a solo remote worker in Puerto Viejo, which reflects the general truth that Costa Rica is not cheap by Central American standards. A one-bedroom place in town or in the surrounding jungle runs about $600 to $900 on a long lease, with beachfront and newer builds well above that, and short-term rates in high season considerably higher again. Groceries land around $350 to $450, with imported goods expensive and the local ferias good value for produce. A casado lunch at a soda is $6 to $9, a restaurant dinner $18 to $30, and a beer about $3. A scooter or bicycle is the standard local transport. Utilities add $80 to $150, largely fans and refrigeration.

Where to Work in Puerto Viejo

Puerto Viejo has no formal coworking space, and remote workers here work from their accommodation or from the town's cafes, several of which have grown up specifically around the surfing and long-stay crowd and are entirely comfortable with laptops. The constraint is connectivity: this is the Caribbean coast, infrastructure is thinner than in the Central Valley, and fixed-line speeds vary sharply by property. Anyone dependent on video calls should test the actual connection before committing to a lease, and should budget for a mobile data backup, as 4G coverage in town is reasonable. Power cuts happen, particularly in the wet season. Many long-stayers keep a small battery backup for the router as a matter of course.

Getting Around Puerto Viejo

Puerto Viejo is a small town and the standard way to move is a bicycle: the coastal road running south through Playa Cocles, Chiquita and Punta Uva to Manzanillo is flat, shaded and lined with the beaches most people come for, and cycling it is a large part of the appeal. Scooters are common. Buses run along the coast and north to Limón and on to San José, a journey of about four and a half hours to the capital. There is no airport nearby; San José's Juan Santamaría is the international gateway, and the domestic strip at Limón has limited service. A car helps for supermarket runs to Limón but is not necessary for daily life.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Puerto Viejo

Costa Rica grants most Western passports, including EU, UK, US, Canadian and Australian, up to 180 days visa-free on arrival, which is one of the most generous allowances anywhere and covers a long stay without any paperwork. Costa Rica also introduced a digital nomad visa in 2022 for remote workers earning above a monthly threshold from foreign sources, granting one year with a one-year extension, exemption from income tax on that foreign income, and the ability to use a foreign driving licence for the duration. It is a well-designed scheme and one of the more attractive in the region. Longer-term residency routes include the pensionado, rentista and inversionista categories, all long established.

Best Time to Visit Puerto Viejo

The Caribbean coast runs on a different calendar from the rest of Costa Rica, which catches people out. The driest and most reliable months here are September and October, precisely when the Pacific side is at its wettest, and February and March are the other dry window. The heaviest rain falls in November, December, July and August. Temperatures barely move: it is around 30 degrees and humid all year. High season for visitors runs roughly December to April and again in July, when prices rise and the town is busy. Surf is best from December to March. Anyone planning around weather should ignore national dry-season advice and use the Caribbean pattern instead.

Pros and Cons of Puerto Viejo

Puerto Viejo is Afro-Caribbean Costa Rica: Creole cooking, calypso, English widely spoken alongside Spanish, and a stretch of coast where rainforest reaches the sand. The beaches south of town at Punta Uva and Manzanillo are among the best in the country, sloths and howler monkeys are ordinary daily sights, and Costa Rica's 180-day visa-free allowance and nomad visa make staying easy. Against that, it is expensive for the region, infrastructure is thin and the internet unreliable enough to matter, and the humidity is constant. Petty theft is a known problem and requires ordinary care. The town has a party reputation that suits some and not others, and healthcare beyond a clinic means Limón or San José.

Who Puerto Viejo Is For

Puerto Viejo suits remote workers who want jungle and Caribbean beach in one place, who can work around variable internet, and whose budget can absorb Costa Rican prices. It is a strong choice for surfers, for people who want wildlife on the doorstep, and for anyone using the Costa Rican digital nomad visa or its long visa-free allowance. It works well for those who like a small, sociable town with an established long-stay foreign population. It is a poor fit for anyone requiring guaranteed connectivity, dry heat, low costs, a coworking space or good healthcare nearby. The Central Valley around San José and Atenas has the infrastructure; Tamarindo and Santa Teresa have the Pacific and more remote workers.

Frequently Asked Questions About Puerto Viejo

How much does it cost to live in Puerto Viejo as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Puerto Viejo is around

,700 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Puerto Viejo?

Puerto Viejo rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Puerto Viejo safe for digital nomads?

Puerto Viejo scores 6 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is average for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Puerto Viejo a good city for digital nomads overall?

Puerto Viejo stands out for nature and the outdoors and widely spoken English among remote workers. With a typical budget near ,700 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
