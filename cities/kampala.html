Kampala at a Glance for Remote Workers

Pearl of Africa's vibrant capital. What pulls nomads here is low costs, backed by nature and climate. Budget around $1,200 a month. The size of the nomad scene is the weak spot.

Cost of Living in Kampala

Kampala is genuinely affordable by global nomad standards, though costs vary significantly depending on where you choose to live. Prices are in Ugandan Shillings (UGX) and US Dollars.

  • Rent, central areas (Kololo, Nakasero): UGX 1,500,000-3,000,000 (~$400-800/month) for a one-bedroom in a gated, generator-backed compound
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Ntinda, Kansanga, Muyenga): UGX 500,000-900,000 (~$130-240/month) for a basic but liveable one-bedroom
  • Groceries: UGX 300,000-500,000 (~$80-135/month) shopping at local markets; supermarkets like Carrefour run 30-50% higher
  • Eating out: A local meal at a market stall runs UGX 3,000-8,000 (~$1-2); a sit-down restaurant in Kololo costs UGX 30,000-80,000 (~$8-22) per person
  • Coworking hot-desk: UGX 35,000-55,000 (~$9-15/day) or UGX 220,000-400,000 (~$60-110/month) for a monthly pass
  • Local transport: Boda-boda (motorcycle taxi) rides run UGX 2,000-10,000 (~$0.50-2.50); Bolt or SafeBoda app rides average UGX 8,000-25,000 (~$2-7)

Total monthly range: A lean budget nomad in a cheaper area spending carefully can get by on $600-800/month. Living in expat neighborhoods with coworking and regular restaurant meals runs $1,200-1,800/month. Few nomads report needing more than $2,000 unless they are renting a standalone house.

Where to Work

Kampala has a small but real set of dedicated coworking spaces, and cafe culture is growing. The best-established options are in the tech hub orbit: Hive Colab on Kanjokya Street in Kamwokya is a community-owned space built for startup founders and tech workers, with solid internet and a focused atmosphere. The Innovation Village in Ntinda offers 24-hour access, fast WiFi, meeting rooms, and free coffee, and attracts a mix of NGO workers and local entrepreneurs.

ZoneIn Coworking on Katalima Road is well-reviewed for reliable backup power and internet, which matters in a city where load-shedding is a regular reality. For cafe work, Endiro Coffee has branches in Muyenga and Kisementi-Kololo: the coffee is high-quality, the WiFi is workable for email and calls, and the power sockets are plentiful. Karveli on Lumumba Avenue is a European-style bakery-cafe with fast internet and a calmer vibe than most.

The honest WiFi picture: speeds at dedicated coworking spaces are generally solid (10-50 Mbps is typical). Cafes can be patchy during peak hours. Load-shedding is a real issue across the city, so any serious work setup needs a space with a generator or UPS backup. Most coworking spaces have this; most cafes do not. A local SIM (MTN or Airtel) with a 4G data bundle is a reliable backup and costs around UGX 50,000-80,000 ($13-22) for 20-30 GB.

Getting Around

Kampala is notoriously congested. Traffic during morning and evening rush hours can turn a 3 km trip into a 45-minute ordeal. Boda-bodas (motorcycle taxis) are the fastest way to navigate the gridlock but carry real road-safety risk: accidents are common, helmets are not always offered, and standards vary widely. Use SafeBoda or Bolt moto rides for transparency on pricing and driver ratings. For longer distances or bad weather, Bolt and Uber cars are reliable and reasonably priced.

Shared minibuses (matatus) connect most parts of the city cheaply (UGX 1,000-2,000 per ride) but routes are not easy to navigate without local knowledge. The city is not particularly walkable outside of upmarket neighborhoods like Kololo and Nakasero, where pavements exist and distances are manageable.

Entebbe International Airport sits roughly 40 km south of central Kampala. The drive takes 45 minutes on a clear day and 90-plus minutes in traffic. A private taxi or pre-booked ride costs UGX 80,000-120,000 ($22-32). Bolt and Uber both operate the airport route. A shared shuttle bus runs for around UGX 10,000 ($2.50) but takes two hours or more.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Uganda requires most nationalities to obtain a visa before arrival. The East African Tourist Visa ($100, covers Uganda, Kenya, and Rwanda) is the best deal for multi-country travel. A single-entry Uganda tourist visa costs $50 and is valid for up to 90 days, though you may be granted fewer days at entry: specify 90 days when applying. Apply online before you travel at visas.immigration.go.ug; walk-up processing on arrival at Entebbe is technically possible but slower and not recommended.

Citizens of most East African Community countries (Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan, DRC, Somalia) enter visa-free. Some African Union countries also have visa-free or reduced-fee access; check your specific passport. There is currently no dedicated digital nomad visa for Uganda.

Extensions are processed at the Kampala Immigration office on Jinja Road and typically require a fee of around $50 per additional 30 days. Total stay on a tourist status can reach up to 6 months with extensions. Confirm current rules and fees directly with the Uganda Directorate of Citizenship and Immigration Control at immigration.go.ug before travel, as policies can change.

Best Time to Visit

Kampala sits at around 1,100 metres above sea level near the equator, which keeps temperatures moderate year-round: average highs of 26-28 degrees Celsius (79-82F), cooler evenings around 17-18 degrees (63-64F). There is no extreme heat, which helps with focus and productivity.

The two dry seasons are the best windows for remote work: June to August (the longer dry season, with the most reliable conditions) and December to February. The rainy seasons run March to May and September to November. Kampala does not get monsoon-level rain, but sustained downpours during the long rainy season (March-May) can flood roads and disrupt transport, adding friction to daily life.

June and July are the clearest months for outdoor mobility. The city is busier with regional visitors in July-August, but coworking spaces rarely fill up. If you want a quieter city with a slightly lower cost for short-term rentals, January-February is a good alternative dry-season window.

Pros and Cons

  • Genuine affordability: One of the cheapest capitals in East Africa for rent, food, and daily costs
  • English is the official language and widely spoken, making daily life accessible from day one
  • Warm, sociable culture: Ugandans are consistently described by expats as welcoming and friendly
  • Growing tech and NGO ecosystem: Real community of founders, developers, and international workers
  • Vibrant nightlife and food scene at very low cost compared to other African capitals
  • Easy access to nature: Gorilla trekking, national parks, and Lake Victoria day trips within a few hours
  • Traffic is genuinely bad: Kampala congestion is among the worst in East Africa and affects every day
  • Air quality is poor: Kampala ranks among the most polluted cities globally due to vehicle emissions and waste burning
  • Power cuts are common: Load-shedding affects residential and commercial areas; backup power is essential
  • Road safety risk: Boda-bodas are the main fast transport but accidents are frequent
  • Coworking infrastructure is limited compared to Southeast Asian or Latin American nomad hubs
  • Cleanliness varies sharply by neighborhood; central markets and informal areas can be chaotic

Who Kampala Is Best For

Kampala suits nomads who are genuinely curious about East Africa and want to be embedded in a real city rather than a tourist-facing bubble. If you are working with NGOs, international development organizations, or the tech startup scene, Kampala puts you close to the action. It works well for budget-conscious nomads who do not need high-speed infrastructure everywhere: if you can rely on a solid coworking space and tolerate some friction in daily logistics, the cost savings are real.

It also suits people who value culture, food, and nightlife on a budget. The city has a genuine creative and social energy that most people do not expect.

Skip Kampala if you need consistent, fast internet for video-heavy work without being tied to a coworking space. It is also a harder sell for nomads who are sensitive to air quality or traffic stress, or who expect the cafe-laptop culture of Chiang Mai or Lisbon. It is not a beach destination and has few scenic outdoor work settings. First-time Africa visitors may find the infrastructure challenges steeper than expected.

Frequently Asked Questions About Kampala

How much does it cost to live in Kampala as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,200 a month in Kampala for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Kampala?

Kampala rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Kampala safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Kampala is 5/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Kampala a good city for digital nomads overall?

Kampala stands out for low costs and nature among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,200 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
