Is Alicante a Good Base for Remote Work?

A sunny beach city with affordable living. What pulls nomads here is climate, backed by safety and food. Budget around $2,200 a month. The language barrier is the weak spot.

Cost of Living in Alicante

Alicante is one of the better-value coastal cities in Western Europe, and that is a big part of why nomads keep showing up. You get a Mediterranean beach city for a fraction of what Barcelona or the French Riviera would cost. Prices below are rough 2025-2026 figures in euros, with US dollar equivalents at roughly 1 EUR to 1.13 USD.

  • Rent, central one-bedroom: around EUR 750-950 (about $850-1,075) per month in the city center or near the beach.
  • Rent, cheaper area: a small flat in outer neighborhoods runs EUR 500-700 (about $565-790), and shared or studio setups can dip lower.
  • Utilities: electricity and water around EUR 130-160 ($145-180), plus home internet and a SIM for roughly EUR 27 ($30).
  • Groceries: budget EUR 200-300 ($225-340) for one person. Spanish supermarkets and the Mercado Central keep produce, dairy, and meat cheap.
  • Eating out: the menu del dia (a full lunch with starter, main, dessert, and a drink) is typically EUR 12-15 ($14-17). A casual dinner for one lands around EUR 15-20 ($17-23).
  • Coworking hot desk: a flexible monthly membership generally sits around EUR 100-160 ($115-180), with day passes available.
  • Local transport: a monthly bus and tram pass is inexpensive, and single rides are cheap.

All in, a single nomad renting a modest place can live comfortably on roughly EUR 1,400-2,000 ($1,600-2,300) per month. That is strong value for a sunny beach city with fast internet, and it undercuts most of Western Europe's coastal alternatives.

Where to Work

Alicante has a small but real coworking scene centered on the old town and city center, so you are rarely far from a desk. Well-known options include ULab near Placa de San Cristobal, which runs a fast connection (around 300 Mbps) and offers hot desks, dedicated desks, and private offices. V11 Coworking sits in the heart of the city with 24/7 access, meeting rooms, and a kitchen. TerretUp leans into the local startup community and is a good bet for meeting other remote workers. Syndicate Coworking is another central pick. If you want a coliving plus coworking setup, Sun and Co. is a short trip up the coast in Javea.

Cafe-work culture is decent but not limitless. Plenty of cafes near the marina and old town are laptop-friendly during quieter hours, though some smaller spots would rather you did not camp out over one coffee at lunchtime. Home WiFi in modern flats is generally fast and reliable (fiber is widespread), so many nomads split their time between a coworking membership and their apartment.

Getting Around

Alicante is compact and very walkable. The old town (El Barrio), the marina, the Explanada promenade, and Postiguet beach are all within an easy stroll of each other, so you may not need transport for daily life at all.

For longer trips, the TRAM Metropolitano is the star. Its light-rail and tram lines (L1 through L5) run from Luceros and the city center up the coast to El Campello, Benidorm, Altea, and Denia, which makes weekend trips and beach-hopping easy without a car. City buses fill in the gaps within Alicante itself and are cheap and frequent.

Getting to and from Alicante-Elche Airport (ALC) is simple. The C6 bus runs 24 hours a day, every day of the year, roughly every 15-20 minutes, connecting the airport with the city center (including the Renfe station and Luceros, where you pick up the TRAM). A single ticket is about EUR 4.60 ($5.20). A taxi is faster but naturally costs more.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

If you hold a passport from the US, UK, Canada, Australia, or another visa-exempt country, you can enter Spain and the wider Schengen Area visa-free for up to 90 days within any 180-day period. That is enough for a long stay, but it resets slowly, so you cannot simply live here on tourist entries year-round.

For longer stays, Spain offers a digital nomad visa aimed at remote workers and freelancers with clients outside Spain. It requires proof of stable income (tied to Spain's minimum wage, roughly EUR 2,540-2,850 per month for a single applicant in 2026, higher with dependents), valid health insurance, and a clean criminal record. Applying from your home country typically grants one year, while applying from inside Spain can grant a longer residence authorization, and the permit is renewable in stages toward long-term residency. It also preserves your right to travel elsewhere in Schengen for 90 days per 180.

Visa rules and income thresholds change, so confirm the current requirements with an official Spanish consulate or the Spanish immigration authorities before you commit.

Best Time to Visit

Alicante is one of the sunniest cities in Europe, with a mild Mediterranean climate and something like 300 sunny days a year. Summers (June to September) are hot and busy, with beach weather and long evenings, but also the peak tourist crush and higher short-term rental prices.

Winters are mild, rarely cold, and you can often sit outside at midday in January. The sweet spot for remote work is the shoulder seasons: April to June and September to November give you warm, sunny days, swimmable sea for much of it, thinner crowds, and better rental value. If you want the quietest, cheapest stretch, deep winter is calm and still pleasant, just cooler for swimming.

Pros and Cons

The honest picture:

  • Pro: Excellent value for a Western European beach city.
  • Pro: Sunny almost year-round with a mild winter.
  • Pro: Walkable center with beaches right in town.
  • Pro: Fast, widespread fiber internet and a workable coworking scene.
  • Pro: Easy coastal day trips via the TRAM and a well-connected airport.
  • Con: Summer brings heavy tourist crowds and higher prices.
  • Con: The off-season is much quieter, which some find too sleepy.
  • Con: The coworking and nomad community is smaller than in Barcelona or Lisbon.
  • Con: Some Spanish goes a long way, especially for admin and outside tourist zones.
  • Con: Spanish bureaucracy for visas and residency can be slow.

Who Alicante Is Best For

Alicante suits nomads who want beach-city living on a reasonable budget, value sunshine and a walkable pace over big-city intensity, and are happy with a smaller, friendlier community rather than a packed international scene. It is a great base if you like the idea of easy weekend trips up the Costa Blanca and do not mind picking up some Spanish.

You should probably skip Alicante if you need a large, buzzing nomad network and constant events, if you dislike tourist-heavy summers, or if you want a city that stays high-energy through the winter. Those travelers tend to be happier in Barcelona, Madrid, or Lisbon. For everyone else chasing affordable Mediterranean sun with solid WiFi, Alicante is an easy recommendation.

Frequently Asked Questions About Alicante

How much does it cost to live in Alicante as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,200 a month in Alicante for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Alicante?

Alicante rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work good. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Alicante safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Alicante is 8/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Alicante a good city for digital nomads overall?

Alicante stands out for climate and safety among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,200 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
