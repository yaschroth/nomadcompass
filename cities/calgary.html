Cost of Living in Calgary

Calgary sits in a useful sweet spot for Canada: it is a real city with big-city amenities, but it costs meaningfully less than Vancouver or Toronto. Alberta also has no provincial sales tax, so day-to-day spending stretches a little further than elsewhere in the country. Figures below are per month in Canadian dollars, with rough US dollar equivalents (1 CAD is about 0.73 USD in 2025 and 2026).

  • Rent, central one-bedroom (Beltline, Kensington, Inglewood, East Village): roughly $1,800-2,200 CAD ($1,300-1,600 USD).
  • Rent, cheaper areas (newer northeast and southeast suburbs): roughly $1,450-1,650 CAD ($1,060-1,200 USD), though you will lean on a car out there.
  • Groceries: around $350-450 CAD ($255-330 USD) for one person.
  • Eating out: a casual meal runs about $18-25 CAD, and a mid-range dinner for two lands near $80-100 CAD. Budget $300-450 CAD ($220-330 USD) monthly if you eat out a few times a week.
  • Coworking hot desk: day passes run about $25-55 CAD, and a monthly hot-desk membership is typically $150-350 CAD ($110-255 USD).
  • Local transport: an adult monthly Calgary Transit pass is $126 CAD ($92 USD).

A realistic all-in total for one person is roughly $2,800-4,000 CAD ($2,050-2,900 USD) per month, depending on how central you live and how often you eat out. That is comfortably below what the same lifestyle costs in Vancouver or Toronto, which is a big part of Calgary's appeal.

Where to Work

Calgary has a solid coworking scene concentrated downtown and in the inner-city neighborhoods. WeWork operates in Stephen Avenue Place downtown and sells day passes and month-to-month memberships. Workhaus has a central location with day passes and dedicated desks. cSPACE Marda Loop, in a converted heritage school building southwest of the core, is a favorite with creatives and freelancers. Regus covers several locations across the city, and independent and community spaces such as TradeSpace and ReSourceYYC round out the options. If you want to sample spaces without committing, on-demand platforms like Deskpass and Coworker list Calgary desks by the day.

Cafe-work culture exists but is more low-key than in a beach-town nomad hub. You will find plenty of independent cafes in Kensington, Inglewood, and the Beltline that tolerate laptop workers, especially outside the lunch rush. WiFi is genuinely reliable: Canada has strong wired and mobile broadband, and fast, stable connections are the norm in cafes, coworking spaces, and rentals. If your work depends on video calls, Calgary will not be the bottleneck.

Getting Around

The backbone of transit is the CTrain, Calgary's light rail, which runs two lines (Red and Blue) through downtown. The stretch through the core along 7th Avenue is free to ride, which is handy for downtown hopping. A single adult fare is $4.00 CAD (valid 90 minutes), a day pass is $12.65 CAD, and the monthly pass is $126 CAD. Buses fill in the gaps beyond the rail lines, and you can pay with the Connect card or the mobile app.

Be honest with yourself about the layout: Calgary is spread out and car-oriented. If you live centrally and work from home or a downtown desk, you can manage on transit, cycling in summer, and the occasional rideshare. If you settle in the suburbs or want easy access to the Rocky Mountains on weekends, a car (or frequent rentals) becomes close to essential. Banff and Kananaskis are roughly 90 minutes to two hours west by road, and there is no convenient train for that trip.

Getting to and from YYC Calgary International Airport is straightforward. Calgary Transit Route 300 (and other connections) links the airport to the CTrain and downtown for a modest fare, taking roughly 45 minutes to an hour. Taxis and rideshares to the core run higher but are quick, and the airport is only about 20 minutes from downtown by car outside rush hour.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

For many passports (including the UK, most of the EU, Australia, and New Zealand), Canada is visa-exempt for tourism. If you arrive by air, you will need an Electronic Travel Authorization (eTA), which costs CA$7, is applied for online, and can stay valid for up to five years. US citizens do not need an eTA. Visitors are typically admitted for up to six months at a time, with the exact length decided by the border officer.

If you want to stay longer, you can apply to extend your visitor status from inside Canada (a Visitor Record) before your authorized stay ends. Note that a tourist entry allows remote work for a foreign employer or clients but does not permit working for a Canadian company; longer-term or work-authorized stays need the appropriate permit. Immigration rules change, so confirm your specific situation on the official Government of Canada site (canada.ca) before you travel.

Best Time to Visit

Calgary's climate is the single biggest thing to plan around. Winters are cold and snowy, with stretches where temperatures sit well below freezing and cold snaps that can dip much lower. The upside is Calgary's famous chinook winds, warm arches of air off the Rockies that can spike temperatures by 15 to 20 degrees Celsius in hours and melt the snow mid-winter. They are a genuine relief, but unpredictable.

Summers are the reward: warm, dry, and sunny, with long daylight hours and easy access to the mountains for hiking. The best window for remote work is roughly May through September, when the weather is pleasant, patios and cafes are lively, and the Rockies are at their most accessible. July also brings the Calgary Stampede, which is fun but crowds the city and pushes up short-term rental prices. If you dislike deep cold and short winter days, avoid December through February unless you specifically want a ski-and-snow base.

Pros and Cons

The honest trade-offs:

  • Pro: Noticeably cheaper than Vancouver or Toronto, with no provincial sales tax.
  • Pro: World-class Rocky Mountains access, with Banff and Kananaskis under two hours away.
  • Pro: Fast, reliable internet and a clean, safe, well-run city.
  • Pro: Strong summers with long daylight and an outdoor lifestyle.
  • Con: Long, cold, snowy winters that can wear you down.
  • Con: Sprawling and car-reliant once you leave the core.
  • Con: A quieter, smaller nomad and social scene than the big coastal cities.
  • Con: Weekend mountain trips realistically need a car.

Who Calgary Is Best For

Calgary suits remote workers who want mountain access and a lower cost base without giving up big-city services. If you ski, hike, or climb, having Banff and Kananaskis on your doorstep is hard to beat, and you get a comfortable, safe city to come home to. It also works well for nomads who value reliable infrastructure and a calmer pace over a buzzing nightlife or a large expat crowd.

Skip Calgary if cold winters are a dealbreaker, if you want to live car-free with everything walkable, or if you are chasing a big, dense nomad community with constant meetups and a lively scene. In those cases Vancouver, Toronto, or a warmer base abroad will fit you better. But if the mountains and the value proposition speak to you, and you can handle the winter, Calgary is a genuinely rewarding place to set up for a season.

Frequently Asked Questions About Calgary

How much does it cost to live in Calgary as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $3,500 a month in Calgary for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Calgary?

Calgary rates 9 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Calgary safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Calgary is 9/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Calgary a good city for digital nomads overall?

Calgary stands out for nature and English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $3,500 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
