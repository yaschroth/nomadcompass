Discover baroque fortress city with gaming industry and Mediterranean sun. Plan on roughly $2,400 a month. Its strengths are safety and English, while nature access is where it scores lower.

Cost of Living in Valletta

Valletta itself is tiny and expensive, so most nomads base themselves in the wider harbour area (Sliema, St Julian's, Gzira, or Msida) and treat the capital as a place to visit rather than live. Costs here run to mid-tier European levels, not budget-Mediterranean. Prices are in euros with rough US dollar equivalents at about 1.14 to the euro, so treat the conversions as approximate.

  • Rent, central 1-bed (Sliema, St Julian's, Valletta): €1,100 to €1,600 ($1,250 to $1,825) a month, furnished, often higher for a seafront view.
  • Rent, cheaper areas (Gzira, Msida, inland towns): roughly €800 to €1,150 ($900 to $1,300) for a comparable 1-bed a short bus ride out.
  • Groceries: €250 to €400 ($285 to $455) a month for one person cooking most meals. Local produce and pastizzi are cheap, imported goods less so.
  • Eating out: a casual local meal runs €10 to €15 ($11 to $17), a mid-range dinner for two €40 to €70 ($46 to $80). Village bars and kiosks serve full plates for €8 to €12.
  • Coworking hot-desk: €15 to €30 ($17 to $34) per day, or roughly €150 to €250 ($170 to $285) a month for a flexible membership.
  • Local transport: effectively free once you hold a personalised Tallinja card (buses and the harbour ferries cost nothing with it). Budget under €30 a month if you rely on cash fares or the odd taxi.

A realistic all-in monthly total for one person lands around €1,900 to €2,900 ($2,150 to $3,300), driven mostly by rent. Sharing a flat or basing yourself inland pulls the lower end down noticeably.

Where to Work

The coworking scene clusters in Sliema, Gzira, and St Julian's rather than inside Valletta. SOHO runs several premium locations across the Sliema and St Julian's strip with a large member community, high-speed internet, and private offices. The Hub Workspace sits near the Sliema seafront with 24/7 access and meeting rooms. In Valletta itself, Grand Central occupies a restored 19th-century building with hot-desks, dedicated desks, and harbour views. Day passes typically run €15 to €30.

Cafe-work culture exists but is patchy. Sliema and St Julian's have plenty of cafes with WiFi, though many get loud and busy, and not every place is happy with laptop campers over the lunch rush. For serious calls, a coworking space or your own flat is the safer bet. On connectivity, Malta is genuinely strong: it has near-universal gigabit-capable fibre coverage, home fibre packages run €30 to €50 a month, and 5G covers Sliema, St Julian's, and Valletta. Video calls and heavy uploads are rarely a problem.

Getting Around

There is no rail or metro anywhere in Malta, so you move by bus, ferry, on foot, or by taxi. The bus network (Tallinja) reaches most of the island and is free for residents who hold a personalised Tallinja card, which is well worth applying for if you stay a while. Buses can be crowded and slow in summer traffic, so leave margin.

The harbour ferries are the pleasant surprise. The Sliema to Valletta crossing takes about 10 minutes and costs roughly €2.50 one way (free with the personalised card), and a separate ferry links Valletta to the Three Cities. The core Sliema, Valletta, and St Julian's zone is walkable in stretches, though hills, heat, and constant construction make it less breezy than it looks on a map.

Malta International Airport (MLA) sits at Luqa, about 8 to 10 km from the harbour towns. Tallinja Direct express buses connect the airport to Valletta, Sliema, and St Julian's for €1.50 to €3, and a taxi or ride-hail is a short, cheap hop by European standards.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Malta is in the Schengen Area, so most visa-exempt visitors (including US, UK, Canadian, and Australian passport holders) can stay up to 90 days within any 180-day period across Schengen as a whole, not just Malta. EU, EEA, and Swiss citizens can live and work here without a permit.

For longer stays, non-EU remote workers can apply for the Malta Nomad Residence Permit. It requires a minimum gross income of about €42,000 a year (roughly €3,500 a month) from work tied to clients or an employer outside Malta, plus health insurance and a rental or purchase agreement. The permit is issued for one year and can be renewed annually up to a total of four years, and renewal generally expects you to spend at least five months a year in Malta. Rules, income thresholds, and fees change, so confirm the current details with the official Residency Malta Agency before you apply.

Best Time to Visit

Malta has hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. July and August are intensely hot and busy, with peak tourist crowds and packed seafronts, which makes focused remote work harder and rents at their steepest. Winters (December to February) are mild by northern-European standards but can be windy, grey, and rainy, and older flats are often poorly heated.

The best windows for remote work are the shoulder seasons: roughly April to early June and late September to November. You get warm, swimmable weather, thinner crowds, and easier prices, without the summer overheating that turns midday into a write-off.

Pros and Cons

The honest trade-offs of basing yourself here:

  • Pro: English is an official language, so daily life and bureaucracy are far smoother than in most of Europe.
  • Pro: Excellent, near-universal fibre and 5G, plus free resident bus and ferry travel.
  • Pro: Sunny climate, swimmable sea, and a compact, walkable harbour zone with a real nomad community.
  • Con: Summer heat and crowds are draining, and peak-season rents and prices are high for the region.
  • Con: Heavy road traffic, no rail, and slow buses make getting around frustrating at busy times.
  • Con: The island is small, so it can feel repetitive over long stays, and constant construction means noise and dust in many neighbourhoods.

Who Valletta Is Best For

The Valletta area suits remote workers who want reliable infrastructure, a Mediterranean lifestyle, and an English-speaking base without a steep language barrier. It fits sun-seekers, sea-swimmers, and anyone who values a short, self-contained island where you can walk to the water after a workday. It also works well for non-EU nomads who meet the Nomad Residence Permit income bar and want a clear legal path to staying longer.

You should probably skip it if you are on a tight budget, need cheap rent, or cannot tolerate summer heat and crowds. It is also a poor match if you want big-city variety, extensive nightlife beyond the St Julian's strip, or the option to hop between many different places by train, since Malta is small and you will feel its edges within a few months.

Frequently Asked Questions About Valletta

How much does it cost to live in Valletta as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,400 a month in Valletta for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Valletta?

Valletta rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work good. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Valletta safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Valletta is 9/10, excellent for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Valletta a good city for digital nomads overall?

Valletta stands out for safety and English among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,400 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
