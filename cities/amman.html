Setting Up as a Nomad in Amman

Discover ancient hills meeting modern cafes in the heart of the Levant. For remote workers, Amman leans on culture and low costs, with a typical budget near $1,800 a month. The main trade-off is air quality.

Cost of Living in Amman

Amman sits at the affordable end of Middle Eastern capitals without being rock-bottom cheap. A realistic monthly budget for a single person living comfortably runs between 800 and 1,400 JOD (1,130-1,980 USD), depending on your neighborhood and lifestyle.

  • Rent, central (Jabal Amman, Abdoun, Sweifieh): 350-550 JOD (495-775 USD) for a furnished one-bedroom
  • Rent, cheaper area (Shmeisani, Gardens, Bayader): 200-320 JOD (280-450 USD)
  • Groceries: 80-130 JOD (115-185 USD) per month for one person
  • Eating out, local restaurant: 4-6 JOD (6-9 USD) per meal; mid-range dinner for two around 30-50 JOD (42-70 USD)
  • Coworking hot-desk: 50-120 JOD (70-170 USD) per month
  • Local transport (Uber/taxi): 60-100 JOD (85-140 USD) per month depending on how much you rely on apps
  • Total range: 800-1,400 JOD (1,130-1,980 USD) per month

The Jordanian dinar (JOD) is pegged to the USD at roughly 0.71 JOD per dollar, so your budget is predictable. A 5% sales tax applies to most goods and services.

Where to Work

Amman has a growing number of purpose-built coworking spaces alongside a genuine cafe-work culture, particularly in the western hills. Internet is generally reliable in coworking spaces and upmarket cafes, with fibre speeds common. Public cafe WiFi can be inconsistent, so keep a local SIM card with a data package as backup: Zain and Orange both offer affordable monthly data plans.

Coworking spaces worth knowing: bldr space (two locations in Sweifieh and Mecca Street) is the most community-oriented option with regular events. Antwork operates in a converted 1920s building in downtown Amman with garden and rooftop access and a pay-as-you-go model. Regus has multiple addresses if you need a corporate-grade address or meeting rooms.

Cafe work is socially acceptable and common, especially in Jabal Amman and Weibdeh. Books@Cafe on Rainbow Street has been a nomad-friendly spot since the late 1990s, with power outlets and a relaxed all-day atmosphere. Kitab Coffee House draws a student and freelancer crowd with reliable WiFi and plenty of seating. Deewan Institute in Weibdeh offers informal coworking at 1 JOD per hour. Note that some cafes enforce minimum orders during busy hours.

Getting Around

Amman is hilly and sprawling, and while walking is pleasant in individual neighborhoods like Rainbow Street or Weibdeh, the city as a whole is not pedestrian-friendly enough to rely on foot for all your daily needs.

Uber, Careem, and InDrive are all active in Amman and are your most practical tools. Rides within central Amman typically cost 2-4 JOD (3-6 USD). Yellow taxis are everywhere and cheaper, but insist the driver use the meter. Short hops should be 1-2 JOD.

Public buses exist but routes are inconsistent and mostly used by commuters on fixed corridors. They are not practical for day-to-day nomad life.

Airport: Queen Alia International Airport is about 30 km south of the city, roughly 30 minutes by road in light traffic. A taxi or ride-hailing app costs 20-25 JOD one way. The Airport Express bus is cheaper but slower and does not serve hotels or residential areas directly.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Jordan offers a visa on arrival or e-visa to citizens of most Western countries, including the US, UK, EU states, Canada, and Australia. The fee is 40 JOD, and you receive a stay of up to 90 days. You can also purchase a Jordan Pass before arrival, which includes the visa fee and entry to major archaeological sites.

There is no digital nomad visa for Jordan. For stays beyond 90 days, you can apply for a Temporary Residence Permit through the local police (civil status office). This is available in practice and allows annual renewable stays, though the process requires patience and some bureaucratic legwork. Working remotely for foreign clients occupies a legal gray area; it is tolerated but not formally sanctioned.

Always verify current requirements with the official Jordanian Ministry of Interior website or your country's embassy before travel, as visa conditions can change.

Best Time to Visit

Amman sits at 800 meters elevation, which keeps it meaningfully cooler than the Jordan Valley. The best windows for remote work and daily comfort are spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November). Temperatures land between 15-25 degrees C, skies are clear, and the city is energized without being overwhelmed by tourist crowds.

Summer (June to August) brings dry heat with Amman temperatures reaching 33-37 degrees C. It is manageable but exhausting mid-afternoon, and many locals and expats head to higher ground or the coast. Air conditioning is universal in coworking spaces and modern apartments.

Winter (December to February) is genuinely cold, occasionally with snow in January. Amman shuts down unexpectedly during rare snow events. Heating in older apartments can be inadequate. If you handle the cold, you will find a quiet and affordable city. Rainy season runs November through March.

Pros and Cons

  • Genuinely affordable for a capital city with good infrastructure
  • Safe for solo travelers including women, with low violent crime
  • English widely spoken in West Amman among professionals and younger residents
  • Rich culture and history: Petra, Wadi Rum, the Dead Sea, and Jerash are all within 1-3 hours
  • Good food scene from street-level hummus to MENA award-winning restaurants
  • Growing expat and startup community with regular networking events
  • No dedicated nomad visa: long stays require workarounds
  • Car dependency: the hilly, spread-out layout makes getting around without apps tedious
  • Conservative social norms outside West Amman require adjustment, especially for women traveling solo
  • Alcohol is available but restricted to licensed venues and off-licenses; nightlife is modest by regional standards
  • Air quality degrades in summer from dust and traffic
  • Traffic is genuinely bad during rush hours despite the city's modest size

Who Amman Is Best For

Amman suits remote workers who want a Middle Eastern base that feels accessible, safe, and affordable without sacrificing food quality, culture, or a functioning expat community. It works well for those who are curious about the Arab world and want a city where they can settle in for a month or two, use it as a hub for regional travel, and leave feeling like they actually understood the place.

It is a particularly good fit for solo travelers comfortable with conservative customs, people interested in Islamic architecture and archaeology, and those who do their best work in a calm, unhurried city rather than a loud, party-first destination.

Amman is probably not the right choice if you need a buzzing digital nomad scene with daily meetups, crave beach or mountain access within the city, prioritize nightlife, or are frustrated by visa uncertainty. It also demands more independent navigation than cities with polished public transport systems.

Frequently Asked Questions About Amman

How much does it cost to live in Amman as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Amman is around $1,800 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Amman?

With a WiFi score of 6/10, connectivity in Amman is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Amman safe for digital nomads?

Amman scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Amman a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Amman is worth a look if you value culture and low costs. Costs sit near $1,800 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
