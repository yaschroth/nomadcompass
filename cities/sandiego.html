San Diego is one of the more expensive US cities, so budget accordingly. As a solo remote worker, expect rent to be your biggest line item by a wide margin. Here is a realistic monthly breakdown in USD:

  • Rent (central): A one-bedroom in or near Downtown, Little Italy, or Hillcrest typically runs $2,000-2,700. A furnished short-term rental will sit at the top of that range or above.
  • Rent (cheaper areas): Look at City Heights, Talmadge, or University Heights and you can find one-bedrooms closer to $1,850-2,300. A room in a shared place trims this further.
  • Groceries: Around $350-450 if you shop at Trader Joe's, Costco, or Sprouts. Expect to pay roughly 10% above the US average.
  • Eating out: A casual meal is $18-25 per person, and a sit-down dinner for two lands near $90-100. Budget $300-500 depending on how often you go out.
  • Coworking hot-desk: Day passes average about $35. A monthly hot-desk membership generally falls in the $200-350 range.
  • Local transport: An MTS monthly pass is modest, but many residents still spend on rideshare or a car. Budget $80-250 depending on how car-reliant you are.

Total monthly range: A lean solo budget starts around $2,800-3,200 in a cheaper neighborhood. A comfortable central lifestyle runs $3,800-4,800 or more. San Diego rewards planning, not spontaneity.

Where to Work

The coworking scene is healthy and spread across the city rather than concentrated in one district. Downtown Works in the East Village offers day passes and a central location near transit. Cowork + Connect in Hillcrest is popular with travelers and nomads and sells no-commitment day passes plus discounted multi-day packs. WeWork operates several locations if you want a predictable chain, and coastal spaces like VentureBeach put you near the water. You will also find independent spots in North Park, La Jolla, and up the coast in Encinitas and Solana Beach.

Cafe-work culture is strong, especially in North Park, South Park, and the beach neighborhoods, where laptop-friendly coffee shops are easy to find. WiFi is genuinely reliable across the city: home fiber and cable are widely available, coworking spaces run business-grade connections, and mobile coverage is solid. Connectivity is not a concern here. Just carry a backup hotspot if calls are business-critical, as you would anywhere.

Getting Around

San Diego is car-centric, and you should be honest with yourself about that before you arrive. The region was built around driving, and neighborhoods sprawl. That said, you can live car-light if you pick your base carefully. The MTS Trolley light rail runs Blue, Orange, and Green lines connecting Downtown with East County, UC San Diego, South Bay, and the Mexico border, and ridership has recovered strongly. Buses fill the gaps, and rideshare is everywhere.

Walkable pockets do exist. Downtown, Little Italy, Hillcrest, North Park, and the beach areas let you handle daily life on foot. Step outside those and you will want wheels. Getting to and from San Diego International Airport (SAN) is easy and cheap: MTS Route 992 links Downtown and the airport in about 15 minutes for around $2.50, and the free San Diego Flyer shuttle connects both terminals to the Old Town Transit Center, where you can pick up the Trolley and regional rail. Because SAN sits so close to Downtown, a rideshare from the center is short and inexpensive.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Most short-term visitors arrive under the Visa Waiver Program (VWP), which covers citizens of roughly 40 countries, including much of Europe, the UK, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and New Zealand. Under VWP you can stay up to 90 days for tourism or business without a visa. You will need a valid e-passport with a chip and an approved ESTA authorization, which costs a small fee and should be obtained before you fly.

The 90-day VWP admission cannot be extended, and there is no digital-nomad visa for the US. If you want to stay longer, you will need to leave and re-enter (not guaranteed, and back-to-back stays draw scrutiny) or apply for a different visa category such as a B-1/B-2 visitor visa, which typically allows longer visits. US citizens and green-card holders can simply stay as long as they like with no entry formalities. Rules change, so confirm your eligibility and current requirements with official US sources such as the CBP ESTA site and travel.state.gov before booking.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit

San Diego has famously mild, stable weather, averaging around 70 degrees Fahrenheit year-round with well over 250 sunny days. There is no bad season for working here, but there is a catch worth knowing: May Gray and June Gloom. In late spring and early summer, a marine layer parks a low gray overcast over the coast, often lasting into the afternoon. It is not cold, but if you came for beach sunshine you may feel cheated.

For the best remote-work window, target March to April or September to October. You get warm, sunny days, thinner crowds, and lower rates than peak summer. July and August deliver the classic blue-sky San Diego but bring the highest prices and busiest beaches.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Reliable, mild weather nearly every day of the year.
  • Pro: Excellent, dependable internet and a spread-out but solid coworking scene.
  • Pro: Beaches, outdoor life, and a relaxed pace with easy access to Mexico.
  • Pro: Cheap, quick airport connection close to the city center.
  • Con: High cost of living, with rent the main pain point.
  • Con: Car reliance outside a handful of walkable neighborhoods.
  • Con: No nomad visa, and the 90-day cap limits long stays for most foreigners.
  • Con: May Gray and June Gloom can dull the coast for weeks.

Who San Diego Is Best For

San Diego suits remote workers with a comfortable budget who value lifestyle over saving money: outdoor-oriented people who want beaches, sunshine, and a laid-back US city with strong infrastructure. It works well for US-based nomads and for VWP travelers doing a 90-day stint. It is a natural fit if you already own or plan to rent a car, or if you commit to a walkable base like North Park, Hillcrest, or the beach neighborhoods.

Skip it if you are optimizing for low costs, if you refuse to touch a car and want everything within walking distance, or if you need a long-term base and hold a passport capped at 90 days. Budget nomads will stretch their money much further elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions About San Diego

How much does it cost to live in San Diego as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in San Diego is around $4,500 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in San Diego?

With a WiFi score of 9/10, connectivity in San Diego is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is San Diego safe for digital nomads?

San Diego scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is San Diego a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, San Diego is worth a look if you value English and climate. Costs sit near $4,500 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
