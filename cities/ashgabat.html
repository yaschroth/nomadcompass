Setting Up as a Nomad in Ashgabat

More white marble per square metre than anywhere on earth, and almost nobody on the streets. Expect $1,000 a month here. Cleanliness and safety are the standout strengths, with visa access the main caveat.

Best Neighborhoods in Ashgabat

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Central Ashgabat

$$$
Monumental & Controlled

Independence Square and the ministries in white marble

The government core around Independence Square, with the ministries, the Ruhyyet Palace, the Independence Monument and the presidential complex, all faced in white marble with gold detailing. The scale is enormous, the boulevards are wide and the streets are notably empty.

Best for: Anyone with business at a ministry or an embassy
Pros
  • The most extraordinary built environment on this site
  • Everything official in the country is here
  • Immaculately maintained and entirely safe to walk
Cons
  • Photography is restricted and enforced seriously
  • Enormous distances between buildings
  • Almost nothing open and very few people about

Berzengi

$$$$
Monumental & Empty

The southern district of monuments and hotels

The area at the foot of the Kopet Dag holding the largest of the marble complexes: the hotels, the Alem ferris wheel enclosed in a building, the wedding palace, the ministries and the Turkmenbashi Ruhy mosque a little further out. It is where the marble density record was set.

Best for: Anyone staying in one of the state hotels
Pros
  • The most striking architecture in the country
  • The Kopet Dag mountains rise immediately behind
  • The hotels here are the best available
Cons
  • Almost entirely empty of ordinary life
  • Nothing walkable in any practical sense
  • Photography restrictions are heaviest here

Choganly

$$
Residential & Plain

The northern housing district

One of the residential areas built on the northern edge of the city to rehouse people displaced by the marble reconstruction of the centre, laid out in blocks with schools and shops. It is where a large share of Ashgabat's ordinary population now lives.

Best for: Understanding where the city's people actually live
Pros
  • The cheapest housing in the capital
  • Ordinary shops and markets at local prices
  • Quieter and less watched than the monumental districts
Cons
  • A long way from the centre with limited transport
  • Architecturally plain by design
  • Very little English or Russian signage

Bagyr and Nisa

$$
Archaeological & Rural

The Parthian royal city on the western edge

A village on the western outskirts about eleven kilometres from the centre, beside Old Nisa, the fortified Parthian royal residence founded in the third century before the common era and inscribed by UNESCO in 2007. The mud-brick walls and the excavated halls sit on a hill above the plain.

Best for: Anyone whose interest in Turkmenistan is its Parthian and Silk Road past
Pros
  • Old Nisa is a UNESCO site of real significance
  • Views across the plain to the Kopet Dag
  • Rural and quiet, unlike anywhere in the city
Cons
  • Eleven kilometres from the centre
  • Almost no visitor infrastructure at the site
  • Reaching it usually means a guide and a driver

Cost of Living in Ashgabat

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Ashgabat, so the $1,000 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 981 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,000 a month, though costs here are difficult to state plainly because Turkmenistan operates parallel exchange rates and the gap between the official and unofficial manat has been very wide. A one-bedroom runs roughly $300 to $600 at official rates. Subsidised utilities were a national policy for decades and have been progressively withdrawn. Local bazaar produce is cheap; imported goods are expensive and sometimes simply unavailable. Eating out is inexpensive by international standards. Bring cash: international cards frequently do not work and withdrawing money can be genuinely difficult.

Where to Work in Ashgabat

Connectivity is the worst on this site. Turkmenistan has among the most restricted and slowest internet in the world, with a state monopoly, extensive filtering that blocks most social media and messaging services, and speeds that make video calls impractical. VPNs are blocked and their use is discouraged in ways that carry risk. There is no coworking. Anyone whose work requires open internet access should treat Turkmenistan as unworkable rather than difficult. The time zone is five hours ahead of GMT. Anyone with work that depends on cloud services, messaging or video should assume none of it will function reliably, and should not plan to be productive here.

Getting Around Ashgabat

Ashgabat is laid out on a monumental scale with wide boulevards and enormous distances between buildings, so walking is possible but slow and the summer heat makes it unpleasant. Taxis are cheap and plentiful, including informal ones, and buses cover the city. The city holds a Guinness record for the highest density of white marble buildings, and the effect is striking and strange. Ashgabat International Airport connects to Istanbul, Dubai, Moscow and a small number of other cities, with Turkmenistan Airlines operating most of them. Anyone photographing the city should also be careful, since government buildings, the presidential palace and many public monuments cannot be photographed and this is enforced.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for Ashgabat

Turkmenistan has one of the most restrictive visa regimes in the world. Independent tourism is effectively not permitted: almost every visitor needs a letter of invitation obtained through a licensed local tour operator, an approved itinerary and a state-assigned guide, and the application is frequently refused without explanation. A five day transit visa exists for those crossing between two neighbouring countries and is also frequently refused. There is no remote work visa and no realistic route to a long independent stay. Anyone applying should also allow months rather than weeks, since the letter of invitation is processed by the state migration service and the timeline is entirely unpredictable.

Best Time to Visit Ashgabat

April to May and September to October are the only comfortable windows, warm at around twenty five degrees. Summer from June to August is severe, regularly above forty degrees in a city surrounded by the Karakum desert, and winter can drop below freezing. Rainfall is negligible year round. The Darvaza gas crater, the burning pit in the desert north of the city, is best visited in the cooler months and requires an overnight camp, which is one of the few things the tour operators reliably arrange. Anyone visiting Merv or Kunya-Urgench should note that both are a long way east and north of the capital and that internal travel is arranged through the same operator.

Pros and Cons of Ashgabat

The case for Ashgabat is that there is nothing else like it: a capital rebuilt almost entirely in white marble and gold since the 1990s, with monumental architecture, empty boulevards and a personality cult expressed in stone. Nearby Nisa is a UNESCO-listed Parthian city and Merv, further east, was once among the largest cities in the world. The case against is comprehensive: the visa regime, the surveillance, the internet restrictions, the heat, and a state that controls what visitors see and where they go. The Darvaza crater has also been burning since a Soviet drilling accident, and the government has repeatedly announced plans to extinguish it without doing so.

Who Ashgabat Is For

Turkmenistan suits determined travellers collecting difficult countries, archaeologists working on Parthian and Silk Road sites, and people posted here by an employer. It does not suit remote workers in any sense, because the internet cannot support the work and the visa regime does not permit the stay. Anyone going should understand that they will be accompanied, that photography is restricted, and that the country is among the most closed in the world. It also suits anyone drawn to the Akhal-Teke horse, the national symbol and one of the oldest breeds in the world, which the state maintains with considerable ceremony.

Frequently Asked Questions About Ashgabat

How much does it cost to live in Ashgabat as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Ashgabat is around

,000 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Ashgabat?

Ashgabat rates 2 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is on the limited side for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Ashgabat safe for digital nomads?

Ashgabat scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.
