A Remote Worker's Guide to Tunis

Discover mediterranean crossroads with ancient medina and French colonial elegance. Expect $1,200 a month here. Low costs and culture are the standout strengths, with cleanliness the main caveat.

Cost of Living in Tunis

Tunis is one of the most affordable capitals on the Mediterranean rim. Prices are quoted in Tunisian dinars (TND); 1 USD buys roughly 3.1 TND in mid-2026.

  • Rent, city center (downtown, Lac district): 900-1,100 TND/month (~$290-$355) for a furnished 1-bedroom
  • Rent, outer areas (Ettadhamen, Ariana, Manouba): 550-750 TND/month (~$175-$240) for a 1-bedroom
  • Groceries (cooking most meals): 350-500 TND/month (~$115-$160)
  • Eating out (mix of local cafes and mid-range restaurants): 300-500 TND/month (~$95-$160)
  • Coworking hot-desk (monthly pass): 140-620 TND/month (~$45-$200) depending on the space
  • Local transport (bus/metro monthly pass): 40 TND/month (~$13)
  • Total comfortable range: 2,000-3,500 TND/month (~$650-$1,130)

Budget nomads sharing apartments and cooking regularly can live on $650-750/month. Those renting solo, eating out often, and using a quality coworking space land closer to $900-$1,100/month. Tunis sits well below comparable Mediterranean cities in western Europe.

Where to Work

Tunis has a small but functional coworking scene concentrated around Lac de Tunis, Centre Urbain Nord, and the downtown Bab Bhar area.

  • Cogite (Lac de Bizerte, Lac district) is the most established community-focused space, rated 4.5 on Google. It offers fiber internet, an outdoor terrace, and regular networking events.
  • Work Zone (Centre Urbain Nord) is a modern high-rise space with 24/7 access, natural light, and a reliable connection. Hot desks run around 150-200 TND/month.
  • Level 1 (Boulevard Cheikh Zaid) operates as an anti-cafe: you pay per hour and get unlimited drinks. It has fast wifi, meeting rooms, and a prototyping lab, and suits shorter sessions well.
  • Regus Berges du Lac offers a more corporate setup with guaranteed bandwidth and access to meeting rooms at international-standard pricing.

Cafe work culture exists but is uneven. Upscale neighborhoods like La Marsa and Sidi Bou Said have laptop-tolerant cafes with decent wifi. In the downtown medina and older districts, cafe wifi is patchy or nonexistent. Avoid leaving devices unattended in any public cafe.

Fixed broadband in apartments reaches 50-100 Mbps on fiber in most central neighborhoods. Mobile 4G covers the greater Tunis metro reliably, with average download speeds around 49 Mbps. 5G launched commercially in early 2025. The infrastructure is solid enough for video calls and standard remote work, though speeds are moderate compared to eastern European or southeast Asian hubs.

Getting Around

Tunis has a metro (light rail), buses, and taxis. The metro network covers key corridors and costs under 1 TND per ride. Monthly transit passes cost about 40 TND (~$13) and cover buses and metro. The system is affordable but can be crowded and slow during peak hours.

Taxis are abundant and metered. A cross-city trip rarely exceeds 10-15 TND (~$3-$5) during the day. Confirm the meter is running before departing. As of mid-2025, ride-share apps including Bolt had their licenses suspended by the Tunisian government, so you rely on street taxis.

Walkability varies sharply by neighborhood. The medina and central Tunis are dense and walkable. Lac district and Centre Urbain Nord are car- and transit-oriented and less pleasant on foot.

Getting to and from Tunis-Carthage Airport: Bus lines 35 and 635 run to the city center (Tunis Marine station) for about 1 TND, operating from roughly 06:00 to 18:00. After hours, take a metered taxi for around 25-35 TND. The airport is about 8 km northeast of downtown; a taxi ride takes 15-25 minutes outside peak traffic.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Tunisia is visa-free for most Western passport holders on arrival. US, UK, EU (most member states), Canadian, and Australian citizens can enter for up to 90 days without a visa. Germans receive a 4-month allowance. No advance application, no fee: you simply show your passport at the border.

There is no dedicated digital nomad visa for Tunisia as of mid-2026. After 90 days, you need a residence permit, obtained through the local district police station (commissariat). Extensions are possible but require paperwork and patience; many nomads do a border run to renew the 90-day allowance.

Always verify current entry rules with the official Tunisian embassy or consulate for your nationality before traveling, as policies can change.

Best Time to Visit

Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the best windows for remote work. Temperatures sit between 19-28C (66-82F), skies are clear, and the city is not overwhelmed with summer tourists. These shoulder seasons combine comfortable working conditions with lower accommodation prices.

Summer (June to August) is hot, with temperatures regularly exceeding 35C (95F) and occasional heat waves pushing higher. Coastal areas get crowded and prices rise. Air-conditioned coworking spaces make it manageable, but evenings outdoors lose their appeal.

Winter (December to February) is mild and rainy. Daytime temperatures of 12-17C (54-63F) are workable, and crowds thin out considerably. January sees the most rainfall. For budget travelers or those who dislike heat, winter is underrated.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Very low cost of living, well below European and most Latin American nomad hubs
  • 90-day visa-free entry for US, EU, UK, and many other passports
  • Fast 4G coverage citywide and fiber available in most apartments
  • Mediterranean coast, ancient ruins, and Sahara day trips all accessible from Tunis
  • Rich food culture with excellent value at local restaurants
  • Short flights to European cities (Rome, Paris, Frankfurt under 3 hours)
  • Growing coworking scene with community-focused spaces

Cons

  • English is rarely spoken; French is essential for daily life outside tourist areas
  • No digital nomad visa; 90-day limit requires planning for longer stays
  • Small nomad community compared to Southeast Asia or eastern Europe
  • Ride-share apps suspended as of 2025; taxi meter disputes are common
  • Nightlife is limited and alcohol availability varies by neighborhood (Lac district is largely dry)
  • Petty theft is a real concern in the medina and on public transport
  • Banking and payment infrastructure lags; many places are cash-only

Who Tunis Is Best For

Tunis suits nomads who prioritize low costs and an authentic, non-touristy base. If you speak French or are willing to learn basics, you will navigate daily life far more easily and build genuine local connections. It is a strong fit for freelancers and remote workers with flexible schedules who want proximity to Europe without European prices, and who are drawn to a city with real history, good food, and a Mediterranean climate.

Tunis also works well for longer-stay slow travelers who want to explore North Africa deeply: day trips reach Carthage, Sidi Bou Said, El Djem, and even the Sahara. The city rewards patience over instant convenience.

Skip Tunis if you need a large, English-speaking nomad community and plug-and-play convenience. It is not a city where everything is optimized for foreigners. If reliable high-speed internet above 100 Mbps matters for your work, test speeds in your specific neighborhood before committing. Those who need predictable nightlife, a wide bar scene, or a city that runs smoothly at night will find Tunis frustrating.

Frequently Asked Questions About Tunis

How much does it cost to live in Tunis as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,200 a month in Tunis for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Tunis?

Tunis rates 5 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Tunis safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Tunis is 5/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Tunis a good city for digital nomads overall?

Tunis stands out for low costs and culture among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,200 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
