Setting Up as a Nomad in Siena

A medieval Tuscan city built around the shell-shaped Piazza del Campo, all brick towers, Gothic art, and rolling vineyard hills. For remote workers, Siena leans on food and culture, with a typical budget near $1,700 a month. The main trade-off is the size of the nomad scene.

Il Campo and Centro

Around the famous shell-shaped square

The heart of Siena around Piazza del Campo, the fan-shaped square hosting the Palio and ringed by cafes and the Torre del Mangia. Central to all three terzi, it is the most atmospheric and connected base, though busy with visitors year round.

Best for: Visitors wanting to live steps from Siena's iconic square and sights

Terzo di Camollia

Residential old town

Northern district with local everyday life

The northern third of the historic center, stretching along Via Banchi di Sopra toward Porta Camollia. More residential and workaday than Il Campo, it holds shops, trattorias and university life, offering a genuine local feel within the walls and close to the train connections.

Best for: Longer stays wanting local life inside the walls near transport

Terzo di Citta

$$$
Elegant cultural quarter

Cathedral quarter of grand palaces

The southwestern third around the Duomo, Santa Maria della Scala and the Pinacoteca, filled with medieval palaces and cultural sights. Elegant and central yet slightly quieter than Il Campo, it suits those who want art and architecture on their doorstep.

Best for: Culture lovers wanting cathedral and museums within a short walk

Terzo di San Martino

$$

Eastern third of quiet contrade

The eastern third running down toward Porta Romana, home to contrade like Leocorno and Nicchio and the Logge del Papa. Residential and less trafficked, with everyday shops and views over the Tuscan hills, it gives a calmer base still walkable to the center.

Siena is not a budget destination. Tuscany carries a premium, and the compact historic centre attracts steady tourism that lifts prices for rent, dining, and daily errands. A single nomad should plan on roughly $1,500 to $2,000 a month, with housing the biggest variable: short lets inside the medieval walls cost more than flats in the newer neighbourhoods just outside. Groceries at local markets are reasonable, and a simple trattoria lunch remains fair value, yet cafes and restaurants on the main squares charge for the view. The figures below are editorial estimates rather than fixed quotes, and they shift with season and how central you choose to live.

  • Rent, one-bedroom flat: roughly $900 to $1,300 a month, higher inside the walls
  • Groceries for one: roughly $250 to $350 a month from local shops and markets
  • Casual meal out: roughly $15 to $30 per person at a trattoria
  • Monthly transport and extras: roughly $80 to $150, buses and occasional trips

Where to Work in Siena

Siena is a small, studious city rather than a coworking hub, so set expectations accordingly. Home and short-let internet is generally fine for calls, video, and everyday remote work, though you should confirm speeds before booking a longer stay. Dedicated coworking spaces are few, reflecting the modest nomad scene, so many remote workers lean on quiet cafes, the calm of a rented flat, or the studious atmosphere that a historic university town naturally provides. The University of Siena keeps the place full of students, libraries, and unhurried cafes suited to focused sessions. If you need a buzzing coworking community, you will find more of it in Florence, an easy trip away by bus.

Getting Around Siena

The historic core is compact, walled, and largely pedestrian, so you will cover most of daily life on foot. Be ready for limestone hills and stepped lanes: the centre rises and falls, and comfortable shoes matter more than any transport pass. Local and regional buses connect the centre with outlying neighbourhoods and nearby Tuscan towns, and buses are generally the most reliable way to reach Florence. Direct train links are more limited, so many travellers prefer the coach. The nearest airports are Florence and Pisa, each reachable by bus or connecting transport within a couple of hours. A car helps for exploring the Chianti and Crete Senesi countryside, but it is unnecessary inside the town.

Visas for Siena

Siena sits in Italy, inside the Schengen area, so the usual rules apply. Many visitors arrive on the standard Schengen allowance of 90 days within any 180-day period, which permits tourism but not local employment. Italy launched a digital nomad visa in 2024 aimed at remote workers and highly skilled professionals employed or contracted outside the country, and this is the route for longer, legitimate stays while working online. Requirements typically cover minimum income, health insurance, and proof of remote work, and details can change. Working locally on a tourist entry is not allowed. Always verify the current terms through official Italian government and consular channels before you plan a move, since rules and processing vary.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit Siena

Spring and autumn are the sweet spots. From April to June and again in September and October, the weather is mild, the surrounding hills are green or golden, and the crowds are thinner than at peak. Summer brings heat and the busiest tourist flows, sharpened around the Palio horse race run in the Piazza del Campo on 2 July and 16 August, when the contrade fill the city and accommodation is scarce and dear. Those dates are thrilling to witness but chaotic for settling in to work. Winter is quiet and atmospheric, with cool days, short daylight, and far fewer visitors, ideal if you value calm over lively streets.

Pros and Cons of Siena

Siena rewards a particular kind of nomad and frustrates others, so weigh it honestly. The upsides are the setting, the food, the safety, and the unhurried rhythm of a beautifully preserved Tuscan hill town. The downsides are cost, a very small remote-work community, limited nightlife, and transport that favours buses over fast trains. Read the lists below before committing to a longer stay.

  • Pro: stunning UNESCO medieval centre, the Piazza del Campo, the Duomo, and the Torre del Mangia
  • Pro: outstanding Tuscan food and wine, from pici pasta to Chianti in the surrounding hills
  • Pro: safe, walkable, and calm, with a studious university-town atmosphere
  • Con: not cheap, with tourist pricing on central squares and premium rents inside the walls
  • Con: tiny nomad scene, few coworking spaces, and quiet nightlife
  • Con: no direct airport and limited fast trains, so you rely on buses to Florence and Pisa

Who Siena Is For

Siena suits the nomad who wants art, history, and Tuscan slow living rather than a party scene or a big community of fellow remote workers. If you dream of morning coffee on a medieval square, long lunches, weekend drives through the Crete Senesi and Chianti, and evenings spent reading rather than bar-hopping, this city delivers deeply. It rewards independent travellers who are comfortable making their own routine and do not need beaches, nightlife, or a ready-made nomad crowd. If you crave a large scene, cheap living, or coastal energy, look elsewhere, perhaps a bigger Italian city or a coastal base. For culture, calm, and food, Siena is hard to beat.
