Setting Up as a Nomad in Siena

A medieval Tuscan city built around the shell-shaped Piazza del Campo, all brick towers, Gothic art, and rolling vineyard hills. For remote workers, Siena leans on food and culture, with a typical budget near $1,880 a month. The main trade-off is the size of the nomad scene.

Best Neighborhoods in Siena

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Il Campo and Centro

$$$
Historic tourist heart

Around the famous shell-shaped square

The heart of Siena around Piazza del Campo, the fan-shaped square hosting the Palio and ringed by cafes and the Torre del Mangia. Central to all three terzi, it is the most atmospheric and connected base, though busy with visitors year round.

Best for: Visitors wanting to live steps from Siena's iconic square and sights
Pros
  • Iconic central location
  • Everything walkable
  • Endless cafes and dining
Cons

  • Limited quiet

Terzo di Camollia

$$
Residential old town

Northern district with local everyday life

The northern third of the historic center, stretching along Via Banchi di Sopra toward Porta Camollia. More residential and workaday than Il Campo, it holds shops, trattorias and university life, offering a genuine local feel within the walls and close to the train connections.

Best for: Longer stays wanting local life inside the walls near transport
Pros
  • More local and calmer
  • Good shops and eateries
  • Near station links
Cons
  • Some uphill walks
  • Still tourist-priced

Terzo di Citta

$$$
Elegant cultural quarter

Cathedral quarter of grand palaces

The southwestern third around the Duomo, Santa Maria della Scala and the Pinacoteca, filled with medieval palaces and cultural sights. Elegant and central yet slightly quieter than Il Campo, it suits those who want art and architecture on their doorstep.

Best for: Culture lovers wanting cathedral and museums within a short walk

  • Beautiful streets

Cons

Terzo di San Martino

Eastern third of quiet contrade

The eastern third running down toward Porta Romana, home to contrade like Leocorno and Nicchio and the Logge del Papa. Residential and less trafficked, with everyday shops and views over the Tuscan hills, it gives a calmer base still walkable to the center.

Siena is not a budget destination. Tuscany carries a premium, and the compact historic centre attracts steady tourism that lifts prices for rent, dining, and daily errands. A single nomad should plan on roughly $1,500 to $2,000 a month, with housing the biggest variable: short lets inside the medieval walls cost more than flats in the newer neighbourhoods just outside. Groceries at local markets are reasonable, and a simple trattoria lunch remains fair value, yet cafes and restaurants on the main squares charge for the view. The figures below are editorial estimates rather than fixed quotes, and they shift with season and how central you choose to live. In a city with this many students, much of the flat stock is let on Italy's student lease, which runs six months to three years and is reserved for enrolled students; a remote worker on a three-month stay needs the transitorio lease of one to eighteen months, which must state the reason for the short term, or else pays holiday-let prices.

  • Rent, one-bedroom flat: roughly $900 to $1,300 a month, higher inside the walls
  • Groceries for one: roughly $250 to $350 a month from local shops and markets
  • Casual meal out: roughly $15 to $30 per person at a trattoria
  • Monthly transport and extras: roughly $80 to $150, buses and occasional trips

Where to Work in Siena

Siena is a small, studious city rather than a coworking hub, so set expectations accordingly. Home and short-let internet is generally fine for calls, video, and everyday remote work. Dedicated coworking spaces are few, reflecting the modest nomad scene, so many remote workers lean on quiet cafes, the calm of a rented flat, or the studious atmosphere that a historic university town naturally provides. The University of Siena keeps the place full of students, libraries, and unhurried cafes suited to focused sessions. If you need a buzzing coworking community, you will find more of it in Florence, an easy trip away by bus. The best free desk in town is the Biblioteca comunale degli Intronati on Via della Sapienza, the city library, open to anyone without registration, with free wifi and reading rooms that keep weekday hours into the early evening and open on Saturday mornings. Calls are out in a reading room, so treat it as the place for deep work and keep meetings for the flat.

Getting Around Siena

The historic core is compact, walled, and largely pedestrian, so you will cover most of daily life on foot. Be ready for limestone hills and stepped lanes: the centre rises and falls, and comfortable shoes matter more than any transport pass. Local and regional buses connect the centre with outlying neighbourhoods and nearby Tuscan towns, and buses are generally the most reliable way to reach Florence. Direct train links are more limited, so many travellers prefer the coach. The nearest airports are Florence and Pisa, each reachable by bus or connecting transport within a couple of hours. A car helps for exploring the Chianti and Crete Senesi countryside, but it is unnecessary inside the town. The railway station sits below the city, and a chain of escalators climbs from it to the Antiporto at Porta Camollia, though the system has had long closures, so check whether it is running before arriving with heavy luggage.

Visas for Siena

Siena sits in Italy, inside the Schengen area, so the usual rules apply. Many visitors arrive on the standard Schengen allowance of 90 days within any 180-day period, which permits tourism but not local employment. Italy launched a digital nomad visa in 2024 aimed at remote workers and highly skilled professionals employed or contracted outside the country, and this is the route for longer, legitimate stays while working online. Requirements typically cover minimum income, health insurance, and proof of remote work. Working locally on a tourist entry is not allowed. Florence's small airport, the closer of Siena's two gateways, takes few flights from outside Schengen beyond British Airways from London and Air Serbia from Belgrade, and passengers off those are registered by the Entry/Exit System in its arrivals hall, about an hour and a quarter from Siena by road.

Best Time to Visit Siena

Spring and autumn are the sweet spots. From April to June and again in September and October, the weather is mild, the surrounding hills are green or golden, and the crowds are thinner than at peak. Summer brings heat and the busiest tourist flows, sharpened around the Palio horse race run in the Piazza del Campo on 2 July and 16 August, when the contrade fill the city and accommodation is scarce and dear. Those dates are thrilling to witness but chaotic for settling in to work. Winter is quiet and atmospheric, with cool days, short daylight, and far fewer visitors, ideal if you value calm over lively streets. The race itself lasts little more than a minute, but the build-up runs for days: the horses are drawn by lot on the morning three days before, six trial races follow in the square, and the trials are free to watch if you take a place before the track is cleared.

Pros and Cons of Siena

Siena rewards a particular kind of nomad and frustrates others, so weigh it honestly. The upsides are the setting, the food, the safety, and the unhurried rhythm of a beautifully preserved Tuscan hill town. The downsides are cost, a very small remote-work community, limited nightlife, and transport that favours buses over fast trains. Daily life here runs through the contrade, the seventeen wards behind the Palio, each with its own church, museum and fountain, and their clubs stay busy all year rather than only in summer. On the eve of each Palio, the ten wards that run open their streets for the dinner of the prova generale, where guests can book a seat at long tables beside the members. On the other side of the ledger, the hills are steeper than photographs suggest: the Campo sits in a bowl, so every errand ends with a climb.

  • Pro: stunning UNESCO medieval centre, the Piazza del Campo, the Duomo, and the Torre del Mangia
  • Pro: outstanding Tuscan food and wine, from pici pasta to Chianti in the surrounding hills
  • Pro: safe, walkable, and calm, with a studious university-town atmosphere
  • Con: not cheap, with tourist pricing on central squares and premium rents inside the walls
  • Con: tiny nomad scene, few coworking spaces, and quiet nightlife
  • Con: no direct airport and limited fast trains, so you rely on buses to Florence and Pisa

Who Siena Is For

Siena suits the nomad who wants art, history, and Tuscan slow living rather than a party scene or a big community of fellow remote workers. If you dream of morning coffee on a medieval square, long lunches, weekend drives through the Crete Senesi and Chianti, and evenings spent reading rather than bar-hopping, this city delivers deeply. It rewards independent travellers who are comfortable making their own routine and do not need beaches, nightlife, or a ready-made nomad crowd. If you crave a large scene, cheap living, or coastal energy, look elsewhere, perhaps a bigger Italian city or a coastal base. For culture, calm, and food, Siena is hard to beat. Riders who like gravel get the white roads of the Strade Bianche, the professional race that finishes in the Campo each March, starting almost at the city's edge.

Frequently Asked Questions About Siena

How much does it cost to live in Siena as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Siena is around $1,700 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Siena?

Siena rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Siena safe for digital nomads?

Siena scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Siena a good city for digital nomads overall?

Siena stands out for its food scene and culture among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,700 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
