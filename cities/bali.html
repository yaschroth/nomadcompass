What Bali (Canggu) Offers Digital Nomads

Experience surf, sunsets, and smoothie bowls, the Instagram-famous nomad destination. For remote workers, Bali (Canggu) leans on a large nomad community and nature, with a typical budget near $1,340 a month. The main trade-off is cleanliness.

Canggu is one of Bali's more expensive areas, and rents have climbed roughly 10 to 25 percent a year since 2021, so treat these numbers as a moving target. A private one-bedroom villa or apartment in central Canggu typically runs $525 to $1,050 a month, and a mid-range villa with a pool starts around $1,050 to $1,770. If you want to spend less, a kos (a simple boarding-house room with air conditioning, an en-suite bathroom, and WiFi) starts near $78 a month, though the cheapest local kos are increasingly reserved for Balinese tenants.

Food is where Bali stays affordable if you eat local. A plate at a warung (a small local eatery) runs $1 to $2, while a Western-style cafe meal is more like $3 to $5. Eating mostly at warungs can keep food near $250 a month, whereas leaning on cafes and Western restaurants pushes it past $700. Groceries land around $165 to $250 a month for one person, with local produce cheap and imported goods pricey.

A coworking hot-desk day pass is roughly $10 to $15, and monthly memberships run about $150 and up. A rented scooter is $55 to $110 a month. Altogether, most nomads spend $900 to $1,500 a month on a modest setup, while a comfortable private-villa lifestyle with daily eating out and a social calendar runs $1,800 to $2,500 or more.

Where to Work

Canggu has a dense coworking scene. Outpost runs 24/7 locations with day passes from about $15 and flexible hour-based monthly plans. Tropical Nomad charges around $10 a day and roughly $150 a month for a hot desk, with dedicated desks a little higher. Dojo is the long-running name in Canggu; note that its original beachside space closed and the brand now operates from an Echo Beach location, so confirm the current address before you commit. In Ubud, Outpost Ubud and Hubud serve the quieter crowd, and Seminyak has its own cafe-and-coworking mix.

Cafe-work culture is strong here, and many nomads bounce between laptop-friendly cafes rather than paying for a desk. That works until it does not. WiFi across Canggu, Ubud, and Seminyak is generally decent, with 50 to 100 Mbps common in villas and coworking spaces, but reliability is the real issue. Storm-related outages happen, villa fiber often tops out around 100 Mbps, and a busy cafe on shared WiFi can crawl. Many long-stay nomads hedge with a local SIM for hotspotting or, increasingly, Starlink, which launched in Indonesia in 2024 and delivers roughly 50 to 200 Mbps in practice. If your income depends on stable video calls, plan a backup connection rather than assuming one line will always hold.

Getting Around

In Canggu the scooter is king, and it is the fastest way to move between beaches, cafes, and rice-field lanes. Daily rentals run $4 to $5.50, dropping sharply on weekly and monthly deals. Be honest with yourself about the risk: Bali's roads are chaotic, many riders are inexperienced, accidents are common, and a serious crash without proper travel insurance and an International Driving Permit can be financially ruinous. Wear a real helmet, ride slowly, and do not ride drunk.

If you would rather not drive, Gojek and Grab both run cheap ride-hailing for cars and scooter-taxis. Download both and compare per trip, since Gojek is often cheaper and Grab sometimes has cars when Gojek does not. Traffic is a genuine drawback: the Canggu, Seminyak, and Ubud corridors clog badly in late morning and late afternoon, and a short distance can eat 30 minutes. From Denpasar airport (DPS), Canggu is about 45 minutes to 1.5 hours depending on traffic. A pre-booked transfer runs roughly $17 to $25; Gojek and Grab cannot always pick up at the arrivals curb, so you may need to walk out of the airport zone to meet a driver.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

The Visa on Arrival (VoA, code B1) gives most nationalities 30 days for $28, and it can be extended once for another 30 days, so 60 days total. As of June 2025 you must attend the immigration office in person for extensions. For longer stays without an extension run, the B211A visit visa allows up to 60 days and can be extended, and it is also the entry route many people use before applying onshore for a KITAS.

The headline option for remote workers is the E33G Remote Worker KITAS, Indonesia's digital-nomad residence permit rolled out in 2024. It grants a one-year stay for people working remotely for companies registered outside Indonesia. Typical requirements include a passport valid at least 12 months, a remote work contract with a foreign employer, bank statements showing about $2,000, and proof of income around $60,000 a year. You cannot earn from any Indonesian entity, and the E33G is not renewable, so you close it and reapply from abroad to continue. Overstaying any visa costs $55 per day, so track your dates. Visa rules change often, so confirm current requirements with official Indonesian immigration sources or a reputable agent before you apply.

Best Time to Visit

Bali has two seasons. The dry season runs April to October, with sunny days, lower humidity, and temperatures around 27 to 32 degrees Celsius, and it is the best window for beaches, surfing, and reliable outdoor plans. The wet season runs November to March, with January and February the wettest; expect heavy afternoon downpours, more humidity, and beaches in the south (including Canggu) that collect washed-up debris during December through February.

For remote work, the shoulder months of May, June, and September hit the sweet spot: good weather, thinner crowds, and slightly easier rents. Plenty of nomads stay through the wet season too, since the rain often comes in short bursts and the island is greener and quieter, but plan for the occasional storm-related power or internet outage.

Pros and Cons

  • Large, established nomad community with easy networking and events
  • Local food, kos rooms, and warungs keep a lean budget genuinely cheap
  • Excellent coworking options and strong cafe-work culture
  • Warm weather year-round, surf, and a healthy wellness and fitness scene
  • A generous one-year remote-worker KITAS now exists for those who qualify
  • Heavy traffic in Canggu, Seminyak, and Ubud, with short trips taking far too long
  • Overtourism and fast development have eroded the quiet many came for, and rents keep rising
  • Scooter accidents are common and can be severe; road safety is a real hazard
  • WiFi is decent but not bulletproof, with storm outages and variable villa speeds
  • Wet-season rain and dirty southern beaches from December to February

Who Bali Is Best For

Bali suits nomads who want an active social scene, warm weather, cheap local living, and a ready-made community, and who are comfortable riding a scooter or budgeting for ride-hailing. It rewards people who eat local, build in an internet backup, and treat road safety seriously. It works well for surfers, wellness-minded remote workers, and anyone wanting to test the new E33G long-stay route.

Skip it if you need dead-reliable infrastructure for high-stakes video work with no fallback, if traffic and crowds will wear you down, or if you are uneasy on two wheels and want walkable, transit-friendly streets. If you crave quiet over buzz, consider Ubud over Canggu, or look at a calmer destination entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions About Bali (Canggu)

How much does it cost to live in Bali (Canggu) as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Bali (Canggu) is around $1,340 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Bali (Canggu)?

With a WiFi score of 6/10, connectivity in Bali (Canggu) is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Bali (Canggu) safe for digital nomads?

Bali (Canggu) scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Bali (Canggu) a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Bali (Canggu) is worth a look if you value a large nomad community and nature. Costs sit near $1,340 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
