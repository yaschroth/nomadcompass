Setting Up as a Nomad in Denver

A mile-high city where mountains meet craft beer culture. A continental climate and strong English make Denver an easy base, and at about $4,200 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep cost in mind.

Cost of Living in Denver

Denver sits in the middle of the US cost spectrum: pricier than the Midwest, cheaper than the coasts. A wave of new apartment construction has softened rents recently, so you have more room to negotiate than you did a couple of years ago. Here is a realistic monthly picture for one person in USD.

  • Rent, central one-bedroom: roughly $1,600-1,900 in walkable areas near downtown, LoDo, or RiNo.
  • Rent, cheaper area: around $1,100-1,400 for a one-bedroom in neighborhoods like Capitol Hill or further out, and less if you share.
  • Groceries: about $400-550 if you cook most meals.
  • Eating out: a mid-range restaurant main runs around $16, and a full night out with drinks lands closer to $40-50 per person.
  • Coworking hot desk: day passes average around $30-40, or roughly $75-200 per month for a membership depending on how often you go.
  • Transport: budget $100-150 a month, more if you run a car with parking and insurance.

Add utilities, a phone plan, and a health buffer, and a comfortable solo budget usually falls between $2,600 and $4,000 a month. Live with roommates, cook at home, and skip the car, and you can push toward the lower end. Rent in a central high-rise and eat out often, and you will clear the top.

Where to Work

Denver has a solid coworking scene spread across downtown and the RiNo and LoDo districts. Named options include Creative Density (hot desking from around $75 per month part-time), Alchemy Creative Workspace (day passes near $25), Shift Workspaces, Serendipity Labs in LoDo, and WeWork at Tabor Center with day passes around $39. Several spaces offer a free trial day, so you can test a few before committing.

Cafe-work culture is genuinely strong here, especially in RiNo, the Highlands, and Capitol Hill, where independent roasters are used to laptops and long stays. WiFi in coworking spaces and established cafes is fast and reliable. As always, do not count on video-call quality in a busy cafe at peak hours, and keep a phone hotspot as backup for anything important.

Getting Around

Denver is served by RTD (the Regional Transportation District), which runs light rail, commuter rail, and an extensive bus network. The system works well along its main corridors and around downtown, but coverage thins out in the suburbs, so many locals still rely on a car for daily life. If you base yourself centrally and stick to walkable neighborhoods, you can manage without one.

Getting to and from Denver International Airport (DEN) is easy: the RTD A Line commuter train links the airport to Union Station downtown in about 37 minutes, running from early morning to past midnight, with trains roughly every 15 minutes during the day. Confirm the current fare on the RTD site, as the airport zone costs more than a standard ride. A car is most useful if you plan frequent mountain trips, since the ski towns and trailheads west of the city are far easier to reach by road.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

If you hold a passport from a Visa Waiver Program country (most of Western Europe, the UK, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and others), you can usually enter the United States for tourism or business for up to 90 days with an approved ESTA, no visa required. Apply online before you fly, ideally well ahead of departure. Note that the ESTA fee increased in late 2025, so check the current amount when you apply.

The 90-day Visa Waiver stay cannot be extended, and it is not designed for working locally for US clients. For longer stays or any work arrangement you will need an appropriate visa. US citizens and permanent residents, of course, can stay indefinitely. Always confirm your exact situation with official US sources (travel.state.gov and the CBP ESTA site), as rules change.

Best Time to Visit

Denver is one of the sunniest cities in the country, with more than 300 days of sunshine a year and four distinct seasons. Winters are cold and bring heavy but short-lived snowstorms, often followed by clear, mild days that melt the snow fast, so the ground is frequently bare even in January. Summers are warm, dry, and packed with festivals and live music.

For remote work, the sweet spots are the shoulder seasons: April to May and September to October bring comfortable temperatures, thinner crowds, and better value than peak summer. One thing to plan for regardless of season is the altitude. At 5,280 feet, the air is thin and dry, and it is normal to feel short of breath, tire quickly, or get headaches for the first few days. Drink far more water than usual, go easy on alcohol early on, and give yourself a few days before any hard hikes.

Pros and Cons

The honest tradeoffs:

  • Pro: Exceptional access to the outdoors, with the Rockies, skiing, and world-class trails within easy reach.
  • Pro: Over 300 sunny days a year and a low-humidity, energetic climate.
  • Pro: Strong coworking and cafe scene with fast, reliable WiFi.
  • Pro: Easy airport connection by train and a growing, walkable city core.
  • Con: Altitude takes real adjustment and can leave you drained for the first week.
  • Con: Outside the central corridors, life leans heavily on owning a car.
  • Con: Costs, especially rent, have risen and still run above the national average.
  • Con: Winters are genuinely cold, with snow and icy stretches to work around.

Who Denver Is Best For

Denver suits remote workers who want an active, outdoorsy base and will actually use the mountains on weekends. If skiing, hiking, biking, and sunshine matter more to you than nightlife density, you will feel at home. It also works well for anyone who wants a clean, functional US city with a real coworking scene and an easy airport.

Skip Denver if you want a fully car-free lifestyle across the whole metro, if cold snowy winters are a dealbreaker, or if you are sensitive to altitude and would rather not spend your first week adjusting. Budget-focused nomads chasing the lowest possible costs will also stretch their money further elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions About Denver

How much does it cost to live in Denver as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Denver is around $4,200 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Denver?

With a WiFi score of 8/10, connectivity in Denver is excellent for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Denver safe for digital nomads?

Denver scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Denver a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Denver is worth a look if you value English and nature. Costs sit near $4,200 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
