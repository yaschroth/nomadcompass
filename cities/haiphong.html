Setting Up as a Nomad in Hai Phong

Vietnam's third city and its main northern port, a low-key French-colonial grid with the best banh da cua and a fast ferry to Cat Ba. Hai Phong pairs low costs with safety and a humid subtropical climate, all for about $700 a month. If anything, the size of the nomad scene lags.

Best Neighborhoods in Hai Phong

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Hong Bang

$$$
Central & Colonial

French colonial core around the Opera House

Hong Bang is the administrative and commercial heart of Hai Phong, built around the ochre Opera House, the flower-lined Tam Bac lake and wide boulevards of French colonial villas. Ground floors hold banks, bakeries, coffee chains and the city's better restaurants. It is where visiting businesspeople and most short-stay foreigners end up.

Best for: Nomads who want a walkable centre with proper cafes and the widest choice of serviced apartments.
Pros
  • Most walkable district with lakes and shaded boulevards
  • Best cafe and coworking-friendly coffee shop density
  • Close to the train station and buses to Ha Long
Cons
  • Highest rents in the city
  • Motorbike traffic and horns from early morning
  • Pavements are largely occupied by parked bikes

Ngo Quyen

$$
Urban & Local

Busy midtown with the best street food

Ngo Quyen stretches east from the centre toward the river port, packed with market streets, banh mi que stalls, seafood restaurants and the Vincom Plaza mall. Newer apartment towers sit alongside old shophouses, so you can get a modern flat without paying colonial-quarter prices. It is where most working Hai Phong residents actually live.

Best for: Nomads who want good value modern apartments with real Vietnamese street life outside.
Pros
  • Best street food in the city, meals around $2
  • Modern towers with lifts and reliable fibre
  • Ten minute ride to the colonial centre
Cons
  • Genuinely noisy, markets start before 5am
  • Air quality suffers from port and truck traffic
  • Almost no English outside the mall

Le Chan

$
Residential & Affordable

Residential southwest with parks and cheap rent

Le Chan covers the southwestern city, a grid of ordinary residential streets, local markets, the Nu Tuong Dai park area and the big Lach Tray stadium. Housing is mostly tube houses and mid-rise blocks aimed at families, and rents drop noticeably compared with the centre. Days are quiet and life runs on local prices.

Best for: Long-stay nomads who want a quiet family neighbourhood and rents well under the centre.
Pros
  • Clearly cheaper rents than Hong Bang
  • Quiet residential streets, good for calls
  • Local markets and gyms without tourist pricing
Cons
  • Few cafes or coworking options nearby
  • Needs a motorbike, walking distances are long
  • Streets flood in heavy summer rain

Hai An

$$
Modern & Spacious

New east-side developments near the airport

Hai An is the eastern district holding Cat Bi airport and the newest planned developments, with wide new roads, gated compounds and modern serviced apartments. It is spacious and orderly compared with the old centre, and popular with Korean and Japanese staff working at nearby factories. Everything is new, which means good buildings and little atmosphere.

Best for: Nomads who fly often and want a new, quiet apartment over old-town character.
Pros
  • Newest housing stock with the best internet
  • Ten minutes to Cat Bi airport
  • Korean and Japanese restaurants and supermarkets
Cons
  • Aircraft noise on some approach paths
  • Little street life, you drive everywhere
  • 20 minutes to the colonial centre

Kien An

$
Budget & Suburban

Cheapest option across the Lach Tray river

Kien An lies southwest across the Lach Tray river, half small-town and half city, with a hill park, university campuses and low-rise streets of local shops. Rents are the lowest of any district within reach of the centre and food prices match. Expect almost no foreigners and a strongly local daily rhythm.

Best for: Very budget-conscious nomads happy to trade a river crossing for the lowest rent around.
Pros
  • Lowest rents within reach of the city
  • Kien An hill park and greener surroundings
  • Student area keeps food and services cheap
Cons
  • Bridge traffic makes the commute unpredictable
  • Very limited cafes, gyms or English services
  • Older infrastructure and patchier internet

Cost of Living in Hai Phong

Hai Phong is one of Vietnam's better-value large cities, and $700 a month is comfortable rather than tight. A furnished serviced studio or one-bedroom in Le Chan, Ngo Quyen or central Hong Bang costs $200 to $330, often with cleaning included; older local apartments go for $130 to $200. Street food is the backbone of the budget: banh da cua at $1.50, com binh dan plates at $2, bia hoi under $0.50 a glass, so $150 to $220 covers food with occasional Western meals at $8 to $12. A Viettel or Vinaphone SIM with abundant data runs $4 to $8 monthly. Dedicated coworking is limited, with desks around $60 to $110 where available. Electricity, water and fibre together come to $35 to $70, higher in the humid summer. Grab trips are $1 to $3. Verdict: strong value, with the trade-off being fewer nomad-specific amenities.

Where to Work in Hai Phong

Central Hong Bang around the Tam Bac lake and the French colonial theatre district is the most walkable base, with Le Chan and Ngo Quyen offering cheaper apartments and dense local food. The city's cafe culture is deep, and Vietnamese chains do the heavy lifting: Cong Caphe, Highlands Coffee and Trung Nguyen Legend branches all have air conditioning, power outlets and workable wifi, while independent roasters along Dien Bien Phu and Lach Tray streets suit longer sessions. Formal coworking is scarce compared with Hanoi or Da Nang, so most remote workers combine a good apartment fibre line with cafes. Fibre performance is solid at 60 Mbps to 300 Mbps, though Vietnam's reliance on undersea cables means periodic international slowdowns even when domestic speeds look fine. Caveats: cafes get loud and smoky in the evening, seating tightens at lunch, and typhoon season brings genuine power and internet outages.

Getting Around Hai Phong

Hai Phong is flat and moderately walkable in the centre, but the city sprawls and the heat and traffic push most people onto two wheels. Grab dominates, alongside the electric Xanh SM fleet: motorbike trips cost $0.60 to $1.50 and car rides $2 to $5 across town. Monthly scooter rental is $45 to $70 and makes life considerably easier, though not strictly essential given app coverage. Cat Bi International Airport lies only about 7km from the centre, roughly a $4 to $7 car ride, with domestic flights nationwide and regional connections. The train to Hanoi is a local favourite: around 2.5 hours for $3 to $6, arriving at Long Bien. Limousine vans to Hanoi cost $7 to $10. Ferries and fast boats from Ben Binh pier and Got connect to Cat Ba island for $5 to $15, the main reason many nomads choose this city.

Visas for Hai Phong

Vietnam runs one of Asia's easiest entry systems. Since August 2023 the e-visa is available to all nationalities, valid up to 90 days, single or multiple entry, applied for online at the official portal for about $25 single and $50 multiple, usually approved within three to five working days. Separately, citizens of the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and several Nordic and Asian countries receive a visa exemption of 45 days, with periodic extensions to that list, so checking the current rules before booking matters. There is no digital nomad visa. Extensions inside Vietnam are possible through agencies but are inconsistent and increasingly discouraged, so most long-stayers simply exit to Cambodia, Thailand or Laos and return on a fresh e-visa, which is legal and routine. Working for a Vietnamese employer requires a work permit and temporary residence card, a separate and much slower process.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit Hai Phong

Hai Phong has a northern Vietnamese climate with four distinct seasons, unlike the tropical south. October and November are the best months: dry, clear, with highs of 25C to 29C and low humidity, ideal for Cat Ba and Ha Long Bay trips. April and early May are the second window, warm and green before the heat sets in. December to March is cool and often grey, with temperatures of 14C to 20C and a persistent drizzle known as mua phun that keeps everything damp; heating is rare in apartments. June to August is hot and sticky at 32C to 36C with high humidity and heavy afternoon downpours. The typhoon window runs from July to September, and Hai Phong takes direct hits, as Typhoon Yagi demonstrated in 2024. Local highlights include Tet in January or February, and the red flamboyant flower festival in May.

Pros and Cons of Hai Phong

Hai Phong offers genuine value: low rents, some of the best street food in Vietnam including banh da cua and nem cua be, a walkable French colonial core, an airport seven kilometres from downtown, cheap trains to Hanoi, and ferries to Cat Ba and Ha Long Bay for weekends. Prices have not been inflated by tourism, locals are welcoming, and the pace is calmer than Hanoi or Saigon. On the other side, this is an industrial port city rather than a nomad hub, so the international community is small, coworking options are thin, and English is less widely spoken than in Da Nang or Hoi An. Air quality is poor in winter, the damp cold season is genuinely unpleasant indoors, traffic is chaotic, typhoons cause real outages between July and September, there is no true beach in the city, and long-stay visa options remain limited.

Who Hai Phong Is For

Hai Phong suits the budget-focused, self-sufficient nomad who has already done Vietnam's obvious stops and wants low costs, authentic food and easy access to Ha Long Bay and Cat Ba without paying tourist prices. It works for solo developers, writers and traders who need little more than a fast apartment line, for people with business or manufacturing interests in the northern industrial belt, and for anyone who enjoys big-city Vietnamese life without a foreigner bubble. Skip it if a ready-made nomad community, frequent meetups and a choice of coworking spaces are what make a base work, if beaches are the priority, or if damp grey winters and poor winter air quality are dealbreakers. Those who want the same country with more infrastructure should compare Da Nang; those who want more culture and nightlife should compare Hanoi, two hours away by train.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hai Phong

How much does it cost to live in Hai Phong as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Hai Phong is around $700 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Hai Phong?

Hai Phong rates 7 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is good for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Hai Phong safe for digital nomads?

Hai Phong scores 8 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Hai Phong a good city for digital nomads overall?

Hai Phong stands out for affordability and safety among remote workers. With a typical budget near $700 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
