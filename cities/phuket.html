Phuket at a Glance for Remote Workers

Thailand's largest island with beaches, diving, and island hopping. What pulls nomads here is nature, backed by nightlife and food. Budget around $2,400 a month. Cleanliness is the weak spot.

Cost of Living in Phuket

Phuket is more expensive than Chiang Mai or Bangkok but remains affordable by Western standards. Here is a realistic monthly breakdown for a solo digital nomad living comfortably:

  • Rent (central areas like Patong, Kata, Bang Tao): 15,000-25,000 THB/month (~$420-700 USD) for a furnished studio or one-bedroom with air conditioning and Wi-Fi
  • Rent (quieter areas like Rawai, Chalong, Nai Harn): 8,000-15,000 THB/month (~$220-420 USD) for similar quality; this is where most long-term nomads base themselves
  • Groceries: 3,000-5,000 THB/month (~$85-140 USD) if you shop at local markets and Makro; more if you rely on imported goods at Villa Market
  • Eating out: Local Thai street food and market meals run 60-120 THB ($1.70-3.40) per meal; mid-range restaurants cost 200-400 THB ($6-11) per person
  • Coworking hot-desk: 3,000-6,500 THB/month (~$85-185 USD); day passes range from 150-350 THB ($4-10)
  • Local transport (scooter rental): 3,000-4,000 THB/month (~$85-115 USD); petrol adds another 500-800 THB
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet at home): 2,000-5,000 THB/month (~$55-140 USD); air conditioning in hot months pushes this higher

Realistic monthly total: 35,000-65,000 THB/month (~$1,000-1,850 USD) for a comfortable lifestyle. Budget travelers can get by on 28,000 THB ($800 USD) with discipline; those wanting beach clubs, western dining, and private pools will spend significantly more.

Where to Work

Phuket has a solid and growing coworking infrastructure, concentrated in the south and west of the island. The best-regarded dedicated spaces include Denz CoWorking Cafe in Kathu (1 Gbps fibre, mountain views, on-site cafe), Grind Time with locations in Chalong Bay and Rawai (community-focused, 24/7 access, around 4,000 THB/month), and Place Coworking in Chalong (five floors, rooftop, 24/7). HOMA in Phuket Town suits nomads who want coliving bundled with workspace.

Cafe-work culture is real but uneven. Spots like Dreamily Coffee Bar and Slow Thrive in Rawai welcome laptop workers, but many beachside cafes have slow or unreliable Wi-Fi. Always ask before settling in for a long session.

The honest Wi-Fi picture: dedicated coworking spaces offer 100-1,000 Mbps and are reliable enough for video calls. Cafes and accommodations outside tourist-facing areas can drop to 10-30 Mbps with inconsistent uptime. A local SIM card (AIS or DTAC) with a 5G data plan is a smart backup; unlimited plans run about 500-700 THB/month.

Getting Around

Phuket is a large island (roughly 50 km by 20 km) and is not walkable beyond small pockets like Phuket Old Town. Most nomads rent a scooter for 200-250 THB per day or 3,000-4,000 THB per month. It is the most practical option but comes with real risk: tourist scooter accidents are common, roads can be poorly lit, and rain makes the island's hilly roads slippery. An international driving permit and a helmet are non-negotiable.

Grab (the regional ride-hailing app) works well in Phuket and is the honest alternative when you do not want to ride. Expect to pay 100-300 THB for most intra-area trips. Taxis exist but drivers often refuse to use meters, so agree on a price upfront or use Grab instead. The Phuket Smart Bus runs along the west coast from the airport to Rawai for 100-170 THB and is air-conditioned and reliable.

From the airport to Patong takes about 45 minutes by Grab or taxi (600-900 THB) or around 60-75 minutes on the Smart Bus. Rawai is closer to 60-70 minutes by car. There is no rail or metro.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Most Western passport holders (EU, UK, US, Australian, Canadian) receive a 30-day visa-exempt entry, extendable once at a local immigration office for 1,900 THB giving you 60 days total. As of late 2025, Thailand has begun cracking down on travelers doing frequent back-to-back border runs; more than two in a year can result in denial of entry.

Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV), launched in 2024, is the clearest option for nomads. It is a 5-year multiple-entry visa allowing stays of up to 180 days per entry. Requirements include proof of remote income or foreign employment and a bank balance of at least 500,000 THB (~$14,000 USD) for the preceding three months. The government fee is 10,000 THB (~$280 USD). There is no official "digital nomad visa" by that name, but the DTV functions as one in practice.

The Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa is a 10-year option for higher earners with a minimum foreign income of $40,000-$80,000 USD per year. Always confirm current entry rules directly with the Thai embassy or official immigration sources before travel, as policies change frequently.

Best Time to Visit

Phuket has two distinct seasons. The dry season runs from November to April, with November through February being the sweet spot: clear skies, calm Andaman Sea, temperatures of 25-30°C, and low humidity. December through February is peak tourist season so accommodation prices rise sharply and popular coworking spaces can fill up. March and April remain dry but grow noticeably hotter and more humid.

The rainy season runs from May through October. Expect afternoon downpours, rougher seas, and occasional storms. September is the wettest month. For remote work specifically, the rainy season is underrated: accommodation is cheaper by 30-50%, crowds thin out significantly, and you can still get solid work done between showers. The tradeoff is grey days, some business closures, and occasional flooding in low-lying areas.

For the best combination of weather and value, November and early December or March are ideal. If budget is your priority and you can tolerate rain, June through August offers good value with less extreme weather than September.

Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Stunning natural setting with beaches, jungle, and clear water within easy reach
  • Pro: Well-developed coworking infrastructure, especially in the Rawai-Chalong corridor
  • Pro: Excellent food at all price points, from 60-baht street noodles to Michelin-recognised restaurants
  • Pro: Large international community means English is functional in most nomad areas
  • Pro: World-class Muay Thai gyms, yoga studios, and fitness options
  • Pro: Good local SIM coverage and reliable backup connectivity
  • Con: Not walkable; you need a scooter or ride-hailing for almost everything
  • Con: More expensive than Chiang Mai or Bali for similar quality of life
  • Con: Patong specifically is loud, congested, and tourist-heavy; not a productive base
  • Con: Rainy season can be genuinely disrupting with storms and service closures
  • Con: The visa-run workaround for long stays is increasingly scrutinised by immigration
  • Con: Traffic is chaotic and scooter risk is real; accidents are common among tourists

Who Phuket Is Best For

Phuket works best for nomads who want a beach lifestyle without sacrificing reliable infrastructure. If you value having coworking options, a well-established expat community, good food diversity, and easy access to outdoor activities like diving, surfing, or hiking, Phuket delivers. The Rawai and Chalong areas in particular suit longer stays: quieter, more affordable than the tourist strip, and with a genuine community of people who work remotely.

It is also a solid choice for nomads on the DTV who want to base themselves in Southeast Asia for multiple months per year, given the visa allows 180-day stays and the infrastructure supports sustained deep work.

Skip Phuket if you need rock-bottom costs (Chiang Mai or cheaper Southeast Asian cities will serve you better), if you have zero interest in beach or outdoor culture, or if you want a highly walkable urban environment. It is not the right base if you are trying to stretch a tight budget while doing serious heads-down work with zero distractions.

Frequently Asked Questions About Phuket

How much does it cost to live in Phuket as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $2,400 a month in Phuket for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Phuket?

Phuket rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work workable. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Phuket safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Phuket is 6/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Phuket a good city for digital nomads overall?

Phuket stands out for nature and nightlife among remote workers. With a typical budget near $2,400 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
