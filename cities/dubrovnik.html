Why Dubrovnik Works for Remote Work

The Pearl of the Adriatic where medieval walls meet crystal-clear waters. A mediterranean climate and strong nature make Dubrovnik an easy base, and at about $2,800 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep the size of the nomad scene in mind.

Cost of Living in Dubrovnik

Dubrovnik is Croatia's most expensive city, and prices swing hard with the season. Croatia adopted the euro in 2023, so everything below is in euros with rough US dollar equivalents (about $1.08 per euro in 2025 and 2026). A realistic solo budget lands somewhere around 1,700 to 2,700 euros ($1,850 to $2,900) per month, depending on where you rent and how much of the year you overlap with peak summer.

Here is a monthly breakdown for one person:

  • Rent, central one-bedroom: roughly 650 to 900 euros ($700-970), and sea-view or old-town-adjacent flats push well past 1,000 euros. Short summer lets cost far more, so aim for a longer off-season lease.
  • Rent, cheaper area: 500 to 700 euros ($540-760) in districts like Gruz or the outer suburbs, away from the tourist core.
  • Groceries: around 220 to 270 euros ($240-290), higher than mainland Croatia because much is shipped in.
  • Eating out: a casual meal runs 12 to 20 euros ($13-22); old-town restaurants are tourist-priced, so budget 350 to 450 euros ($380-490) monthly if you eat out often.
  • Coworking hot desk: expect roughly 100 to 200 euros ($110-215) for a monthly flexible membership, though the small local scene means you should confirm current rates directly.
  • Local transport: a single Libertas bus ride is 1.73 euros bought in advance at a kiosk or 2.50 euros ($1.90-2.70) from the driver; a 24-hour ticket is 5.31 euros.

Add utilities, a SIM, and coworking, and a comfortable central life sits near 2,200 to 2,700 euros ($2,400-2,900). Rent cheaper and cook at home and you can hold it closer to 1,700 euros ($1,850).

Where to Work

The dedicated coworking scene is tiny. The main purpose-built option is Desk & More in the Gruz district on Lopudska ulica, near the ferry port and main bus station, offering flexible hotdesks and workstations with 27-inch monitors, free printing, a meeting room for calls, air conditioning, and fast WiFi. International operators Regus and Spaces also run flexible offices and hot desks in Dubrovnik if you want a more corporate setup. Beyond that, options thin out fast, so do not expect a big nomad-hub choice.

Cafe-work culture is limited. Dubrovnik cafes are built around slow coffee and tourists, not laptops, and staff in the old town may not love you camping at a table during a busy lunch. You will have better luck in quieter Gruz and Lapad spots. WiFi is generally reliable across the city and in most modern apartments, but confirm speeds before booking a longer stay, and keep a mobile data plan as backup for video calls.

Getting Around

The walled old town is compact and entirely pedestrian, so you will cover it on foot, up and down a lot of stone steps. For everything outside the walls, the Libertas bus network connects the old town, Gruz, Lapad, and the suburbs. Buy tickets in advance at kiosks or Tisak newsstands to get the cheaper 1.73-euro fare; card payment is accepted. Uber and Bolt both operate here and are handy when buses thin out at night.

From Dubrovnik Airport (DBV), which sits about 20 kilometers southeast of the city, the Platanus shuttle bus is the simplest link. It is timed to flight arrivals, departs roughly 30 minutes after each landing, stops at the Ploce Gate by the old town, and continues to the Gruz bus station. The ride takes about 30 to 35 minutes and costs 10 euros one way ($11) or 15 euros for an open return. Taxis, Uber, and private transfers are the alternative if you land late or have heavy bags.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Croatia is in the Schengen Area, so the standard rules apply. Citizens of the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and many other countries can stay visa-free for up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the whole Schengen zone, not just Croatia. That is the default for most short and medium stays.

For longer, Croatia offers a digital nomad residence permit for non-EU/EEA citizens who work remotely for foreign companies or clients. As of a March 2025 change, it can be granted for up to 18 months. You will need to show a minimum income (recently around 3,300 euros per month, updated annually), valid health insurance, a clean criminal record, and proof of accommodation. Importantly, time on the permit does not count against your Schengen 90/180 allowance, though trips to other Schengen countries still do. Rules and income thresholds change, so confirm the current requirements with the Croatian Ministry of the Interior or an official government source before you apply.

Best Time to Visit

Dubrovnik has a Mediterranean climate: hot, dry summers around 27 to 30 degrees Celsius (low-to-mid 80s Fahrenheit) and mild, sometimes rainy winters near 10 degrees Celsius (around 50 Fahrenheit). Snow is rare.

The best windows for remote work are the shoulder seasons, roughly May to early June and mid-September to mid-October. You get warm weather, lighter crowds, a still-swimmable sea in autumn, and more reasonable rents. July and August are the opposite: brutal heat, packed streets, cruise-ship surges, and peak prices, so avoid them if you actually need to work. Winter is genuinely quiet, cheap, and calm, but many old-town restaurants and services close, and the town can feel deserted, so it suits you only if you want deep focus over social life.

Pros and Cons

The honest picture:

  • Pros: stunning walled old town and coastline; reliable WiFi; easy euro and Schengen access; a genuine 18-month nomad permit for non-EU citizens; excellent shoulder-season weather; very safe; walkable historic core.
  • Cons: expensive by Croatian standards; severe summer overtourism driven by cruise ships and Game of Thrones fame; a tiny nomad and coworking scene; steep, stepped terrain everywhere; dead, half-shuttered winters; short summer rentals are pricey and hard to lock down.

Who Dubrovnik Is Best For

Dubrovnik suits an independent remote worker who values scenery, safety, and a walkable old-world setting over community, and who plans around the shoulder seasons rather than summer. It works well if you already have your own routine and clients and do not need a nomad crowd to plug into. It also fits non-EU nomads who want a longer, tax-friendly base in the EU via the residence permit.

Skip it if you want a large, buzzing nomad community, cheap living, or reliable year-round energy. Budget travelers, people who crave a big coworking and social scene, and anyone who cannot tolerate summer crowds should look at Split, Zagreb, or elsewhere on the Croatian coast instead. Dubrovnik is a beautiful base for the right, self-sufficient person, but it is not a plug-and-play nomad hub.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dubrovnik

How much does it cost to live in Dubrovnik as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Dubrovnik is around $2,800 a month: a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. Live a little outside the center or eat local to spend less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Dubrovnik?

With a WiFi score of 6/10, connectivity in Dubrovnik is workable for calls and focused work. A prepaid local SIM keeps you online when you are away from the apartment.

Is Dubrovnik safe for digital nomads?

Dubrovnik scores 9 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travel and long stays. Use normal precautions at night and with valuables, and choose well-reviewed areas.

Is Dubrovnik a good city for digital nomads overall?

For nomads, Dubrovnik is worth a look if you value nature and safety. Costs sit near $2,800 a month and the community keeps growing, though, like anywhere, it will not match every checklist.
