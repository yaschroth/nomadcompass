Setting Up as a Nomad in San Marino

The oldest republic in the world, on a limestone crag above the Italian plain. A mediterranean climate and strong safety make San Marino an easy base, and at about $1,900 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep the size of the nomad scene in mind.

Best Neighborhoods in San Marino

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Citta di San Marino

$$$
Medieval & Elevated

The walled old town on the summit of Monte Titano

The historic capital on the ridge of Monte Titano at around seven hundred metres, a UNESCO World Heritage site of stone streets and three fortified towers on the crag, with views across Romagna to the Adriatic about twenty kilometres away.

Best for: Anyone who wants to live inside the postcard
Pros
  • A UNESCO World Heritage site you can walk daily
  • Views to the Adriatic and inland to the Apennines
  • The government, museums and towers all on foot
Cons
  • Two million day visitors a year on the same streets
  • Steep, and everything is uphill from the car park
  • Very little housing, held tightly and expensively

Borgo Maggiore

$$$
Local & Connected

The market town below the old capital, linked by cable car

The town on the slope directly below the capital, connected to it by a cable car that takes under two minutes, holding the country's main weekly market and a good deal of its more ordinary housing. It is more lived-in than the town above it.

Best for: Anyone who wants daily life with the old town two minutes above
Pros
  • Cable car to the capital in under two minutes
  • The country's main weekly market
  • Cheaper and less visited than the summit
Cons
  • Still on a steep slope, with limited flat ground
  • Fewer restaurants than the capital
  • Traffic on the road up passes through it

Serravalle

$$
Commercial & Flat

The largest and busiest municipality, on the plain

The most populous of the nine castelli, on the flat ground towards the Italian border, holding the country's industry, its retail parks, the football stadium and most of its newer apartment blocks. It is where the majority of San Marino's economy actually sits.

Best for: Longer stays that want space, parking and lower rents
Pros
  • Cheapest housing in the republic
  • Flat ground, shops and easy parking
  • Quickest access to Rimini and the Italian motorway
Cons
  • Industrial and commercial rather than scenic
  • Ten minutes of climbing road to the old town
  • Little evening life

Dogana

$$
Border & Practical

The border quarter where the country meets Italy

The settlement at the frontier with Italy, continuous with the Italian town of Rimini's hinterland and holding the bus terminus for the service down to Rimini station. It is the practical entry point to the republic and the least ceremonial part of it.

Best for: Anyone commuting to Rimini or relying on the bus
Pros
  • The bus to Rimini and its railway station starts here
  • Supermarkets and services on both sides of the border
  • Lowest rents within the republic
Cons
  • No character and nothing historic
  • Traffic through the border is constant
  • Twelve kilometres of climbing to the old town

Cost of Living in San Marino

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers San Marino, so the $1,900 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 1000 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Budget around $1,900 a month as a solo remote worker in San Marino, which is broadly similar to the surrounding Italian region and slightly cheaper on some things because the republic sets its own indirect taxes. A one-bedroom flat runs roughly $600 to $1,000, and much of the affordable housing is in the lower towns of Borgo Maggiore and Dogana rather than on the mountain itself. Groceries are Italian-priced, which is to say reasonable. The euro is the currency although San Marino is not in the European Union. This is our own estimate rather than measured data.

Where to Work in San Marino

San Marino's connectivity is good: fibre reaches the populated areas at solid speeds and mobile coverage is comprehensive, with the surrounding Italian networks also reaching most of the republic. Power is reliable. There is very little coworking, since the republic has almost no visiting professional population. Italian is the only language of daily life and administration; English is limited outside the tourism economy on the mountain. The republic sits in central European time and functions economically as part of Emilia-Romagna. Anyone working with the rest of Europe will find no offset at all, since the republic keeps central European time like the country entirely surrounding it.

Getting Around San Marino

San Marino is built on and around Monte Titano, and the historic city on top is entirely pedestrian, reached by road, by a cable car from Borgo Maggiore below, or on foot up a serious climb. The republic has no airport and no railway; the old electric line from Rimini closed in 1944 and its tunnels are still visible. Buses run to Rimini in about fifty minutes, which is where the nearest station and the coast are. Bologna airport is around two hours away. Rimini's beaches are close enough that a great many Sammarinese treat the Adriatic coast as their summer, and the bus runs frequently in season.

Sorting transport? Book an airport transfer, or compare car rental on Auto Europe and QEEQ.

Visas for San Marino

San Marino is not in the European Union and not formally in Schengen, but it has open borders with Italy on every side and no border controls at all, so in practice reaching it means entering Schengen through Italy and the day count applies there. There is no remote work visa. Residence in San Marino is tightly controlled and generally requires either substantial investment or a genuine employment relationship with a Sammarinese company, and the process is slow. Anyone counting Schengen days should treat time in San Marino as time in Italy, since there is no border and no separate record of entry.

Best Time to Visit San Marino

May, June, September and October are the best months, with warm dry weather, clear views across to the Adriatic and fewer of the day-trippers who come up from the Rimini beaches in summer. July and August are hot and the mountain is busy. Winter is cold and often in cloud at this altitude, with the city on the ridge frequently sitting above or inside a bank of fog while the plain below is clear, which is either atmospheric or oppressive depending on the day. The Medieval Days festival at the end of July fills the old city with re-enactment and crossbow competitions, which is the republic's largest event.

Pros and Cons of San Marino

San Marino claims to be the oldest surviving sovereign state and the oldest constitutional republic in the world, founded by a stonemason in the fourth century, and its three towers on the ridge of Monte Titano are visible for fifty kilometres. It is safe, cheap by Western European standards, and the views across to the Adriatic are exceptional. Against that, it is tiny, Italian is essential, the historic centre is largely given over to duty-free shops and tourist retail, and there is no airport or railway. The republic also issues its own stamps and coins, minted in euro but with Sammarinese designs, which are collected far beyond the country itself.

Who San Marino Is For

San Marino suits an Italian speaker who wants a very quiet hilltop base within an hour of the Adriatic coast and two of Bologna, and who is spending a Schengen allowance rather than trying to settle. The republic is more pleasant out of season than its reputation suggests, once the day-trippers have gone. It is a poor fit for anyone without Italian, anyone needing a community, and anyone hoping the sovereignty translates into an easier residence route than Italy's, which it does not. It also suits anyone cycling in Emilia-Romagna, since the climb up Monte Titano is a serious one and the descent to the coast is thirty kilometres.

Frequently Asked Questions About San Marino

How much does it cost to live in San Marino as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in San Marino is around

,900 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in San Marino?

San Marino rates 8 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is excellent for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is San Marino safe for digital nomads?

San Marino scores 10 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is excellent for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is San Marino a good city for digital nomads overall?

San Marino stands out for safety and cleanliness among remote workers. With a typical budget near ,900 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
