Cost of Living in Bucharest

Bucharest is one of the most affordable EU capitals for remote workers. A comfortable solo lifestyle runs roughly 4,500-6,800 RON per month (about $950-1,450 USD), depending on where you live and how you eat.

  • Rent, 1-bed central (Universitate, Floreasca, Dorobanti): 2,500-3,500 RON/month (~$530-745)
  • Rent, 1-bed outside center (Tineretului, Titan, Militari): 1,800-2,400 RON/month (~$380-510)
  • Groceries: 600-900 RON/month (~$125-190) at local supermarkets like Lidl or Mega Image
  • Eating out: A sit-down lunch costs 40-70 RON (~$8-15); dinner with drinks, 80-150 RON (~$17-32)
  • Coworking hot-desk: 400-900 RON/month (~$85-190); day passes typically 50-80 RON
  • Metro monthly pass: ~160 RON (~$34); single ride 3 RON (~$0.64)
  • Mobile data: 30-60 RON/month (~$6-13) for a generous local SIM

Monthly total range: 4,500-6,800 RON (~$950-1,450 USD). Add a premium apartment and daily restaurant meals and you can push toward $1,800. Live modestly and cook most meals and you can stay under $1,000.

Where to Work

Bucharest's internet infrastructure is genuinely exceptional. Romania consistently ranks among the top countries in Europe for fixed broadband, with typical home fiber speeds of 300-1,000 Mbps. If you rent an apartment, fiber is standard and included or priced at around 40-60 RON/month extra.

For dedicated workspace, Impact Hub Bucharest Universitate (near Piata Universitatii) is the most established coworking option, with 2,800 sqm, private offices, phone booths, and a strong community of local startups. V7 Startup Studio operates two boutique spaces: V7 Opera (Cotroceni) and V7 Athenaeum (near Calea Victoriei), with a creative, design-forward atmosphere. Mindspace offers two upmarket locations with polished fit-outs and reliable high-speed connections.

Cafe work culture is active but uneven. Spots like Saint Roastery (Floreasca), Handsome Monk Coffee Roastery (near Dorobanti), and M60 (Piata Romana area) are genuinely laptop-friendly with consistent WiFi and plenty of power outlets. The Old Town cafes are better for socializing than deep work due to noise and table turnover pressure on busy nights. For video calls, a coworking space is a better bet than a cafe.

Getting Around

The metro is clean, fast, and cheap. Four lines cover most of the central city and connect the main transport hubs. A single tap costs 3 RON; a monthly pass is roughly 160 RON. Contactless card payment works at metro gates.

Street-level traffic is a real problem. Bucharest has heavy congestion during rush hours and infrastructure that has not kept pace with the number of cars. Walking short distances in the center works well, but the city is not particularly pedestrian-friendly once you move beyond the historic core. Bike lanes are sparse and often blocked. Rideshare apps (Bolt and Uber both operate here) are affordable and widely used.

Airport (Henri Coanda, OTP): The express train (Henri Coanda Express) runs directly to Gara de Nord station in about 20-25 minutes for 14.5 RON. From Gara de Nord you can take metro line M1. A taxi or Bolt to the center takes 30-50 minutes depending on traffic and costs around 60-100 RON.

Walkability in the center is reasonable for daily errands, but the city rewards those with a metro pass or a reliable rideshare app over those who try to walk everywhere.

Visas and Staying Long-Term

Romania joined the Schengen Area in January 2025. Most Western passport holders (US, UK, Canada, Australia, and similar) can enter visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, now counted within the broader Schengen zone.

For stays beyond 90 days, Romania offers a dedicated Digital Nomad Visa (long-stay D visa) aimed at non-EU remote workers. Key requirements as of 2026: proof of remote employment or self-employment outside Romania, a minimum monthly income of roughly 18,000 RON (~3,700 EUR, updated annually), health insurance, and confirmed accommodation. The application fee is 120 EUR. On arrival you apply for a temporary digital nomad residence permit, initially valid for 6 months and renewable. Holders benefit from Schengen free movement for up to 90 days in any 180-day period in other Schengen states.

EU and EEA citizens can live and work in Romania freely. Always verify current requirements with the Romanian Embassy or the official Romanian Immigration portal before applying.

Best Time to Visit

Bucharest has a continental climate with hot summers and cold, occasionally harsh winters. The sweet spots for remote work are April to June and September to October.

Spring (April-May) brings mild temperatures of 17-22°C (63-72°F), low humidity, and the city's parks and gardens at their best. Early autumn (September-October) is similarly pleasant, with average highs of 20-25°C (68-77°F) and fewer crowds than peak summer. Both windows mean comfortable cafe and outdoor work sessions without needing air conditioning or heating.

July and August are hot, often reaching 35-40°C (95-104°F), with afternoon thunderstorms and a city that partially empties as locals head to the coast or mountains. Winter (December-February) brings freezing temperatures, grey skies, and occasional heavy snow. The city functions, but it is not enjoyable for those without a warm base to work from.

Pros and Cons

Pros
  • Some of the fastest and most affordable internet in Europe, standard in apartments
  • Low cost of living by EU standards with a full range of amenities
  • Excellent and cheap metro system with contactless payment
  • English widely spoken by younger residents and in business districts
  • Vibrant food and cafe scene with a growing specialty coffee culture
  • Easy day trips to Sinaia and the Carpathian mountains (under 2 hours)
  • Flat 10% income tax rate and a dedicated digital nomad visa pathway
Cons
  • Heavy traffic congestion makes car travel and some areas stressful
  • Air quality can be poor in autumn and winter, occasionally reaching unhealthy levels
  • Old Town nightlife noise is disruptive if you live or stay nearby
  • Heating in older apartment buildings can be unreliable and costly in winter
  • The nomad community is small compared to cities like Lisbon or Tbilisi
  • Bureaucracy for the digital nomad visa requires careful document preparation

Who Bucharest Is Best For

Bucharest suits budget-conscious remote workers who want EU-standard infrastructure at Eastern European prices. If you prioritize fast internet, affordable rent, a credible food scene, and easy access to nature on weekends, it delivers well. It also works for anyone who wants a legal long-term EU base with the Romanian digital nomad visa as a stepping stone.

Experienced Europe-based nomads who want a quieter, underrated destination to park for a few months will find Bucharest underestimated. History and architecture enthusiasts will appreciate the layers of the city, from Belle Epoque buildings to communist-era boulevards.

It is a less natural fit for nomads who prioritize a built-in community of other digital nomads, a walkable compact city, or a beach or mountain backdrop. If you need warm weather year-round or want a city that feels energetically social from day one, you may find Bucharest requires more effort to crack than destinations like Split, Lisbon, or Chiang Mai.

Frequently Asked Questions About Bucharest

How much does it cost to live in Bucharest as a digital nomad?

Budget roughly $1,800 a month in Bucharest for a furnished place, food, coworking, and transport. The most popular central areas cost more; suburbs and local spots cost noticeably less.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Bucharest?

Bucharest rates 9 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so it handles video calls and daily remote work excellent. Carry a local SIM with data as a backup for cafes and travel days.

Is Bucharest safe for digital nomads?

Our safety score for Bucharest is 6/10, workable for nomads. As anywhere, watch your belongings, favor busier streets after dark, and research neighborhoods before you book.

Is Bucharest a good city for digital nomads overall?

Bucharest stands out for fast WiFi and nightlife among remote workers. With a typical budget near $1,800 a month and a growing nomad scene, it suits anyone whose priorities line up with those strengths.
