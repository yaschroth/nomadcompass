Setting Up as a Nomad in New Delhi

India's capital and its densest layer cake of history, from Mughal tombs and Lutyens boulevards to Old Delhi's street food lanes. A semi-arid climate and strong culture make New Delhi an easy base, and at about $900 a month it fits a range of budgets. Keep air quality in mind.

Best Neighborhoods in New Delhi

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Hauz Khas

$$$
Creative & Social

Lakeside ruins with the densest cafe scene in South Delhi

A 13th century madrasa and a deer park sit right beside rooftop cafes, design studios and boutique co-working floors. The lanes of Hauz Khas Village are narrow and crowded at night, while the surrounding blocks of Hauz Khas Enclave are leafy, quiet and full of barsati apartments rented by designers and students.

Best for: Freelancers and creatives who want cafes, culture and a lake walk within ten minutes of the desk.
Pros
  • Highest cafe and co-working density in South Delhi
  • Green Park and Hauz Khas metro both walkable
  • Deer Park gives a real morning run route
Cons
  • Village lanes get loud and rowdy after 9pm
  • Parking and traffic near the village are chaotic
  • Winter smog is severe from November to January

Greater Kailash

$$$$
Upscale & Residential

Wide leafy streets and the M Block market crowd

GK-1 and GK-2 are planned residential colonies of two and three storey builder floors on tree lined roads, wrapped around the M Block and N Block markets. Families, returning NRIs and long stay professionals dominate. It feels calm on a weekday morning and turns into a shopping and dining hub by evening.

Best for: Nomads staying three months or more who want a proper apartment rather than a hostel bed.
Pros
  • Quiet, well maintained residential streets
  • Excellent markets, clinics and gyms nearby
  • Strong stock of furnished builder floor rentals
Cons
  • Rents are among the highest in South Delhi
  • Very few walkable co-working options
  • Metro access needs an auto ride from inner blocks

Saket

$$$
Practical & Convenient

Malls, hospitals and metro, all in one grid

Saket pairs quiet DDA housing blocks with the Select Citywalk and DLF mall cluster, Max hospital and a straight run on the Yellow Line. The streets are wide and car oriented rather than charming, but everything practical is close, which is why long stay remote workers keep landing here.

Best for: Remote workers who value reliable infrastructure, hospitals and metro over street character.
Pros
  • Direct Yellow Line metro into central Delhi
  • Malls, cinemas and top hospitals within walking distance
  • Quieter and greener than the village areas
Cons
  • Bland, mall driven streetscape with little local texture
  • Mall area traffic snarls on weekends
  • Air quality is poor through the winter months

Connaught Place

$$$
Central & Walkable

The colonial white colonnades at the centre of everything

CP is Delhi's original central business district, a set of white Georgian colonnades in concentric circles around Central Park. Offices, airline counters, old coffee houses and serviced workspaces fill the blocks, and three metro lines converge underneath. Almost nobody lives inside the circles, so most stays are serviced apartments in the radial roads nearby.

Best for: Short stay nomads who want maximum metro access and meetings across the city.
Pros
  • Three metro lines interchange at Rajiv Chowk
  • Dense cluster of paid co-working desks
  • Genuinely walkable colonnades and Central Park
Cons
  • Persistent touts and scams aimed at foreigners
  • Very little real residential housing
  • Noise and exhaust from constant traffic on the circles

Lajpat Nagar

$$
Budget & Bustling

Central Market bargains and the cheapest South Delhi rents

Built around the packed Central Market, Lajpat Nagar is a working, bargaining, food stall kind of place with tightly stacked four storey blocks behind the shops. Rents run well below Hauz Khas or GK, and Violet Line metro plus the Ring Road put you a short hop from most of South Delhi.

Best for: Budget nomads who want cheap South Delhi rent and do not mind constant street noise.
Pros
  • Cheapest decent rents in the South Delhi belt
  • Violet Line metro and Ring Road connections
  • Superb street food and low cost tailoring and repairs
Cons
  • Market crowds and horns from morning to late night
  • Few quiet cafes suited to a full work day
  • Narrow lanes with heavy parking pressure

Cost of Living in New Delhi

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers New Delhi, so the $900 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 801 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

New Delhi runs comfortably at $900 per month for a single nomad, and can be done for far less. A furnished monthly rental in a good central-south neighbourhood such as Hauz Khas, Safdarjung Enclave, Saket or Greater Kailash costs $300 to $550 for a one-bedroom; barsati rooftop rooms and serviced studios start nearer $200, while Lutyens' Delhi and Vasant Vihar climb well past $800. Food is very cheap: local thalis, street food and home delivery keep monthly eating at $120 to $220, with restaurant meals in Khan Market or Aerocity at $12 to $25 pushing that higher. A Jio or Airtel SIM with generous data is $5 to $10, among the cheapest in the world. Coworking hot desks run $70 to $160. Utilities are $40 to $90, with summer air-conditioning the dominant cost. Metro and cabs total $25 to $50. Value verdict: exceptional purchasing power, with air quality rather than money the real tax on living here.

Where to Work in New Delhi

South Delhi is the sensible base. Hauz Khas and its village, Saket, Greater Kailash and Shahpur Jat combine cafes, metro access and residential calm; Gurugram, technically in Haryana, holds the densest concentration of corporate coworking. WeWork, Awfis, 91springboard, Innov8 and IndiQube all operate multiple Delhi and Gurugram locations, with Awfis and 91springboard the best value. Cafe work is well established at Blue Tokai Coffee Roasters, which has numerous Delhi outlets and reliable wifi, and at the cafes around Hauz Khas Village and Champa Gali in Saidulajab, a converted lane of studios and coffee shops built for exactly this. Fibre broadband from Jio or Airtel delivers 100 to 300 Mbps cheaply and 4G and 5G are fast and inexpensive, so a hotspot is a viable primary connection. No VPN is needed. Power cuts are shorter than they once were but inverters or backup are worth confirming with any landlord, especially in summer.

Getting Around New Delhi

The Delhi Metro is the best in India: clean, air-conditioned, extensive, with fares of about $0.15 to $0.75 and a contactless card or the DMRC app for entry. It is the fastest way across a city where road traffic is severe. Uber and Ola both operate, and Rapido offers cheap motorbike taxis; a typical car ride across South Delhi is $2 to $6, an auto-rickshaw the same trip $1.50 to $4, best booked through an app to avoid meter arguments. Walkability is poor in most areas: footpaths are broken or absent, distances are large and summer heat and winter smog discourage it, though Lodhi Gardens, Khan Market and Hauz Khas are pleasant on foot. Indira Gandhi International Airport connects via the Airport Express in about twenty minutes to New Delhi station for roughly $0.70. Rail from Nizamuddin and New Delhi stations reaches Jaipur and Agra in a few hours. No scooter or car is necessary.

Visas for New Delhi

India offers no digital nomad visa as of 2026, and remote work is done on a tourist entry. The e-Tourist visa is the standard route and is granted online to citizens of most Western countries, typically in 30-day, one-year and five-year variants; the one-year and five-year versions allow multiple entries with a maximum continuous stay of 90 days for most nationalities, extended to 180 days per visit for US, UK, Canadian and Japanese passport holders. Apply only through the official indianvisaonline government portal, since numerous lookalike sites charge inflated fees. Tourist visas cannot generally be extended inside India; the expected pattern is to exit and re-enter, and while the old 60-day re-entry gap was removed years ago, immigration officers still scrutinise back-to-back tourist stays. Employment in India requires a separate business or employment visa. Protected Area Permits apply to parts of the northeast, Ladakh and the Andamans, none of which affect Delhi itself.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit New Delhi

Delhi's year has one clearly good stretch and several difficult ones. October to March is the season, with the sweet spot from late October to early December and again in February and March: dry, sunny, highs of 20C to 28C. Deep winter, late December and January, is cool at 5C to 18C and afflicted by dense fog that delays flights and trains, compounded by the city's worst air pollution, which peaks after Diwali as crop stubble burning coincides with cold stagnant air. April and May are punishingly hot, regularly above 40C and touching 45C, with hot loo winds. The southwest monsoon arrives around late June and runs through September, breaking the heat but bringing humidity, flooding and waterlogged roads. Holi falls in March, Diwali in October or November, and Durga Puja and Dussehra in the autumn; all are worth planning around, and the air after Diwali is the single biggest reason to avoid November.

Pros and Cons of New Delhi

Delhi delivers enormous value and depth. The cost of living is extremely low relative to the quality of housing, food and coworking available, mobile data is world-beating in price and speed, no firewall or VPN complicates work, and English is widely spoken in professional and urban settings, making daily life far easier than in China. The metro is excellent, the coworking sector is mature and competitive, and the historical layers, from Mughal tombs to Lutyens' avenues, are extraordinary. Rail and cheap flights open the whole subcontinent. The overwhelming drawback is air quality: winter pollution in Delhi routinely reaches hazardous levels and represents a genuine health cost, not an inconvenience. Summer heat above 45C is dangerous, monsoon flooding disrupts the city, traffic and noise are relentless, and walkability is poor. Women travellers consistently report Delhi as less comfortable than southern Indian cities. Bureaucracy, scams targeting new arrivals, and inconsistent power and water in cheaper housing add friction.

Who New Delhi Is For

New Delhi fits the nomad who wants maximum purchasing power in a large, English-functional city with real professional infrastructure: founders and freelancers working with Indian teams, journalists and researchers, consultants in tech or development, and long-haul travellers using the capital as a base for the subcontinent. It rewards those who plan around the calendar, arriving in October or February and leaving before the November smog and the May heat. Budget-conscious workers who want a $400 flat, $8 monthly data and $100 coworking will find few equivalents anywhere. It is a poor fit for anyone with asthma, allergies or respiratory sensitivity, for whom Delhi's winter air is disqualifying, and for nomads who prioritise walkability, clean air, quiet or outdoor exercise. Those seeking a gentler Indian introduction should consider Bengaluru, Goa or Pondicherry; those wanting a beach or mountain lifestyle will be happier in Rishikesh, Dharamshala or the coast.

Frequently Asked Questions About New Delhi

How much does it cost to live in New Delhi as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in New Delhi is around $900 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in New Delhi?

New Delhi rates 6 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is average for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is New Delhi safe for digital nomads?

New Delhi scores 5 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is average for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is New Delhi a good city for digital nomads overall?

New Delhi stands out for culture and its food scene among remote workers. With a typical budget near $900 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
