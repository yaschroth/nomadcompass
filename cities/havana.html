Setting Up as a Nomad in Havana

Peeling pastel facades, 1950s Chevrolets and live son on every corner, in a capital where the internet is the one thing that has not improved. Expect $800 a month here. Culture and climate are the standout strengths, with the size of the nomad scene the main caveat.

Best Neighborhoods in Havana

Where to base yourself for the perfect nomad experience

Vedado

$$
Central & Leafy

Leafy 1950s grid with the deepest supply of casas

Vedado is Havana's mid-century district, a numbered grid of shaded streets, faded modernist apartment blocks and grand porticoed houses running down to the Malecon seawall. It holds the greatest concentration of casas particulares with reliable rooms, plus the Hotel Nacional, cinemas, jazz clubs and paladares. Most long-stay visitors end up here.

Best for: First-time long-stayers who want space, quiet nights and everything within a walk or a short taxi ride.
Pros
  • Largest choice of casas and month-long rentals
  • Walkable flat grid with real neighbourhood shops
  • Best cluster of restaurants, bars and live music
Cons
  • Rolling power cuts hit here like everywhere else
  • Street lighting is poor once the sun drops
  • Mobile data is the only realistic internet and it is slow

Habana Vieja

$$$
Historic & Touristy

The restored colonial core, beautiful and busy

Old Havana is the UNESCO-listed colonial quarter of cobbled lanes, baroque churches and four restored plazas, wrapped around a still-crumbling residential centre. Boutique hotels, galleries and cafes with generators sit beside tenements with laundry over the balconies. It is the most photogenic base and by far the most touristed.

Best for: Short stays where atmosphere and walkability matter more than value or peace.
Pros
  • Everything is walkable, no transport needed
  • Most cafes with generators and paid wifi
  • Dense concentration of sights and museums
Cons
  • Constant hustling and inflated tourist prices
  • Loud terraces and music until very late
  • Cramped rooms with weak natural light

Centro Habana

$
Local & Gritty

Raw, lived-in and the cheapest central option

Centro is the tight tenement grid between Old Havana and Vedado, where balconies sag over narrow streets, dominoes are played on the pavement and the Malecon is a short walk north. Rents are the lowest of any central district and daily life is entirely local, with peso markets and hole-in-the-wall pizza counters.

Best for: Budget travellers who want to live among Habaneros rather than beside tour groups.
Pros
  • Cheapest rooms within walking distance of the centre
  • Local peso prices for food and produce
  • Sits between Vedado and Old Havana
Cons
  • Noisy, crowded and short on green space
  • Buildings are poorly maintained, water can cut out
  • Unlit streets warrant care after dark

Miramar

$$$$
Quiet & Upscale

Embassy avenues, gardens and generators

West of the tunnel, Miramar is Havana's diplomatic quarter: wide avenues lined with royal palms, walled 1940s mansions turned embassies, and a handful of business hotels. It is the calmest and greenest part of the city, with a few private clinics, supermarkets carrying imported goods and buildings that often have backup power.

Best for: Remote workers who will pay more for calm, greenery and the steadiest power in the city.
Pros
  • Quietest streets and most reliable electricity
  • Better stocked shops and private clinics
  • Seafront walks without the tourist crush
Cons
  • Spread out, so you rely on taxis daily
  • Priced for diplomats, not for locals
  • Little street life or spontaneous nightlife

Nuevo Vedado

$
Residential & Calm

Residential hillside just south of the action

Nuevo Vedado climbs the slope south of Vedado proper, a mix of low-rise apartment blocks, single-family houses with gardens and the leafy edge of Bosque de La Habana. It is almost entirely residential, with the zoo, Colon cemetery and the Plaza de la Revolucion nearby, and rents noticeably below the Vedado grid.

Best for: Stays of a month or more where a quiet desk and a garden beat being in the thick of it.
Pros
  • Cheaper long-stay rents than Vedado
  • Genuinely quiet, with gardens and trees
  • Ten to fifteen minutes by taxi to the centre
Cons
  • Few restaurants or bars within walking distance
  • Hilly and spread out, transport needed
  • Very little happening after dark

Cost of Living in Havana

Where this figure comes from. No cost survey covers Havana, so the $800 a month above is our own estimate rather than a measurement. On 330 of our 831 city guides the figure is built from a published Numbeo basket, a one-bedroom rent in the centre plus one person's monthly costs without rent, converted to USD; this city is not one of them. Treat it as a starting point and check current rental listings before you budget against it. How we source everything.

Havana at $800 a month is achievable but the cost structure is unlike anywhere else, and much of it depends on the informal exchange rate rather than the official one. A furnished casa particular room or small apartment on a monthly arrangement in Vedado or Centro Habana runs $250 to $500, with Habana Vieja and Miramar higher. Food is the volatile line: state shops are frequently empty, so groceries come from private markets and hard-currency stores at $150 to $300, while a paladar dinner is $8 to $20 and a peso street pizza under $1. ETECSA is the only mobile operator; a tourist data package with a few gigabytes costs $10 to $35 and is the single biggest working expense. Formal coworking barely exists, so budget $0 to $60 for hotel lobby access. Utilities in a rental usually bundle in at $30 to $80. Transport is $30 to $60. Value verdict: cheap headline, expensive friction.

Where to Work in Havana

Havana is the hardest working environment on this list, and expectations should be set accordingly. Vedado is the practical base: leafy, mid-century, close to the Malecón, the university and most of the reliable wifi points. Centro Habana is cheaper and more chaotic; Habana Vieja is beautiful but touristed and noisy; Miramar holds the diplomatic and business infrastructure and the most stable connections. There is essentially no coworking sector. The workable options are hotel lobbies and bars, with the Habana Libre, the Nacional and the larger Miramar hotels offering the steadiest signal, plus ETECSA wifi parks and cafes in Vedado. Mobile data on ETECSA is the main lifeline but speeds are typically a few Mbps, congested in the evening, and video calls often fail outright. Nationwide blackouts and rolling power cuts of several hours are routine, so a power bank and offline-first workflow are essential. Occasional national internet restrictions during unrest are a real risk.

Getting Around Havana

Havana has no metro and no functioning ride-hailing apps in the Western sense; Uber does not operate. The dominant private option is the colectivo, a shared fixed-route almendrón running along arteries such as Línea and Neptuno, costing roughly $0.40 to $1 per person in local pesos. Private taxis negotiate a flat fare: expect $5 to $15 for a crosstown trip, more if you look like a tourist and do not agree the price first. State buses, the guaguas, cost pennies but are overcrowded and unreliable. A local app called La Nave functions in parts of the city but coverage and payment are inconsistent. Vedado, Centro Habana and Habana Vieja are highly walkable and best explored on foot, with broken pavements the main hazard. José Martí airport is 20 km south: a taxi is $25 to $35, fixed by convention. No car needed; renting one is expensive and fuel shortages are chronic.

Visas for Havana

Cuba does not issue a digital nomad visa and there is no remote-work route. Nearly all Western visitors enter on a tourist card, the tarjeta del turista, which is a paper visa bought from an airline, a Cuban consulate or an authorised agency for roughly $25 to $110 depending on source. It grants 30 days for most nationalities and 90 days for Canadians, and can be extended once in country for an equal period at an immigration office, giving a practical maximum of 60 days for most Europeans and 180 for Canadians. Travel medical insurance is mandatory and checked. US citizens face additional constraints: travel must fall under one of the authorised OFAC categories, ordinary tourism is not one of them, and travel to Cuba disqualifies a traveller from later using ESTA visa-waiver entry to the United States, a rule that also applies to Europeans who have visited Cuba since 2021. Working remotely on a tourist card is not formally sanctioned.

General information, not legal advice. Visa rules change often and turn on your nationality, income and reason for staying. Confirm the current requirements with the country’s immigration authority or a qualified immigration adviser before you book anything.

Best Time to Visit Havana

Havana is tropical and warm all year, with a clear dry and wet split. The dry season, November through April, is the best window: highs of 26C to 29C, lower humidity, sea breezes along the Malecón, and the least rain. December to March is the peak and books out. May through October is hot, humid and wet, with July and August reaching 32C or more and feeling considerably worse in buildings without air conditioning, especially during power cuts. Hurricane season officially runs June to November, with the real risk concentrated in September and October; Cuba is directly in the Atlantic track and major storms cause extended outages and damage. Key dates include the Havana Jazz Festival in January, the Havana Biennial in spring on its irregular cycle, the International Film Festival in December, and Carnaval de La Habana in August. Book anything around Christmas and new year far ahead.

Pros and Cons of Havana

The appeal is genuine and almost entirely non-practical. Havana is one of the most visually and culturally extraordinary cities in the Americas: mid-century Vedado, crumbling Habana Vieja, live son and jazz most nights, and street life untouched by chains. Accommodation and food can be cheap, violent crime is minimal, and casa particular hosts provide local access hotels never match. The drawbacks are severe and disqualifying for most remote workers. Internet is slow, expensive, ETECSA-monopolised and prone to national outages during unrest; video calls frequently fail. Blackouts of four to twelve hours are routine, not exceptional. Shortages of food, medicine, fuel and cash are chronic, ATMs often fail, and US-issued cards do not work at all, so hard currency must be carried in. Bureaucracy is opaque, the dual exchange rate confuses constantly, heat without air conditioning punishes half the year, and no nomad community exists.

Who Havana Is For

Havana suits travellers first and remote workers a distant second. It fits writers, photographers, musicians, researchers and anyone whose work is asynchronous, offline-tolerant and unbothered by a week without a stable connection. It rewards people with genuine curiosity about Cuban history, music and politics, patience for improvisation, and a willingness to carry cash, plan around blackouts and accept that nothing runs on schedule. Spanish is close to essential. Stays of a few weeks between projects work far better than a base. It is emphatically wrong for anyone doing client calls, live collaboration, trading, streaming, or any job that assumes reliable bandwidth and power. Skip it if a stable coworking routine, card payments, food variety, air conditioning or a nomad social scene matter, and skip it entirely if a US ESTA or frequent US travel is part of the plan, since a Cuba stamp forfeits visa-waiver eligibility.

Frequently Asked Questions About Havana

How much does it cost to live in Havana as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad budget in Havana is around $800 a month, covering a furnished rental, food, coworking, and getting around. You can spend less by living outside the center and eating local, or more in the most popular neighborhoods.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Havana?

Havana rates 2 out of 10 for WiFi in our scoring, so connectivity is on the limited side for video calls and everyday remote work. A local SIM with a data plan is a smart backup for cafes, coworking, and travel days.

Is Havana safe for digital nomads?

Havana scores 7 out of 10 for safety in our ratings, which is good for solo travelers and long stays. Use the usual precautions with valuables and at night, and favor well-reviewed neighborhoods when you choose where to stay.

Is Havana a good city for digital nomads overall?

Havana stands out for culture and great weather among remote workers. With a typical budget near $800 a month and a growing nomad scene, it is worth a look if those priorities match what you want from a base.
