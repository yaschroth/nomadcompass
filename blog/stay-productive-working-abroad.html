How to Stay Productive While Working Remotely Abroad

Yannick Schroth February 2026 7 min read

Contents

  2. Build a Routine That Travels
  3. Finding Your Workspace
  4. Managing Time Zones
  5. Setting Boundaries
  6. Essential Tools & Apps
  7. Making It Work Long-Term

It's 2 PM. You meant to ship the deliverable by noon, but the "quick walk to find coffee" turned into a three-hour wander through a market you didn't know existed, and now the laptop is still closed on the kitchen table. The same freedom that makes working abroad feel like winning is the freedom that quietly eats your week if nothing holds it in place.

I spent two years shipping code from apartments in Medellin, Chiang Mai, and a dozen places in between, and the lesson that stuck is this: productivity abroad has almost nothing to do with willpower. It comes down to an environment and a handful of habits that survive a new time zone, a strange wifi router, and the smell of someone frying garlic two doors down. Here is what holds up.

The Productivity Challenge Abroad

Working from your home city, you lean on structure you never notice. The desk is where it always is. The morning runs on autopilot. Coworkers expect you online by nine. Move abroad and all of that scaffolding vanishes at once. Each new city hands you a fresh apartment, a fresh wifi password, an unrated coffee shop, and a queue of distractions that did not exist last month.

The first week is reliably the worst. You are wrestling with SIM cards and jet lag while the city outside the window keeps inviting you to come look. That part is fine and expected. The trap is assuming the slump will lift on its own. It will not. I have watched genuinely talented people drift through six months abroad and fall steadily behind, never because they stopped caring, but because they kept running a home-office workflow in a life that changed every few weeks.

Here is the upside. Get the framework right and you can out-produce the version of you that stayed home. Fewer interruptions, full control of your own calendar, and the low hum of living somewhere that still surprises you all push in the same direction, as long as you point them at the work.

Build a Routine That Travels

The biggest single win has been a morning routine that does not care where I woke up. It is almost boringly simple: get up, make coffee or walk to the nearest cafe, spend 20 minutes deciding what actually matters today, then drop into a focused block. The order never changes whether I am in Chiang Mai or Medellin.

The trick is to anchor the routine to actions instead of clocks and addresses. "I work at my desk from 9 to 5" shatters the first time you cross a time zone or move flats. "I do the hardest thing first, break after two hours, then deal with people" travels with you anywhere on the map.

The Two-Block System

My day runs on two deep-focus blocks of 2-3 hours each, with a long gap in the middle. The first block starts within an hour of waking, before the city has a chance to talk me out of it. The second lands after lunch and whatever I did with the break. That is 4-6 hours of real focus a day, more genuine deep work than most office desks produce, and it still leaves the afternoon open for the reason I came here in the first place.

Morning block (high energy): Complex coding, writing, strategic thinking. No Slack, no email. Phone on airplane mode if necessary.

Afternoon block (moderate energy): Meetings, code reviews, communication, lighter tasks. This is when I'm available to my team.

The gap between them is for living, not for guilt. Walk to the market. Sit somewhere with no screen. Eat a lunch you can't pronounce. This is not stealing time from work. It is the thing that makes the afternoon block possible at all.

Finding Your Workspace

Your workspace will decide more about your output than your discipline will, and the postcard fantasy of typing away at a beach bar deserves a quiet burial. Sand jams the keyboard, glare kills the screen, and the wifi reaches exactly as far as the bartender's patience. What you actually need is a chair that doesn't wreck your back, a connection that holds, and a room where focused work is the default mood rather than a heroic exception.

The Three-Option Strategy

In every new city, I establish three workspace options within the first 48 hours:

  • Home base: Your apartment's desk or dining table. Essential for early mornings, late evenings, and days when you just need to grind. Always test the wifi speed before committing to a rental.
  • Coworking space: For days when you need structure, community, and guaranteed fast internet. A good coworking space is worth every cent of the membership fee, especially in cities where cafe wifi can be unreliable.
  • Backup cafe: A tested, laptop-friendly cafe with outlets and decent wifi. For variety and those days when you need a change of scenery without committing to the coworking commute.

With three options in your pocket, you are never actually stuck. Apartment wifi dies, you walk to coworking. Cabin fever sets in, you take the laptop to the cafe. It all sounds like overkill right up until the building loses power 30 minutes before a client call, and you grab your bag and make it anyway.

"The best workspace is the one you'll actually use consistently. A perfect setup you avoid is worse than a mediocre one you show up to every day."

Managing Time Zones

Time zones are the hidden tax on working abroad, and the bill comes due whether or not you read it. They are perfectly manageable, but only if you get ahead of the communication. The worst move is to pretend the gap isn't there and quietly hope nobody on your team does the math.

The Overlap Window

Find the 2-4 hours where your working day actually touches your team's, and guard that window like rent money. That is where meetings live, where you answer Slack within minutes, where anything synchronous has to happen. Everything on either side of it belongs to deep, uninterrupted, async work.

From Southeast Asia with a US team, your overlap is probably your evening. From South America with Europeans, it lands in your morning. Neither slot is the one you would pick, but pinning it down does something quietly powerful: it kills the low-grade dread of feeling like you should be reachable every waking hour.

Async-First Communication

Lean on async communication harder than feels natural at first. Write the detailed update instead of booking the meeting. Record a two-minute Loom instead of asking everyone to be on a call at once. Drop your thinking into a shared doc and let people comment on their own clock. This is not just a workaround for your odd hours. Teams that default to writing things down tend to produce clearer, more considered work, time zones or not.

Pro tip: Put your current time zone in your Slack status and email signature, and change it the day you move. It takes ten seconds and quietly answers the question a colleague would never ask out loud: whether you're heads-down or three coconuts deep on a beach somewhere.

Setting Boundaries

Boundaries run both ways out here, and almost everyone guards only one of the two doors.

Work Boundaries: When to Stop

With no office to walk out of, work seeps into the gaps. The laptop is always within reach. The notification only needs a quick reply, surely. Then it's 10 PM and you're typing into Slack across a dinner table while your food goes cold. Pick a hard stop and treat it as real. Shut the lid. Zip it into a bag in another room if that's what it takes. Tomorrow's work waits perfectly well overnight.

Life Boundaries: When to Start

The other door is harder to shut. Drop into a city that's lighting you up and every day starts to feel like a holiday with a little work wedged in around the edges. Chase that during work hours and you get the worst of both: you can't enjoy the rooftop view because a half-finished task is nagging at you, and you can't finish the task because the view keeps pulling your eyes back to the window.

So the rule is blunt: work hours are work hours. Exploring gets the evenings, the weekends, and that long midday break. On top of that I block one full "explore day" a week, usually a Tuesday or Wednesday when the sights are empty, with work entirely off the table. Knowing that day is coming makes it far easier to stay locked in on the other four.

Traveling with a partner or friends raises the stakes, so say the boundary out loud. "I'm working until 2, then I'm all yours" lands a hundred times better than the foggy, always-half-here state that leaves nobody satisfied, least of all you.

Essential Tools & Apps

You do not need an elaborate stack to work well abroad. A handful of tools earn their place, and the rest just become more things to babysit. Here is what genuinely opens every day on my machine:

Notion: My single source of truth for tasks, notes, travel logistics, and project documentation. The offline mode is critical for those moments between wifi connections. I keep a running "city setup checklist" template that I duplicate every time I arrive somewhere new.

Slack: Non-negotiable for team communication. I use aggressive notification schedules, muting everything outside my overlap window and setting custom statuses with my local time.

Loom: Replaced at least half my meetings. A 3-minute screen recording is faster to make than scheduling a call and more useful than a wall of text in a Slack thread. Especially valuable across time zones.

1Password: Managing passwords across devices and networks is a security essential. Public wifi at coworking spaces and cafes makes a password manager mandatory, not optional.

NordVPN: A VPN is non-negotiable when you're regularly connecting to unfamiliar networks. Beyond security, it's essential for accessing region-locked services and keeping your banking apps happy when they detect a foreign IP.

Past these few, hold the line. Every extra app is one more login to sync and one more thing to debug at the worst possible moment, hunched over a router with two bars of signal. Know five tools cold and you'll move faster than the person juggling fifteen half-learned ones.

Making It Work Long-Term

The people who keep this going for years instead of burning out by month six all share one habit of mind: they treat remote work abroad as a craft to get better at, not a perk to cash in. They tinker with their systems, they say out loud when something isn't working, and they protect their professional name with the same energy they put into chasing waterfalls on the weekend.

Start slow: If this is new to you, resist the urge to open with a different continent and a 12-hour gap from your team. Pick a country a short flight away, in a time zone that still roughly lines up. Build your systems where a bad day costs you little, then take them somewhere harder.

Stay longer: Moving cities carries a real tax on your output. Each hop burns 2-3 days while you hunt down wifi, groceries, and a workspace that holds. Settle in for 4-8 weeks instead of bouncing every fortnight and your average week gets noticeably better.

Invest in your setup: A portable monitor, a real pair of noise-cancelling headphones, a keyboard that survives a backpack. None of it is indulgence. Each one earns its weight back inside the first week, the moment a screaming cafe goes silent and your second screen turns a four-hour task into two.

Check in with yourself: Every few weeks, ask yourself straight whether the work you're shipping holds up against your home-office self. If the honest answer is no, some part of the system needs a tune-up. The aim was never to grind more hours. It's to work well enough that the whole life stays standing without your career quietly paying for it.

Getting paid to work from anywhere is one of the rare gifts of this decade, and it's easy to fumble by treating every week as either a vacation or a grind. Guarding your productivity isn't about shrinking the adventure. It's the thing that lets you keep having it, city after city, for as long as you like. Build the systems, hold the boundaries, and most of the rest sorts itself out.

Frequently Asked Questions About Staying Productive While Working Abroad

How do you stay productive while working abroad?

Treat productivity as a system, not willpower. Build a routine anchored to actions rather than places, set up reliable workspaces in advance, protect a fixed overlap window with your team, and define hard stop times. Designed habits, not constant effort, are what keep your output steady as you move between cities.

What is the best daily routine for staying productive working remotely?

Use a two-block system: two deep-focus blocks of two to three hours each, separated by a long midday break. Start the first block within an hour of waking, save communication and meetings for the afternoon, and use the break to live. Anchoring the sequence to actions keeps it working across time zones. Our remote work routine guide goes deeper.

How do you find a good workspace to stay productive in a new city?

Establish three options within the first 48 hours: a home base desk, a coworking space for fast internet and structure, and a tested backup cafe. This redundancy means apartment wifi failures or power cuts never derail your day. A reliable coworking space is worth every cent of the membership.

How do you manage time zones to stay productive working abroad?

Identify the two to four hours where your day overlaps with your team and protect that window fiercely for meetings and synchronous work. Treat everything outside it as async deep-work time. Push for written updates and short videos instead of calls, and keep your current time zone visible in your status and signature.

How do you set boundaries to protect productivity while working remotely?

Boundaries run both directions. Define a hard stop so work does not bleed into your evenings, and keep work hours genuinely for work so exploration does not create a guilt spiral. Save sightseeing for evenings, weekends, and one dedicated explore day each week, then communicate those boundaries clearly to anyone traveling with you.

What tools help you stay productive when working abroad?

Keep the stack minimal so there is less to manage on shaky wifi. A central workspace for tasks and notes, a team chat tool with disciplined notifications, async video for explaining complex topics, a password manager, and a VPN cover most needs. Mastering five tools beats dabbling with fifteen.

Is it possible to be more productive working abroad than at home?

Yes, once the framework is in place. Fewer office distractions, more control over your schedule, and the energy of living somewhere exciting can push your focused output above home levels. The two-block routine often delivers four to six hours of genuine deep work, more than many office workers manage in a full day.

How do you sustain working remotely abroad long-term?

Treat remote work abroad as a skill you develop, not a perk you enjoy. Start in a nearby time zone, stay four to eight weeks per city to cut moving costs, and check honestly whether your output matches what you would deliver from home. Weighing destinations?
