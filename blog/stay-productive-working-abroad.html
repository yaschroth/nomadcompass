Remote Work

Stay Productive Working Abroad: Time Zones, Wifi, Jet Lag

Yannick Schroth February 2025 12 min read

Your alarm goes off at 2:40 a.m. in Canggu. Batu Bolong beach and the temple beside it are dark, and in New York it is 2:40 in the afternoon: the weekly client call starts in twenty minutes. You came to Canggu for the surf. You are about to present a quarterly plan at the hour your body is least able to think.

That hour is not bad luck. Bali runs on Central Indonesia Time, eight hours ahead of UTC, and never changes its clocks. New York is on daylight saving time from 8 March to 1 November in 2026, so the gap is 12 hours for most of the year and 13 in winter.

A 3 p.m. meeting in New York lands at 3 a.m. in Canggu until November, then at 4 a.m. Both fall inside the window from about 2 to 6 a.m., when the body clock's drive for wakefulness is at its weakest.

Staying productive working abroad rarely fails for lack of discipline. It fails on problems the trip itself creates: a clock that puts your meetings in the middle of the night, jet lag, a flat whose wifi cannot carry a call, the lost first week in every new city, and being alone. In a 2023 survey of 3,000 remote workers, working across time zones was the single biggest struggle for 14 percent. Each of these problems has a fix, and most of the fixes happen before you book.

Time zones abroad: find the overlap before you book

Start with the arithmetic, because it decides everything else. A New York team working nine to five in summer is online from 9 p.m. to 5 a.m. in Bali. There is no overlap at all with a normal Balinese working day. The least bad arrangement is a split day: your own deep work in the morning, a free afternoon, and a two-hour window from 8 to 10 p.m., which in summer is 8 to 10 a.m. in New York.

That window is where calls, reviews and quick decisions happen, and everything outside it goes into writing: a detailed update instead of a meeting, a recorded walkthrough instead of a demo. Agree the window with the team in writing, put it in the shared calendar in their time zone, and hold it like an office would hold opening hours.

Some gaps are simply too wide for some jobs. If yours involves a daily stand-up or clients who expect same-day answers, choose a base the clock favours. From New York, Medellín is never more than an hour off; Lisbon, five hours ahead, still shares the New York morning with the Lisbon afternoon. Why the gap to your team changes twice a year

The table splits the year three ways for a reason. The US and the EU both use summer time but switch on different dates: the US on the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November, the EU on the last Sunday of March and the last Sunday of October. In 2026 that leaves three weeks in March and the last week of October when New York and the EU sit an hour closer than usual.

Bases near the equator add a second shift. Bali and Colombia do not change their clocks, so your team's switch moves every recurring meeting by an hour on your side. The New York call that sat at 8 p.m. in Canggu all summer starts at 9 p.m. from 1 November. Keep a second time zone visible in your calendar, book recurring meetings in the team's zone, and look at the week ahead each time either side changes.

What a 3 a.m. call costs your productivity abroad

One night call is a bad morning. A weekly one is something else. In a University of Pennsylvania lab study, healthy adults spent two weeks with their time in bed cut to four, six or eight hours a night. At six hours or less they built up cognitive deficits equivalent to up to two nights without any sleep, while their own sleepiness ratings barely registered the decline. Short sleep feels better than it performs.

So treat a recurring meeting between midnight and 6 a.m. as a problem to solve, not a sacrifice to make. Ask to move it to your evening and the team's morning. Offer a recorded update instead, or a rotation so the awkward hour is shared across the team. If none of that is possible and the meeting matters, the base is wrong for the job, and the honest fix is to move closer to the team's clock.

Jet lag: how many days before you work at full speed

Jet lag starts after crossing two or more time zones and brings poor sleep, daytime sleepiness, slower thinking and an upset stomach. The CDC puts the body clock's average rate of adjustment at about 1.5 hours a day after flying west and 1 hour a day after flying east. West is easier because people naturally drift later. A flight from Berlin to Bali, seven hours east in winter, can take about a week to absorb.

Light is the main lever. Bright morning light moves the body clock earlier and evening light moves it later, so after flying east, get outside early and dim the evenings; after flying west, do the opposite. For big shifts, a jet lag calculator times the light more precisely. The CDC notes that 0.5 to 1 mg of melatonin is often enough to shift the clock, advises against alcohol, and for trips shorter than two days recommends not adapting at all.

For your work, the rule is simple: never land the day before a deadline. Fly in at the end of a week, keep the first working days for tasks that forgive a slow brain, and move anything important to the second week. The first week in a new city abroad

Even without jet lag, the first night somewhere new is a poor one. Sleep researchers call it the first-night effect, and a 2016 study at Brown University traced it: in an unfamiliar room, one half of the brain sleeps less deeply and stays alert to unusual sounds, a night watch that had disappeared by the next sessions. Expect a poor night one.

Use the first days for setting up rather than output. Before the first important call, test the flat's connection at the hour you will actually use it; find two places to work besides the flat; buy a local SIM or eSIM with a generous data allowance; and walk the route to the nearest coworking space. In Canggu, our guide to Bali's coworking spaces covers the Canggu options with their monthly prices.

None of this takes long, and it turns a crisis later into a ten-minute walk. The worst version of the first week is the one where you discover the wifi problem during the client call.

Unreliable internet abroad: what a video call really needs

Most listings advertise download speed. Calls live or die on upload. Zoom asks for 1.2 Mbps up and down for a one-to-one call in 720p, and 3.8 Mbps up for group video in 1080p. A connection that downloads at 100 Mbps but uploads at 2 falls short of what group video in HD asks for.

Ask the host for a speed test screenshot before you book, then run your own on arrival, at the time of day your calls happen. Then build a backup that does not share the flat's weak points: a phone hotspot on a different network from the building's line, and a coworking space for days with big presentations. If power is the problem rather than the line, ask whether the building or the coworking space runs a generator.

When the connection fails anyway, drop the video before you drop the call. Sharing a screen without video needs only 50 to 75 kbps, a small fraction of what HD video asks, so the meeting survives.

Loneliness while working abroad

The problem nobody plans for is the quiet one. A World Health Organization commission reported in 2025 that 1 in 6 people worldwide is affected by loneliness, which it links to more than 871,000 deaths a year. It defines loneliness as the gap between the social connection you want and the connection you have, and a new city where you know nobody widens that gap overnight.

Remote work makes it wider. In the same Buffer survey, 23 percent of remote workers picked loneliness as a struggle, and one in three said they stay home too often because they have no reason to leave. That second answer points to the fix: build reasons to leave into the week.

A coworking membership instead of the kitchen table, a class that meets on the same evening every week, a surf lesson, a running club: anything recurring that puts the same faces in front of you. If you arrive knowing nobody, a coliving space solves the first week for you; our look at coliving spaces for digital nomads compares what they cost.

When to slow travel: staying longer to stay productive

Add it up and every move carries a bill: the first-night effect, days of jet lag if you cross time zones, a connection to test, workspaces to find, people to meet from scratch. Move every two weeks and you pay it constantly. Stay a month or more and the setup costs shrink to a small share of the stay.

The rules reward planning too. For visitors from outside the EU, the Schengen area allows at most 90 days in any 180, counted across all its member countries, so crossing from Portugal into Spain resets nothing; decide where those 90 days are best spent. Stay long in one country, on the other hand, and you can become tax resident there; our guide to how digital nomads are taxed explains where that line falls.

So slow down when your output drops after each move, when the time zone forces night calls, or when you notice that you know nobody in the city you are in. Choose the next base for its clock first and its beaches second. Frequently Asked Questions

How do you stay productive working abroad in a different time zone?

Fix a two-hour overlap window with your team, put it in the shared calendar in their time zone, and move everything else into writing. From Bali with a New York team, 8 to 10 p.m. local time meets their morning. If the job needs daily calls, pick a base with more overlap.

How long does jet lag affect productivity after a flight abroad?

The CDC puts the body clock's adjustment at about 1 hour a day after flying east and 1.5 hours a day after flying west. A seven-hour eastward shift can take about a week, so keep the first working days light.

What internet speed do you need to work remotely abroad?

For video calls, upload matters most. Zoom asks for 1.2 Mbps up and down for a one-to-one 720p call and 3.8 Mbps up for group video in 1080p. Test at the hour you will call, and keep a phone hotspot on a different network as a backup.

Why does the time difference change in March and October when working abroad?

The US and the EU change their clocks on different Sundays, and bases such as Bali and Colombia do not change at all. In 2026, New York and the EU sit an hour closer for three weeks in March and one week in late October.

How do digital nomads avoid loneliness when working abroad?

Build recurring reasons to leave the flat: a coworking membership, a weekly class, a sports club, or a coliving space for the first weeks. One in three remote workers in a 2023 survey said they stay home too often because they have no reason to leave.

How long should you stay in each place to stay productive working abroad?

A month or more is a sensible minimum. Every move brings a poor first night, possibly jet lag, and new internet and workspaces to set up. Longer stays spread those costs over more working weeks and give you time to meet people.
