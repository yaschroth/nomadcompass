Oslo Digital Nomad Guide: Real Costs and Residence Rules

Oslo is an easy city to want to work from. Fibre passes nine in ten homes, one ticket covers the metro, the trams and the fjord ferries, and in late June the sun does not set until quarter to eleven. It is a much harder city to afford, and without an EU or EEA passport, a harder city to stay in with the laptop open.

So the answer depends on who you are. With an EU or EEA passport and a good income, Oslo works as a proper base: you can register, sign a real lease and stay as long as you keep working or supporting yourself. From the US, the UK, Canada or Australia, treat it as a summer city, because Norway has no digital nomad visa and a visit is meant to be a visit. Either way, skip Oslo if you are counting every dollar or can only come between November and January.

Money shapes everything else here, so start with it. The average one-bedroom in Oslo and neighbouring Bærum rents for about $1,605 a month, and that figure flatters the city. It averages every sitting tenant, including people on leases signed years ago, in a market where rents rose 6 percent in a single year. A flat you find this month will usually cost more, which is why the budget below assumes a central one-bedroom closer to $2,000.

Can you work from Oslo on a visit?

Norway sits outside the EU but inside Schengen, and that is the first thing to understand on an American, British, Canadian or Australian passport. You get 90 days in any 180, shared with every other Schengen country, so a fortnight in Lisbon in the spring comes out of the same allowance as your summer in Oslo. Nobody counts them by hand any more: the EU's Entry/Exit System, fully in place since 10 April 2026, logs your fingerprints, a photo and every crossing in place of passport stamps.

The harder question is what you may do with those days. Norway treats remote work as work: outside the EU and EEA, you need a residence permit for it, even when your employer and every client are abroad. What a visitor may do is keep up. Answering emails, taking calls and joining digital meetings for an employer or your own business back home is allowed, as long as the work is not the main purpose of the stay and creates no value in Norway.

Read plainly, that permits a holiday with a laptop and rules out a three-month work stint timed to fill the Schengen allowance. If the honest reason you are in Oslo is the job, the rule was not written for you. If you are here for the summer and your inbox follows you, it was.

Is there a permit that fits a remote worker?

No, and it is worth knowing why before you pay for an application: the two permits most often described as Norway's nomad options were built for other people.

The independent contractor permit is for a self-employed person based abroad with a contract to deliver a time-limited job for a client, plus skilled-worker qualifications and pay. It is tied to that contract, runs six years at most and never leads to permanent residence. It fits a consultant sent to deliver a defined project, not a freelancer whose clients change from month to month.

The self-employed permit is narrower still. It is for someone who has to live in Norway to run a lasting business here, a business that must essentially provide your income, and you may work in that business only. Nordic citizens need no permit at all. For everyone else who wants a long, legal working stay, our comparison of digital nomad visas covers the countries that do offer one.

If you hold an EU or EEA passport

This is where Oslo opens up. You can stay three months without formalities. After that you register with the police for a registration certificate, and you need to be using one of your EEA rights: as an employee, a self-employed person, a service provider, a student, someone living on their own funds, or a job seeker.

Treat the certificate as a receipt, not a permit. It records that you registered but does not prove your right to live here, so keep the contract, payslips or bank statements that do. Tax runs on a separate track, and you can book an appointment with the Tax Administration to report your move before the certificate arrives, so do not wait for it. If an office asks for a certified translation of a foreign document, our Oslo services directory lists translators and interpreters by the language they work in.

How long before Norway taxes you?

Longer than most visitors stay, though the count has a second trap. You become tax resident in Norway after more than 183 days in any twelve months, or more than 270 days in any 36, and every part day counts. The first line only matters if you move here. The second catches the people who fall for the city and come back every summer: three summers of just over 90 days each will cross it. Keep to an average of 90 days a year and you stay under both.

If you are salaried, your pay has its own test. Norway can tax a foreign employer's employee who spends more than 183 days here in twelve months, whose employer has a permanent establishment in Norway, or who is hired out to or working for a Norwegian business. A tax treaty with your home country can limit that claim, and our guide to how digital nomads are taxed explains how residence and treaties fit together.

Where should you live, and how do you rent?

Start with the lease, because it decides which market you are in. A fixed-term lease on a home in Norway cannot be shorter than three years, apart from narrow exceptions such as a landlord who will move back in. Ordinary renting is built for people who stay, so for one to three months you will be looking at furnished short lets and aparthotels, a market the official survey does not measure. Budget above the averages in the table.

Staying a year or longer changes the picture. You can sign a normal lease, and the law caps the deposit at six months' rent, held in a separate account in your name. On an average one-bedroom that maximum comes to about $9,600, so have the cash ready before you start viewing.

Where you live matters less to the bill than you might think. A modelled 50 m² one-bedroom runs to about $1,757 a month in Sentrum, Frogner or St. Hanshaugen, and the same flat out in Grorud or Stovner is only about $230 cheaper, with a longer metro ride attached. Grünerløkka is priced almost exactly like the centre. So choose the neighbourhood for how you want to live, not to save money. The real saving comes from size: a studio averages about $540 a month less than a one-bedroom.

What a working day in Oslo is like

The connection is the one thing you will rarely have to think about. Almost every household in Oslo can order a gigabit line, and at least one 5G network covers 99 percent of them as a backup. The catch is that fibre running past a building is not fibre in the flat: across Norway, far fewer homes are connected than are passed. Before you sign, ask whether the fibre runs into the unit itself and what speed the landlord's plan delivers.

For days away from the flat, Mesh, with coworking houses at Nationaltheatret and Youngstorget, publishes its rates: a lounge membership starts at $188 a month, and a desk runs two to three times that. If you only need a change of scene a few days a week, the lounge tier is the sensible buy.

Getting around is the cheap part. Ruter runs the metro, trams, buses and ferries on one ticket system, which also works on Vy trains inside Oslo and Akershus. The 30-day pass for zone 1 pays for itself at 30 trips a month, which a weekday commute reaches on its own. Tickets are anonymous, so you need no Norwegian ID number; buy in the app before you board.

When should you go?

In Oslo the calendar matters more than the thermometer. Say you land in late November. The sun comes up at half past eight and is gone before twenty to four, and if your clients are in the Americas, your working afternoon starts after dark and runs into the night. December is darker still, with under six hours of daylight and average highs below freezing.

Now flip it. In June the sun rises before four and sets at quarter to eleven, and average highs reach 21°C, so a full working day still leaves a whole evening of light on the water or in the forest. May and August come close, and September, with about twelve hours of light and highs around 16°C, is the last comfortable month before the dark closes in.

Summer has one small catch: July is the wettest month, with about 118 mm of rain. For a first stay, aim for May, June or late August. Come in winter only if you already know you work well through long dark afternoons.

What happens if you get sick?

Anyone staying in Norway is entitled to urgent care; the question is who pays. With a European Health Insurance Card, you are treated in the public system on the same terms as Norwegians and pay only the user fee. UK residents can use the GHIC the same way.

Bring the physical card, because a digital one is refused. A US or other non-EU citizen living in the EU usually cannot use an EU-issued card here, and private doctors without a public agreement, most prescriptions and most dental work are paid in full whatever card you carry.

From outside the EU, the EEA and Switzerland you get nothing back from the Norwegian scheme, Australian residents apart, so travel insurance carries the cost. At night, the legevakt, Oslo's emergency clinic, is open around the clock at Trondheimsveien 233 at Aker hospital: call 116 117 if you are ill, walk in with an injury, dial 113 if a life is at risk. Oslo, Stockholm or Copenhagen?

Among the Scandinavian capitals, Oslo is the dearest. On our one-person budgets it costs about $430 a month more than Stockholm and $290 more than Copenhagen, and on a non-EU passport you cannot stretch a stay by hopping between them, because all three draw on the same Schengen allowance.

What the extra money buys is the setting: long summer evenings of light, with the fjord ferries on the same pass that gets you to your desk. If that is what you want, Oslo is worth the premium for a summer. For a cheaper Nordic base over a longer stay, start with the other two.

If you do choose Oslo, the plan is short. On a non-EU passport, plan a summer stay of well under 90 days, keep the work in the background, and look for a furnished let. On an EU or EEA passport, register once you pass three months and book the tax appointment without waiting for the certificate. Either way, check the fibre before you sign for a flat, carry a physical EHIC or proper insurance, and buy the 30-day pass on your first day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Norway have a digital nomad visa for Oslo?

No. No Norwegian permit covers remote work for foreign clients or employers. The independent contractor permit needs a contract for a time-limited job, and the self-employed permit covers only a business you run from Norway. EU, EEA and Nordic citizens need no permit at all.

Can I work remotely in Oslo during a Schengen visit?

Only around the edges of the trip. Visitors may answer emails, take calls and join digital meetings for an employer or business abroad, as long as work is not the main reason for the stay and creates no value in Norway. A stay built around the job needs a residence permit, unless you are an EU or EEA citizen.

How much does it cost to live in Oslo as a digital nomad?

Plan on about $3,250 a month for one person with a central one-bedroom. The average one-bedroom rents for about $1,605, but that includes old leases, and a central flat found today is closer to $2,000.

When does a digital nomad in Oslo become tax resident in Norway?

After more than 183 days in Norway in any twelve months, or more than 270 days in any 36, with part days counted. An average of 90 days a year keeps you under both. Salaried workers can owe tax sooner if their employer has a permanent establishment in Norway.

Is the internet in Oslo good enough for remote work?

Yes. Fibre reaches more than nine in ten Oslo homes and almost every household can order a gigabit line. Before you sign for a flat, check that the fibre is actually connected to the unit, since many homes are passed by fibre without being hooked up.

What is the best time of year to work from Oslo?

May to August, with June the brightest at close to 19 hours of daylight. September is the last comfortable month. December has under six hours of daylight and highs below freezing, and clients in the Americas push your working afternoon into the dark.

Does the EHIC cover healthcare for visitors in Oslo?

Yes, for medically necessary care in the public system at the Norwegian user fee. Bring the physical card, because Norway refuses digital ones, and expect to pay in full for private doctors, most prescriptions and most dental work.

Is Oslo more expensive than Stockholm or Copenhagen for nomads?

Yes. On our one-person budgets Oslo costs about $430 a month more than Stockholm and $290 more than Copenhagen. Non-EU visitors also share one 90-day Schengen allowance across all three, so you cannot extend a stay by moving between them.
