Buying Property in Split as a Foreigner: Costs and Lawyers

Whether buying property in Split takes six weeks or six months depends on your passport. A citizen of the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein or Norway buys a flat in Split on the same terms as a Croatian. An American, Briton or Turk first needs written consent from Croatia's Ministry of Justice, granted only if Croatians can buy in their country. An Australian cannot get that consent for an existing home until 31 March 2027.

One change is already law and not yet in force. In March 2025 Parliament amended the ownership act so that citizens of every OECD member state buy on Croatian terms, with no consent. It takes effect on the day Croatia joins the OECD. When OECD ministers met in June 2026, Croatia was still an accession candidate, and we found no record of accession since.

This guide covers the legal steps, what a real estate lawyer checks, the costs, and what ownership does not give you. Dollar figures convert euro amounts at the exchange rate of 1 August 2026, $1.151 per euro.

Buying property in Split step by step

The sequence is the same for a studio in Varoš and a stone house on the Marjan slopes. Non-EU buyers add one step, the consent application, which sits between signing and registration.

  1. Get an OIB, the Croatian personal tax number, from the Tax Administration.
  2. Pull the land registry extract and the cadastral plan, and check the building's permits.
  3. Sign a preliminary contract and pay a deposit.
  4. Sign the main contract and have the seller's signature certified by a notary.
  5. Non-EU buyers only: apply to the Ministry of Justice for consent and wait for the decision.
  6. Have the notary or your lawyer file electronically for registration at the Split Municipal Court's land registry department, within 60 days.
  7. Pay the 3% transfer tax within 15 days of receiving the assessment.
  8. From the following year, pay the annual property tax unless the flat is your permanent home or let long-term.

Two of these steps carry legal deadlines with penalties attached, so the order matters.

Who can buy a flat in Split, by passport

The rule sits in the Act on Ownership and Other Real Rights. Citizens and companies of EU member states, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway acquire real estate in Croatia on the same conditions as Croatians, according to the government's gov.hr page on purchases by foreign nationals. Swiss citizens get the same treatment but must show a certificate of temporary residence in Croatia when they register.

Everyone else is a "third-country national" and needs the Minister of Justice's consent, which depends on reciprocity: your country must let Croatians buy there on the same or similar terms. The ministry publishes its reciprocity list country by country. As of September 2026 it reads:

  • United Kingdom: reciprocity met. Since Brexit, British buyers go through consent like any non-EU buyer.
  • United States: met, except for residents of Arkansas, Hawaii, Kentucky, Minnesota, Mississippi, New Hampshire, Oklahoma and Vermont, where the list requires permanent or long-term residence.
  • Australia: no reciprocity for existing homes from 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2027, with narrow exceptions, matching Australia's own two-year ban on foreigners buying established dwellings.
  • Canada, Mexico and the UAE: "verification underway", with no confirmed status.
  • New Zealand: met, up to a value of 10 million New Zealand dollars or 5 hectares.
  • Russia, Ukraine, Serbia, Turkey, Brazil, Japan, South Korea, South Africa, Argentina: met. Bosnia and Herzegovina has full reciprocity.
  • India and Taiwan: no reciprocity.

Check the list yourself before paying anyone. Commercial buying guides still tell Canadians and Australians they can buy under reciprocity, and on the ministry's own page neither is true today.

Ministry consent for non-EU buyers of property in Split

Consent is an administrative procedure run by the Ministry of Justice, Administration and Digital Transformation in Zagreb, not by anyone in Split. The application goes to the ministry's registry at Ulica grada Vukovara 49, 10000 Zagreb.

The ministry lists what it expects: the contract or other legal basis for the purchase, the land registry extract proving the seller owns the property, an urban planning certificate on the plot's legal status, a certified copy of your passport, a power of attorney if someone files for you, and a named representative in Croatia if you live abroad.

Note the first item. You sign the purchase contract, then ask permission to complete it. That is why a lawyer drafting for a non-EU buyer writes the ministry's consent in as a condition, with the deposit returned in full if consent is refused. Without that clause, your deposit rides on a decision you do not control.

The General Administrative Procedure Act gives an authority 30 days to decide a simple request and 60 days where it must examine the case (Article 101); if it misses the deadline, you may appeal or start an administrative dispute. Two independent buyers' guides put the real wait at two to six months, and a third attributes the gap to the ministry asking for further documents.

The OECD amendment (Narodne novine 52/2025, adopted 14 March 2025) rewrites Article 358.a so that citizens of OECD member states are exempt from consent. Its final article puts its entry into force on the day Croatia accedes to OECD membership. Until that day, the consent rule applies to Americans, Britons, Canadians and Australians alike.

The text of the amendment also says that consent procedures still open on that day are finished under the old rules. A buyer who files a month before accession still waits for the decision.

Olive groves and vineyards: the agricultural land rule around Split

The Agricultural Land Act is separate from the ownership act, and stricter. Article 2 says foreign natural and legal persons cannot own agricultural land unless an international treaty or special law says otherwise; the only exception for everyone else is inheritance, subject to reciprocity. EU, EEA and Swiss citizens have bought it on Croatian terms since 1 July 2023, when the transitional ban ran out.

What counts is decided by how the cadastre describes the parcel, not by what you see. Article 3 lists arable land, gardens, meadows, pastures, orchards, maslinici (olive groves), vinogradi (vineyards), fishponds, reeds and marshes.

On the Dalmatian coast that catches much of what gets sold as "a house with land". A stone cottage whose garden is recorded as an olive grove sits on two parcels under two regimes, and a non-EU buyer can take the house with consent and still be barred from the grove. Check the recorded use of every parcel number in the sale.

Getting an OIB before you buy property in Croatia

Nothing in a Croatian purchase moves without an OIB (osobni identifikacijski broj), the eleven-digit number the notary, the court, the bank and the tax office all key your file to. The Tax Administration's page for foreigners says you email the completed request form and a scan of your passport or EU identity card to OIB@porezna-uprava.hr, naming your nearest tax office.

The office then invites you to collect the OIB certificate in person with the original documents, and says it handles these requests with priority, within eight days. The page lists no fee. The land registry application must carry the OIB of both buyer and seller, so request it before you sign anything.

Checking the Split land registry and cadastre

Croatia keeps two records of every plot. The cadastre (katastar) records the technical facts: parcel number, area, use and buildings. The land registry (zemljišna knjiga) adds the rights: who owns it and what burdens it. The local cadastral offices and court registry departments long kept separate databases that did not match, which is why the State Geodetic Administration built the joint system, ZIS.

You can read both on the public Uređena zemlja portal with the cadastral municipality and the parcel or folio number. The extract has three sheets. Sheet A (posjedovnica) describes the property. Sheet B (vlastovnica) names the owners and any restrictions on selling. Sheet C (teretovnica) lists the burdens: mortgages, pre-emption rights, leases, easements and enforcement notes.

A lawyer reading the extract for a Split flat looks for five things. First, that the seller on sheet B is the person signing, with every co-owner and heir accounted for. Second, that sheet C is empty, or that each mortgage is paid off from your money at completion with a deletion statement from the bank. Third, any plomba, the seal the court puts on a folio when an application is pending.

Fourth, whether the flat is registered as a separate unit (etažno vlasništvo) or only as a share of the whole building. Fifth, whether the cadastral area and use match the registry and the building on the ground. That last check fails most often in older Dalmatian stock, where an extension, an attic conversion or an enclosed terrace never reached either record.

Usage permits and legalisation in Split property

Under the Building Act, a building may be used only once the authority has issued a usage permit (uporabna dozvola). The large exception: a building completed before 15 February 1968 is treated as if it had a final building permit, and the authority confirms the date on request. Much of central Split, from the palace quarter to Varoš, predates that line.

For anything built or extended later without a permit, the answer is a legalisation decision (rješenje o izvedenom stanju). Applications closed on 30 June 2018 and have now reopened: an amendment published in Narodne novine 48/2026 on 8 May 2026 abolished the deadline, with applications filed through the eDozvola system. The building ministry's condition is that the structure stood by 21 June 2011 and is visible on the State Geodetic Administration's orthophoto map of that date. The owner also pays a fee for illegal construction.

For a buyer that becomes three written questions to the seller: which permit covers each part of the building, whether any part postdates it, and whether a legalisation decision exists or was applied for. An extension built after 21 June 2011 can never be legalised. Make the permits a condition of the preliminary contract, not a question for after the deposit.

The preliminary contract and the deposit on a Split flat

Croatian sales usually start with a preliminary contract (predugovor) and a deposit called kapara. Three independent buyers' guides put the usual deposit at about 10% of the price; on a $400,000 flat that is $40,000 leaving your account months before you own anything.

The Civil Obligations Act sets what the deposit does. Under Article 303, the contract is concluded when the deposit is paid, and the deposit is credited to the price on completion. Unless the contract says otherwise, neither side can walk away simply by giving it up: you cannot leave by forfeiting it, and the seller cannot leave by paying it back double.

Under Article 304, if the seller causes the sale to fail, you can demand completion, or damages plus the deposit back, or double the deposit. If you cause it, the seller can keep it. A court can reduce an excessively large deposit. So a buyer's lawyer adds the conditions that protect the money: consent for non-EU buyers, a clean sheet C, the permits above, a deadline for clearing any mortgage, and who keeps what if a condition fails.

Notary certification and registering your Split property

The main contract does not have to be a notarial deed. The Land Registry Act allows registration on a private document if the seller's signature is certified (Article 57) and the document contains the seller's express consent to your registration as owner, the clausula intabulandi (Article 59). A notary (javni bilježnik) certifies the signature.

If you sign through an attorney, the signature on your power of attorney must be certified too (Article 58), and a foreign certification follows treaty rules. If you do not read Croatian, have the contract drawn up bilingually and bring an interpreter: we list the 135 court interpreters in Split appointed by the Ministry of Justice, sorted by language.

Filing is electronic only. Under Article 105, the registration application goes to the land registry department of the Split Municipal Court through ZIS, submitted by a notary or a lawyer, and a paper filing is rejected. A notary who certified the signature must file unless you expressly object. The gov.hr registration page adds a deadline: file more than 60 days after the conditions for registration were met and the court fee is charged five times over.

The fee itself is small: the 2023 court fee tariff charges about $7.60 for the application and $30.55 for the decision, roughly $38, and gov.hr says electronic filing halves it. When an application arrives, the court marks the folio with a plomba showing the date, time and file number (Article 108), and must decide a complete application within 15 working days (Article 115). That seal protects you against a second sale in the meantime.

Once the decision is entered you appear on sheet B, and Article 8 protects a buyer who relied in good faith on what the registry showed. What buying property in Split costs: tax and fees in dollars

The largest cost is the real estate transfer tax: 3% of market value, paid by the buyer (Real Estate Transfer Tax Act, Articles 6 and 12). The base is market value, so a contract price below market can be reassessed. If no notary handled the contract, you report the purchase yourself within 30 days (Article 19). Either way, you pay within 15 days of receiving the assessment (Article 26).

A new flat bought from a developer with VAT does not attract the transfer tax: the Tax Administration's page excludes acquisitions taxed with VAT. To make the rest concrete, take a 55-square-metre central flat at the $7,270 per square metre our city page carries from Numbeo's August 2026 data: about $400,000.

  • Transfer tax at 3%: $12,000.
  • Agency commission: commonly 2% to 3% plus VAT according to buyers' guides, so $8,000 to $12,000 before VAT. It is negotiable and sometimes split with the seller.
  • Lawyer: the Croatian Bar Association's tariff gives about $7,500 before VAT for drafting a contract of this value, plus about $1,150 for the registration application.
  • Land registry court fees: about $38, or half that filed electronically.
  • Notary: charged under the official notary tariff; ask for the amount before the appointment.
  • Annual property tax: about $317 a year for this flat if it is not your permanent home.

The lawyer figure needs context. The tariff counts in points worth $2.30 each and prices a contract by its value, roughly 3,270 points here. Tariff No. 41 lets a lawyer cut that by half, and Tariff No. 43 lets you and the lawyer agree any fee in writing. Treat the tariff as a reference and get a written quote before you instruct anyone.

What a real estate lawyer in Split does, and how to find one

Croatian law does not require a lawyer to draft the contract. It requires a certified signature, a registration application that only a notary or a lawyer can file, and, for non-EU buyers, a consent file. A foreign buyer uses a lawyer for the parts that carry risk: reading sheets B and C, chasing the permit history, drafting the preliminary contract's conditions, preparing the consent application and filing the registration inside the 60-day window.

A lawyer holding your power of attorney can do all of it while you are abroad. Every practising lawyer in Croatia is entered with the Croatian Bar Association (Hrvatska odvjetnička komora), which also publishes the fee tariff, so check a name there before you sign anything.

Five come from the UK Foreign Office's list of lawyers abroad, where each has confirmed it can work in English and names real estate among its practice areas. Three come from the Italian embassy in Zagreb.

If none fits, the 31 lawyers we list in Zagreb can act on a Split purchase, since the consent file is decided in Zagreb anyway, and the Croatia-wide lawyer directory covers seven cities.

Renting out your Split property to tourists

If you plan to let the flat while you are away, check the building first. Since 1 January 2025, Article 34 of the Act on Building Management and Maintenance (NN 152/24) requires prior written consent from a two-thirds majority of co-owners before a flat is used for short-term rental, and every owner whose walls, floors or ceilings adjoin your flat must be among them.

The amended Hospitality Act (NN 152/2024) made that consent a condition for the rental decision and gave existing hosts five years to produce it. A flat already let to tourists may therefore have no signatures yet, so ask the seller for them before you price in rental income. The same amendment stopped new temporary rental decisions from 1 January 2025, and those already issued lapse on 31 December 2026.

Two yearly lump sums follow. The flat-rate income tax (paušalni porez), set by Split's city council in February 2025, is about $288 per bed in zone I, the old town, $173 in zone II, and $115 in zones III and IV.

Split-Dalmatia County's tourist tax lump sum for private accommodation in Split for 2026 is about $81 per bed. If you keep the flat for yourself, the county instead charges a holiday-home lump sum of about $12 each for the first two household members and $6 for each further one.

The property tax (porez na nekretnine) replaced the holiday-home tax on 1 January 2025. The Tax Administration exempts a home used for permanent residence and one let long-term for at least ten months a year under a registered lease. Everything else pays what the city sets, from $0.69 to $9.21 per square metre nationally. Split charged $2.29 for 2025, and local reporting on the council's vote puts it at $5.76 from 2026, about $317 a year on the example flat.

Does owning property in Split give you residence in Croatia?

No. The Ministry of the Interior lists ten purposes for temporary stay under the Aliens Act: family reunification, education, scientific research, humanitarian grounds, life partnership, work, posted work, long-term residents of another EEA state, other purposes, and digital nomads. Property ownership is not among them. A non-EU owner without another ground has the Schengen short stay: 90 days in any 180, counted across every Schengen country.

EU and EEA citizens need no permit. They register their stay at the local police station no later than eight days after the first three months, with proof of health insurance, means of support and accommodation, and the certificate is free.

For a non-EU remote worker, the realistic route is the digital nomad temporary stay. The Ministry of the Interior's digital nomad page sets the income test at 2.5 times the previous year's average net monthly salary, currently about $4,170 a month, or savings of about $50,000 for 12 months or $75,000 for 18.

Stay is granted for up to 18 months, the page provides for an extension capped at six months, and after it expires you wait six months before applying again. The threshold moves with Croatian wages each year; our digital nomad visa comparison tracks it next to the other European schemes. Tax residence is a separate test again, and a home you own is one of the facts weighed in it, as our digital nomad tax guide explains.

Is buying property in Split worth it for a remote worker?

For an EU citizen the process is ordinary: an OIB, a clean extract, a conditional preliminary contract, a certified signature, registration within 60 days and 3% tax. The risk sits in the building rather than the law, mostly in the permit history of older Dalmatian stock.

For everyone else, timing decides. British and most American buyers can buy now with a consent that takes months. Australians cannot buy an existing home until April 2027, and Canadians wait on a verification. On the day Croatia joins the OECD, the consent step disappears for all of them. Frequently Asked Questions

Can Americans buy property in Split, Croatia?

Yes, with the Ministry of Justice's prior consent. The ministry's reciprocity list confirms reciprocity with the United States, except that residents of Arkansas, Hawaii, Kentucky, Minnesota, Mississippi, New Hampshire, Oklahoma and Vermont must meet a permanent or long-term residence condition. The purchase contract is signed first and consent requested afterwards.

How long does Ministry consent take for a property purchase in Croatia?

The General Administrative Procedure Act allows 60 days for a request that needs examination, and you can appeal if the ministry misses it. Two independent buyers' guides put the real wait at two to six months, so write consent into the contract as a condition. EU, EEA and Swiss buyers need no consent.

Do I need a lawyer to buy property in Split?

Not to draft the contract, but only a notary or a lawyer can file the electronic registration application. Foreign buyers use a lawyer to check the land registry and permits, draft the preliminary contract's conditions and prepare the consent file. We list eight lawyers in Split working in English or Italian.

What is the property transfer tax in Croatia?

The real estate transfer tax is 3% of market value, paid by the buyer, and a contract price below market can be reassessed. You pay within 15 days of receiving the assessment. A new build bought from a developer with VAT is excluded from the transfer tax, because VAT applies instead.

Can a foreigner buy agricultural land near Split?

EU, EEA and Swiss citizens can, on Croatian terms, since 1 July 2023. Other foreigners cannot, except by inheritance with reciprocity. The cadastre decides what counts, and olive groves, vineyards, orchards and gardens are all agricultural land, so check the recorded use of every parcel in the sale.

Does buying a flat in Split give me a Croatian residence permit?

No. Property ownership is not among the ten purposes for temporary stay under Croatia's Aliens Act. Non-EU owners need a separate ground, such as work or the digital nomad stay, which requires about $4,170 a month in income from abroad and is granted for up to 18 months.

Can I rent out a flat in a Split apartment building to tourists?

Only with prior written consent from a two-thirds majority of co-owners, including every neighbour whose walls, floors or ceilings adjoin the flat, plus a rental decision. In Split you then pay about $81 per bed in tourist tax and $115 to $288 per bed in flat-rate income tax each year.
