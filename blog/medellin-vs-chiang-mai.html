Medellín vs Chiang Mai: Which City Wins for Nomads in 2026?

Yannick Schroth February 2026 10 min read

Contents

  1. The Contenders
  2. Cost of Living
  3. Internet & Coworking
  4. Lifestyle & Culture
  5. Visa & Logistics
  6. The Nomad Community
  7. The Verdict

Ask a hundred digital nomads where they would base themselves tomorrow, and the same two names keep surfacing through the noise: Medellín and Chiang Mai. One hums with reggaeton and mountain air at 1,500 meters. The other smells of charcoal and lemongrass drifting off a night market. Both are cheap, both are loaded with remote workers, and they share almost nothing else. So which one earns your next flight?

I have logged more than six months in each city, spread across multiple stays, working full days as a remote software engineer the whole time. None of this is scraped from other blogs. It is what I actually paid, where the WiFi actually held up on calls, and which immigration queue actually ate my afternoon, all the way down to where I parked a laptop between Zoom meetings.

The Contenders

Medellín, Colombia, fills a green valley in the Andes at roughly 1,500 meters, high enough that the air stays soft and spring-like every month of the year. Locals call it the city of eternal spring and mean it. Two decades ago the name carried a far darker association. Today it runs a cable-car metro up the hillsides, a startup scene that actually ships products, and a tempo you do not find anywhere else in Latin America. It pulls in nomads who want mountains out the window, salsa leaking from the corner store, and a city that makes you feel awake.

Chiang Mai, Thailand, is the slow exhale to all that. Ringed by hazy hills in the north of the country, it stacks ancient temples next to motorbike repair shops and night markets, and it costs almost nothing to live well there. It was a working nomad hub before the word "nomad" got a hashtag, with coworking desks and meetups years ahead of the trend. It draws people chasing quiet, low overhead, and the rare luxury of turning a modest income into a genuinely comfortable life.

Both punch far above their size for remote workers. The real question is not which city wins on paper. It is which one matches how you actually work, what you can spend, and the kind of person you are after a hard week. So let us go category by category.

Cost of Living Compared

It is usually the first thing anyone asks, so here it is without padding: Chiang Mai is cheaper. The gap is not a rounding error. In almost every line of the budget, a dollar buys you more in northern Thailand than it does in Colombia's second city, and the difference adds up fast over a few months.

Accommodation

In Chiang Mai, a furnished one-bedroom in Nimman, the main nomad neighborhood, runs $350-550/month on a monthly lease. Drift out to Santitham or Chang Phueak and clean, modern studios drop to $250-400/month, often with utilities, WiFi, a rooftop pool, and a gym folded into the rent. Medellín asks more. A comparable one-bedroom in El Poblado, the obvious nomad zone, lands at $600-1,000/month on Airbnb. Shift to Laureles, the leafier, more local area where the longer-stay crowd settles, and that falls to $450-700/month. Find a direct rental through a local contact or a Facebook group and you can sneak under $500, but it takes legwork and usually a bit of Spanish.

Food & Coffee

Chiang Mai feeds you absurdly well for almost nothing. A bowl of khao soi, the rich northern curry noodle soup the city is known for, runs $1.50-2.50 from a neighborhood stall, the broth thick enough to count as lunch and dinner. Sit down at a proper restaurant and a full meal is $3-6. Specialty coffee at one of the dozens of serious cafes is $2-3.50, and a month of groceries from Rimping or Tops lands around $100-150. Medellín costs a notch more for less variety. Street arepas and empanadas go for $1-2, a set-lunch almuerzo at a local spot is $3-5, and dinner at a mid-range place runs $8-15. Coffee is $2.50-4, and groceries from Exito or Carulla average $150-200 a month.

Transport

This is the one category where Medellín flips the script. Its metro, cable cars and all, is genuinely good, and a single ride is about $0.70 with a monthly pass near $25. Uber fills the gaps, and a ride clear across town rarely tops $5. Chiang Mai has no real public transit at all. You rent a motorbike for $80-120/month and learn to weave through it, or lean on Grab, the Southeast Asian Uber, at $2-4 a trip. Red songthaews, the shared pickup trucks that rattle around town, undercut everything at $0.50-1, but they follow no route and you negotiate the fare through the window.

Bottom line: plan on $1,000-1,500/month to live comfortably in Chiang Mai, or $1,400-2,200/month in Medellín. Both would feel like theft against any Western city. On raw cost, though, Chiang Mai takes it cleanly.

Internet & Coworking

A call that drops mid-sentence can cost you a client, so this matters more than rent. Both cities hold up, in their own ways. Chiang Mai has been a nomad hub so long that its coworking layer is almost comically overbuilt; there are more spaces per head here than in cities ten times the size. Punspace, the old guard, rents hot desks for about $100-130/month. CAMP, up on the top floor of Maya Mall, is a free coworking space bankrolled by a Thai telecom, and it has become an institution for nomads counting every baht. Alt_ChiangMai runs $80-110/month and Yellow $120-150/month. Desk WiFi usually clocks 50-200 Mbps, and apartment fiber has shot up to 100-300 Mbps in most buildings.

Medellín has closed the gap quickly. Selina in El Poblado bolts a coworking floor onto hostel-style rooms at roughly $150-200/month for a desk. Tinkko skews more buttoned-up and corporate at $120-180/month, and a newer crop of Laureles spaces undercuts both at $80-130/month. The cafe culture pulls real weight too: plenty of coffee shops openly court laptop workers with outlets at every table, solid WiFi, and a stated welcome to sit and work. Home fiber generally holds at 50-150 Mbps across most neighborhoods, though I hit more dead spots and flaky evenings here than I ever did in Chiang Mai.

Where Chiang Mai truly pulls ahead is the sheer wall of work-friendly cafes. In Nimman alone you could open your laptop somewhere new every day for two months and never repeat. Half of them function as off-the-books coworking spaces, where nursing one latte for four hours raises exactly zero eyebrows. Medellín's scene is climbing toward that, but it is not there yet, and a few popular spots have quietly started shooing away the all-day laptop campers. If you are weighing Southeast Asia more broadly, our guide to coworking spaces in Bali is worth a read too.

Lifestyle & Culture

Weather

Medellín barely has weather, in the best sense. It holds 22-28°C (72-82°F) all year, no winter, no summer, just a rainy stretch (April-May and September-November) when the sky opens hard in the afternoon and clears by dinner. Chiang Mai swings more. The cool season (November-February) is the payoff, 15-28°C (59-82°F) under clean blue skies, the months everyone books for. Then the hot season (March-May) climbs past 35°C (95°F), and it lands right on the burning season, when farmers torch their fields and the air thickens into a gray haze you can taste. A lot of nomads simply leave for those weeks. The rainy season (June-October) is warm, green, and broken up by daily storms.

Food & Nightlife

Both cities feed you well, but they are not playing the same game. Chiang Mai serves some of the best food anywhere at prices that feel like a typo. The night markets alone justify the flight, rows of vendors ladling out pad thai, grilling northern sausage, and bagging mango sticky rice while you watch. Vegetarians and vegans eat like royalty here. Medellín is earthier and built around meat. Colombian plates lean on beans, rice, plantains, and whatever came off the grill, and the bandeja paisa is a heaped platter that can carry you through a full day of coding without a second thought. The fruit is the quiet star, though: lulo, guanabana, maracuya, things you will taste for the first time and then chase for weeks.

After dark, Medellín is not close. Salsa clubs in the center, reggaeton spilling out of El Poblado, a craft beer scene taking root in Laureles, it is a city that treats a Tuesday night as a real event. Chiang Mai keeps it mellow with rooftop bars, live-music joints, and the sprawling Zoe in Yellow complex, and that suits a lot of people just fine. It simply does not run at Medellín's voltage once the sun drops.

Safety

Chiang Mai is about as safe as a city gets in Southeast Asia. Violent crime against foreigners is close to nonexistent, and bag snatching or scams stay rare. The things that actually hurt people here are motorbike spills and the occasional territorial street dog. Medellín has come a long way, but it still asks you to keep your head up. Phone snatching and pickpocketing are genuine risks, especially where tourists cluster, and scopolamine drugging, while uncommon, is real enough that locals warn you about it. Stay in the neighborhoods you know, keep the expensive gear out of sight, and read the room after dark. Most long-term nomads here tell me they feel fine day to day. You just carry a low background hum of alertness that Chiang Mai never asks of you.

Visa & Logistics

Colombia hands most Western passport holders a 90-day visa on arrival, and you can extend it once for another 90 days at a Migración Colombia office. The extension costs around $50; the process is painless on paper and slow in the chair. Want to stay longer? Colombia rolled out a Digital Nomad Visa in 2022 (for a European alternative, see our Portugal digital nomad visa guide) good for up to two years of residency. You prove a monthly income of at least $2,750 (3x minimum wage), carry health insurance, and apply online, with a turnaround of roughly 2-4 weeks.

Thailand greets most nationalities with a 30-day visa exemption at the airport, extendable by another 30 days at an immigration office for about $16. Need more room? A 60-day tourist visa from a Thai embassy stretches to 90 days once extended. Thailand also runs a Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa aimed at remote workers, but the $80,000/year income bar quietly disqualifies most of the people reading this. The far friendlier option is the Destination Thailand Visa (DTV), live since mid-2024, which allows stays of up to 180 days against proof of remote work or digital skills. Plenty of nomads still bounce across the border to Laos or Myanmar to reset their stamp, though immigration has tightened the screws on serial visa runs.

The quieter dealbreaker is the clock. Time zones shape your whole day. Medellín sits on UTC-5, the same as US Eastern Time, which makes it close to perfect if your clients or team are in North America. You take meetings during their business hours and still have your evening to yourself. Chiang Mai runs on UTC+7, golden for Australian and East Asian work and rough on anything American. When your team in New York books a 9 AM standup, that is 9 PM for you, and the US-aligned nomads here quietly grind through evening shifts that wear on them month after month. Flights tell the same story. Medellín flies direct to Miami, Fort Lauderdale, and New York in 3-5 hours. Chiang Mai routes almost everything international through Bangkok, and getting there from Europe or North America usually means 15-20+ hours with layovers.

The Nomad Community

Chiang Mai's nomad community is one of the oldest on earth, and you feel that history the moment you arrive. This was basically ground zero for the whole movement in the early 2010s. The "Chiang Mai Digital Nomads" Facebook group runs past 80,000 members (both cities also rank near the top on Nomad List) and stays one of the busiest forums in the scene. Meetups, mastermind tables, and loose café hangouts happen most weeks, often splintered by niche: developers in one corner, content creators in another, e-commerce sellers and crypto traders somewhere past that. It skews young, scrappy, and entrepreneurial, thick with bootstrapped founders. The flip side is that it can feel like a closed loop, a room talking endlessly about location-independent living to itself.

Medellín's community is younger but filling out fast. The main hub, the "Digital Nomads Medellín" Facebook group, is active and genuinely useful when you first land. Weekly meetups float between coworking spaces and the cafes everyone already loves, and the crowd is simply more mixed: freelancers next to startup founders next to corporate remote staff next to retirees riding out the spring weather. The Latin energy gives it a social warmth Chiang Mai often misses. You also break out of the nomad bubble more easily here, especially with a little Spanish, since salsa classes, language exchanges, and neighborhood fiestas hand you a way in with locals.

One difference matters more than people expect: Chiang Mai's scene runs on a clock. The cool season (November-February) floods the city with nomads, and that is when the events and groups truly come alive. Come the hot and burning months, half of them scatter. Medellín stays steadier all year, propped up by the unchanging weather and a time zone that lines up with the Americas, though December and January still bring a clear surge.

The Verdict

So after all those months in both places, my honest answer is the unsatisfying one: nobody wins outright. The two cities are built for different people, and the right pick comes down to what you refuse to compromise on.

Choose Medellín if your clients sit in US time zones and you need that overlap to feel sane. Pick it if nightlife and a loud social calendar matter to you, if you want modern infrastructure (metro, Uber, delivery in minutes) wrapped around a city that still feels a little untamed, and if Spanish is in your life or about to be. The spring weather without the brutal heat is a real draw. So is trading a higher monthly burn for a city that never goes quiet.

Choose Chiang Mai if the budget is the whole point and you want every dollar to stretch. It suits the quiet, low-stimulation hours that deep work demands, and it rewards anyone who loves Southeast Asian food and rhythm. Working solo, or with clients across Asia-Pacific, makes the time zone a gift rather than a tax. You also get the most battle-hardened coworking and nomad infrastructure anywhere, plus a launchpad into the rest of the region.

New to all this and want the soft landing? Chiang Mai is the safer first bet. The whole place is pre-configured for you, the costs forgive your mistakes, and the community folds you in on day one. Got a few stamps in the passport and craving more friction and voltage? Medellín pays that back with one of the most alive nomad experiences going. Either way, you are picking off the top shelf. Frequently Asked Questions About Medellin vs Chiang Mai

Is Medellin or Chiang Mai cheaper for digital nomads?

Chiang Mai is cheaper across nearly every category. A comfortable life there runs about $1,000 to $1,500 a month, while Medellin tends to land between $1,400 and $2,200. Rent, food, and coffee all cost less in northern Thailand, so budget-focused nomads usually stretch further in Chiang Mai.

How does internet and coworking compare in Medellin vs Chiang Mai?

Both cities work well for remote work. Chiang Mai has unusually mature coworking, with hot desks around $80 to $150 and fiber from 100 to 300 Mbps. Medellin has caught up fast, offering desks near $80 to $200 and fiber of 50 to 150 Mbps, though speeds can be slightly less consistent.

Which city has a better time zone, Medellin or Chiang Mai?

Medellin sits on UTC-5, matching US Eastern Time, so it suits nomads serving North American clients with normal hours. Chiang Mai is on UTC+7, which fits Australian and East Asian teams but forces awkward evening shifts for US work. Your client base should drive this decision.

Is Chiang Mai or Medellin safer for remote workers?

Chiang Mai is one of the safest cities in Southeast Asia, with violent crime against foreigners almost unheard of and low petty theft. Medellin has improved enormously but needs more situational awareness, since phone snatching and pickpocketing happen. Stick to known neighborhoods and stay alert, especially after dark.

What are the visa options for Medellin and Chiang Mai?

Colombia gives most Western passports 90 days on arrival, extendable once, plus a Digital Nomad Visa for longer stays. Thailand offers a 30-day exemption, a 60-day tourist visa, and the newer Destination Thailand Visa for up to 180 days. Our digital nomad tax guide covers the wider picture.

How do the nomad communities in Medellin and Chiang Mai differ?

Chiang Mai has one of the oldest, largest nomad scenes, skewing younger and entrepreneurial, but it feels seasonal and peaks in the cool months. Medellin's community is newer, more diverse in age and profession, and stays consistent year-round thanks to its stable climate and Americas-friendly time zone.

Should a first-time nomad pick Medellin or Chiang Mai?

For the gentlest landing, Chiang Mai is the safer first choice, since the infrastructure is battle-tested and the community welcomes newcomers immediately.
