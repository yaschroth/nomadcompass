Siem Reap Digital Nomad Guide: Costs, Wifi and Visas

Yannick Schroth August 2026 10 min read

Siem Reap scores 10 for culture, 9 for cost and 9 for visas, and a comfortable month runs $700 to $1,200. Three numbers pull the other way: wifi 5, cleanliness 4, and a climate score of 5 that has more to do with humidity than with temperature. This guide is about the gap between those two sets.

It is a small city, and that cuts both ways. You can live well here on very little, next to one of the great archaeological sites on earth, and you can also run out of city after two or three months. Both are true, and which one you get depends mostly on when you come.

Cost of living in Siem Reap

Our Siem Reap city page puts a month at $660. A comfortable nomad month, not a survival one, runs $700 to $1,200.

A furnished apartment is $150 to $500 depending on the area. Local meals run $2 to $5. Coworking is $90 to $120 a month. Beer starts at $0.50 and a massage is $8 to $10. A budget traveller can sit at $600 a month without feeling deprived.

The important consequence is not that things are cheap. It is that the coworking desk, which is the single most useful thing you can buy here, costs about a tenth of what the same reliability costs in Western Europe. Buy it in week one.

Internet: 55 Mbps, and the generator matters more

Wifi scores 5 out of 10. Fixed-line speeds average around 55 Mbps, which is fine. The real variable is not bandwidth, it is power.

Outages happen, and they cluster in the rainy season when heavy storms come through. This is why the coworking spaces with backup generators are worth paying for if you have deadlines, and why cafe wifi, which is hit-and-miss at peak tourist hours anyway, is not somewhere to take a video call.

Mobile data is cheap enough to solve the rest. A 100GB SIM costs around $15, which makes hotspotting a genuine backup rather than a last resort. Get one at the airport.

Where to work in Siem Reap

The scene is small compared to Phnom Penh or Chiang Mai, and four places cover it properly.

Format CoWork

On Wat Bo Road, and the most productivity-focused option: 100 Mbps symmetric fibre and a backup generator, so a power cut does not stop your day. Consistently rated highest for speed and reliability.

AngkorHUB

150 Mbps, free coffee, a rooftop terrace and 24/7 access on premium plans. Day passes start around $7 and monthly memberships around $89. The easiest one to try for a day before committing.

BioLAB Coffee and Office

Also on Wat Bo Road, blending specialty coffee with proper desk infrastructure: ergonomic seating, two balconies and personal lockers. Rated 4.5 on Tripadvisor.

Footprint Cafes Hub

In Sala Kamroeuk, a social enterprise cafe downstairs with a quieter coworking floor above, plus community events and video recording equipment. The most sociable of the four.

Cafes

The Little Red Fox Espresso in Kandal Village is the nomad favourite and has reliably fast wifi. Common Grounds Cafe and Temple Coffee n Bakery along the riverside are the other consistent options.

Where to stay in Siem Reap

The five areas people talk about are genuinely different, and the choice matters more here than the price does.

Old Market, Psar Chas, is the tourist centre: convenient, lively, and the place where you will be sold something every 40 metres. Pub Street is the party zone and is loud in a way that is not negotiable. Neither is where most long-stayers end up.

Wat Bo, the residential east side, is quiet, cheap and genuinely local, and it is where two of the four coworking spaces sit. Sala Kamreuk is the expat residential zone: quiet streets, good restaurants, affordable rent, and the closest thing to a nomad neighbourhood. The French Quarter around Charles de Gaulle is the fifth option, closer to the temple road.

For a stay longer than a fortnight, Wat Bo or Sala Kamreuk. That is the short answer.

Getting around

Siem Reap is low-rise and spread out. The central area around Old Market, Pub Street and Wat Bo is walkable if the heat does not bother you. Beyond that you will use something with wheels, because there is no city bus network worth relying on.

Grab is the reliable default for tuk-tuks and cars, with fixed upfront pricing, and a cross-town tuk-tuk is $2 to $4. Tuk-tuks flagged on the street will quote tourist prices, so use the app or negotiate first. Bicycles rent for $3 to $5 a day and the terrain is flat. Motorbikes are $5 to $10 a day if you are comfortable in mixed traffic.

The international airport is about 10 km out. Grab runs $12 to $18, official airport taxis $25 to $35, and a newer airport bus around $8.

Visas: the reason a lot of people are here

Visas score 9 out of 10, and Siem Reap sits tenth in our nomad visa ranking. Cambodia has no dedicated digital nomad visa, and that is not the point: what it has is an extension system that quietly works better than most nomad visas do.

Most Western passport holders enter on a 30-day tourist visa, costing $30 by e-visa or on arrival, extendable once for another 30 days through a local agent. For anything longer, the E-class ordinary visa can be extended by 1, 3, 6 or 12 months through an agent without leaving the country.

One piece of admin catches people out: all air arrivals must complete the free Cambodia e-Arrival card online within seven days before flying. It costs nothing and takes minutes, and not having it is a problem at check-in rather than at the border. Our nomad visa finder covers the countries that do run formal nomad visas, if you want to compare.

Best time to go, and the two months to think twice about

Dry season, November to early April, is the comfortable window: roughly 24 to 32C, low humidity, little rain. December to February is the coolest and most pleasant stretch, and also peak tourist season, so accommodation costs more.

Rainy season, May to October, brings afternoon downpours, high humidity and more power cuts. Temperatures peak around 35C in April and May, which is the hardest stretch of the year. The rain itself is usually short-lived, mornings are often clear, and prices drop a long way. September is the wettest month at around 387mm.

The nomad sweet spot is October to November: the rain tapers off, the landscape is still green, the crowds have not returned and prices are still low.

The caveat that does not appear in most guides is air quality. It worsens noticeably from January through March because of regional agricultural burning, which is the same stretch the weather is otherwise at its best. If you are sensitive to air pollution, check current readings before committing to those months rather than after arriving.

The 10 for culture

Culture is the highest score in the whole profile, and it is not a close call. Angkor Wat and dozens of surrounding temples sit within easy reach of the city, and a multi-day pass turns them from a sightseeing tick into something you can return to on quiet mornings.

This is the thing that makes Siem Reap different from every other cheap Southeast Asian base. Chiang Mai has better infrastructure and a bigger scene. It does not have this.

Community: small, and unusually purposeful

Community scores 6 out of 10. AngkorHUB and Footprint Cafes run events and connect people, and informal meetups happen regularly. It does not compare in scale to Chiang Mai or Bali, and it would be misleading to suggest otherwise.

What it has instead is character. A large share of the long-term foreign population works in social enterprise or the NGO sector, which gives the scene a more purpose-driven feel than a typical nomad hub. Whether that appeals is personal, but it is a real difference.

The honest downsides

Cleanliness scores 4, the lowest number in the profile, and air quality 6. Tourism dependency means constant sales pressure in the central areas, and interactions there can feel transactional in a way that wears on you over weeks.

The other one is size. Siem Reap is quiet and can start to feel repetitive after two to three months. Plenty of people treat that as a feature and use it as a low-cost base between busier stops. Just do not plan a year here on the strength of a good fortnight.

Who Siem Reap is for

It suits someone building runway, someone who wants a genuinely low burn rate without living badly, and anyone for whom being 20 minutes from Angkor is worth more than a large coworking scene. Safety scores 6 and violent crime is rare.

It does not suit anyone who needs guaranteed uptime without a generator behind it, anyone sensitive to air quality in the first quarter of the year, or anyone who needs a big city. If the budget is the driver, our cheapest cities ranking covers the rest of the field.

Frequently asked questions about Siem Reap

How much does it cost to live in Siem Reap as a digital nomad?

A comfortable month is $700 to $1,200. A furnished apartment runs $150 to $500, local meals $2 to $5, and coworking $90 to $120 a month. Budget travellers manage on around $600 without much sacrifice.

Is the internet in Siem Reap good enough for remote work?

Yes, with a caveat about power rather than speed. Fixed-line averages around 55 Mbps and coworking spaces run 100 to 150 Mbps. Outages happen in rainy season, so a space with a backup generator plus a cheap 100GB SIM as a hotspot covers you.

Does Cambodia have a digital nomad visa?

No. It has something arguably more useful: a $30 30-day tourist visa extendable once, and an E-class ordinary visa that agents can extend by 1, 3, 6 or 12 months without you leaving the country.

What is the Cambodia e-Arrival card?

A free online form that every air arrival must complete within seven days before flying. It takes minutes and costs nothing, but missing it causes problems at check-in, so do it before you leave for the airport.

When is the best time to work from Siem Reap?

October to November is the sweet spot: rain tapering off, green landscape, low prices, no crowds. December to February is the most comfortable weather but the busiest and most expensive, and January to March carries the worst air quality.

Which neighbourhood should a remote worker stay in?

Wat Bo or Sala Kamreuk. Both are quiet, affordable and residential, and Wat Bo holds two of the four coworking spaces. Old Market is convenient but very touristy, and Pub Street is too loud to live in.

Is Siem Reap safe?

Safety scores 6 out of 10 and violent crime is rare, including for solo travellers. The everyday issues are petty theft, traffic if you ride a motorbike, and persistent sales pressure in the tourist core.

How long do people usually stay in Siem Reap?

Weeks to a few months. The city is small and can feel repetitive after two to three months, which is why many nomads use it as a low-cost base between longer stops rather than as a year-round home.
