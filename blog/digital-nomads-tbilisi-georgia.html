Why Digital Nomads Are Flocking to Tbilisi, Georgia

Yannick Schroth January 2026 7 min read

Contents

  1. Why Tbilisi?
  2. Cost of Living
  3. Best Neighborhoods
  4. Internet & Coworking
  5. Visa & Stay
  6. Culture, Food & Nightlife
  7. Final Verdict

Picture a sulfur bathhouse with a brick dome, steam curling off it into the cold, and a medieval fortress on the hill directly above. Now picture a $3 taxi dropping you there straight from a natural-wine bar where the laptops outnumber the locals. That contrast is Tbilisi, the capital of Georgia, and it explains better than any statistic why remote workers keep showing up here and not leaving.

I landed in late 2020, when Georgia threw its doors open to remote workers while most of Europe was bolting theirs shut. The plan was two months. I have now logged over fourteen months in the country across several stays, and the pattern repeats every time: the nomad scene is a little bigger than I left it, and the city is somehow exactly as rough and unvarnished as before. That stubbornness is the whole appeal.

Why Tbilisi?

None of this happened by accident. During the pandemic, Georgia launched its "Remotely from Georgia" program, a streamlined path for remote workers to relocate. The policy got people in the door. What kept them was something the established hubs like Bali and Lisbon had long since spent: novelty. Tbilisi is not an Instagram beach town or a European capital your whole feed has already photographed. Here, ninth-century churches share a hillside with brutalist Soviet housing blocks, and the line between ancient and falling-apart is not always clear.

On paper the math is loud, and in practice it is louder. You can rent a decent apartment for $400, eat out every night for less than a week of groceries costs in Berlin, stay a full year without touching a visa form, and pull download speeds that embarrass plenty of Western European flats. Then there is the food, one of the planet's oldest and strangest wine-and-bread traditions, a nightlife scene that keeps getting written up abroad, and a national habit of hospitality that can feel almost confrontational. Refusing a third glass at a Georgian table is its own small negotiation.

There is a harder-to-pin-down quality too. Tbilisi runs on a creative, mildly chaotic current that selects for a specific kind of nomad: less the person collecting business cards at a coworking mixer, more the person who ends up in a basement wine cellar at midnight with three Georgian painters and no clear plan to leave. It does not click for everyone. For the people it does click with, it clicks hard and stays clicked.

Cost of Living for Digital Nomads

This is the part that closes the deal. Even measured against the rest of Eastern Europe, Tbilisi is cheap, and from a Western European or North American salary it stops feeling like a budget and starts feeling like a cheat code. The usual catch does not apply here. Cheap places often mean small rooms and grim dinners. In Tbilisi the apartments are big, the food is some of the best you will eat all year, and daily life feels comfortable rather than like an exercise in going without.

Accommodation

A furnished one-bedroom in a central neighborhood like Vera or Vake runs $350-550/month short-term. Commit to three months or more and you can talk landlords down to $250-400/month without much effort. Two-bedrooms in the same streets go for $450-700/month. And forget the mental image of a shoebox studio. Georgian flats tend to come with high ceilings, a balcony, and enough square meters to spread out a desk, a second monitor, and your laundry drying rack all at once.

One rule saves you the most money: do not book long stays through Airbnb. Work the local Facebook groups ("Tbilisi Expats," "Tbilisi Apartments for Rent") or the Georgian listing site SS.ge instead. Landlords here are flexible, and most will settle a monthly rate over a single phone call. I have watched people land a renovated Old Town flat for under $400 simply because they walked the neighborhood and asked the right cafe owner who had a cousin with a spare apartment.

Food & Coffee

Georgian food is one of the world's great underrated cuisines, and the prices border on unfair. A proper sit-down dinner of khinkali (soup dumplings you eat by hand), khachapuri (boat-shaped cheese bread), a salad, and a beer lands at $5-8. A flat white from one of the city's serious third-wave cafes runs $1.50-3. A week of groceries from Carrefour or Goodwill is about $25-40. Add it up and you can eat extremely well, dining out most nights, for $250-350/month.

Transport

The metro charges $0.20 per ride on a rechargeable card that also covers the buses. For door-to-door, Bolt is the local ride-hailing app, and most cross-town trips clear at $1-3. The airport into the center runs about $8-12. Honestly, plenty of nomads never load a metro card at all, because at those taxi prices the math rarely justifies the walk to the station.

Bottom line: a comfortable nomad life in Tbilisi lands at $800-1,200/month, central apartment and weekend nights out included. Watch your spending a little and $600-800/month works without any sense of going without. Few capital cities anywhere return this much quality of life per dollar.

Best Neighborhoods to Stay In

Tbilisi is small enough that the neighborhoods worth living in mostly sit within a walk of one another. The Mtkvari River cuts through the center, and the best nomad areas cluster on both banks. Here is where to look.

Vera: the default nomad neighborhood, and the choice is earned. Vera climbs the hills just north of Rustaveli Avenue, the main boulevard, in a tangle of crumbling art nouveau facades, independent cafes, wine bars, and one-room galleries. It is walkable. It is quiet enough to take a call from your kitchen table and loud enough that you never feel marooned. Most of the city's good coworking and serious coffee is here or a few streets over. Want to be in the middle of the scene? This is the address.

Vake: the polished, pricier cousin one rung up. Vake is Tbilisi's upscale district: tree-lined streets, buildings that have actually been maintained, restaurants worth a reservation, and Vake Park, one of the city's best stretches of green. It reads as more put-together, which some nomads want. Figure a 15-minute walk or a $1.50 Bolt ride to the center, with rents 10-20% above Vera and still, by any honest measure, cheap.

Old Town / Abanotubani: the historic core, and the most photogenic place to wake up in the city. Narrow lanes, carved wooden balconies, the sulfur bathhouses, churches notched into the hillside under Narikala Fortress. You get beauty and tourist foot traffic in roughly equal measure, so it suits a short run of one or two months, or anyone who can tune out crowds and noise. Having the sulfur baths a two-minute walk from your front door is a genuinely good way to end a workday.

Saburtalo: the most local option on the list, by a distance. This is a big residential district with a Soviet-era backbone: wide boulevards, tall apartment blocks, supermarkets the size of warehouses. A lot of Tbilisi State University students live here, which keeps the energy young. Rents are the lowest of the four, and you will queue at the same markets as Georgian families instead of fellow laptop owners. Pick it when you want immersion over convenience.

Internet & Coworking Spaces

The internet is better than the city's rough edges lead you to expect, and it keeps improving year on year. Most apartments ship with fiber at 50-100 Mbps, and newer buildings reach 150-300 Mbps. Mobile data is fast and almost free: Magti or Geocell sell unlimited 4G for roughly $5-8/month. I have run Zoom calls from cafes, from a park bench, and once from the back of a rattling marshrutka (the shared minibus) without anyone on the other end noticing.

The caveat is the edge case. If your day depends on rock-solid uptime, live streams, fat file uploads, video work, treat a coworking membership as insurance rather than a luxury. The scene has grown up a lot, and three spaces anchor it.

  • Impact Hub Tbilisi: The most established space in the city, located near Vera. Professional environment, fast and reliable internet, regular community events, and a mix of local startups and international remote workers. Around $80-120/month for a flexible desk.
  • Terminal: A creative-leaning coworking space popular with designers and developers. Good vibes, solid internet, and a cafe on-site. Membership runs about $70-100/month.
  • Lokal: Part cafe, part coworking space, and very popular with the nomad crowd. The atmosphere is relaxed and social, making it easy to meet people. Day passes available for about $5-8, monthly memberships around $60-80.

Plenty of nomads skip the memberships and run their whole week out of the cafe scene. Prospero's Books, Entrée, and Stamba Cafe are laptop-friendly and will not glare at you for nursing one coffee through a four-hour deep-work block. Visa & Stay

Here is where Georgia simply beats almost everywhere else. Citizens of 95 countries, the US, Canada, UK, EU, Australia, and most of South America among them, can walk in and stay 365 days without a visa. No application. No fee. No proof-of-income spreadsheet. You hand over your passport, the officer stamps it, and you have a year.

Sit with that number for a second. Nomads in Thailand reset the clock with a border run every 60 days. Schengen rules push you out after 90 days in any 180, unless you go fight for something like Portugal's D8 visa. Georgia hands you a full calendar year and asks for nothing. When the 365 days run out, you ride a few hours to Armenia or Turkey, cross the border, come back, and a fresh year begins on the next stamp. Some Tbilisi regulars have run this loop for the better part of a decade.

If you want something more formal, Georgia runs a freelancer-friendly residency program, and it does not tax foreign-sourced income for anyone staying under 183 days. Cross that line into tax residency and the flat income tax rate is 20%, though exemptions and the country's "Small Business Status" can shave that down hard. Talk to a tax professional about your own numbers, and our digital nomad tax guide covers the wider picture. The point stands: the baseline legal framework is about as nomad-friendly as they come.

"Georgia's 365-day visa-free policy is a game-changer. No other country in the world makes it this easy for remote workers to just show up and stay. It removes the single biggest headache of the nomad lifestyle."

Culture, Food & Nightlife

Georgian culture is ancient and fiercely proud of it. The country has its own alphabet, one of only 14 in the world, a UNESCO-recognized polyphonic singing tradition, and a serious claim to being the birthplace of wine, with 8,000-year-old archaeology to back it up. Living here means soaking in a culture that genuinely does not resemble anywhere else, and that strangeness is a big slice of why nomads keep coming back.

The food alone justifies the flight. Stay a few weeks and you will have opinions: which bakery pulls the best shotis puri from its clay oven, whether Imeretian or Adjarian khachapuri wins, and exactly how many khinkali you can put away in one sitting (always more than you planned, and the count is on the table, since you pay by the dumpling). Look past the headline dishes too, into lobio (bean stew), pkhali (walnut-and-herb spreads), churchkhela (a grape-and-walnut candy strung like a candle), and mtsvadi (grilled meat). Then the wine, made by the qvevri method in clay vessels buried in the ground, tasting like nothing in a Western bottle shop. The amber wines, in particular, will rearrange what you thought wine could be.

Nightlife is its own reason to come. Fabrika, a Soviet sewing factory rebuilt into a complex of hostels, studios, cafes, and shops in Marjanishvili, is the unofficial living room for nomads and creatives. Its courtyard fills every evening with people working through bottles of natural wine. Then there is the techno. Tbilisi gets called the "Berlin of the Caucasus" and earns it: Bassiani, built into a swimming pool under a football stadium, ranks among the best clubs on earth, with Khidi, Mtkvarze, and Cafe Gallery filling out the rest. Nothing here starts before 2 AM and a real night can run past noon, so pace yourself and eat first.

Final Verdict

Tbilisi is not perfect, and pretending otherwise does you no favors. The infrastructure stutters. Outside the nomad bubble the language barrier is real, and Georgian, with that gorgeous alien alphabet, is genuinely hard to crack. Winter air quality drops. Healthcare is improving but still trails Western Europe. And the same roughness that makes the city feel alive can grind on you by month four, usually on the day the hot water goes out mid-shower.

Best for: budget-conscious nomads who want every dollar to stretch without giving up comfort. Restless types who are sick of the usual circuit and want somewhere that does not look like everyone else's photos. Freelancers who rank visa simplicity above all. Anyone who reads "8,000-year-old wine culture" and immediately wants a glass. People who are drawn to cities with a creative, slightly off-kilter pulse.

Not ideal for: nomads who need polished, dependable systems every single day (point yourself at Lisbon or Tallinn). Anyone whose business runs on a large English-speaking professional network. Families needing international schools, where the options are few and steep. And anyone who cannot get through a week without a Whole Foods run or mail that reliably arrives.

So, the bottom line. At $800-1,200/month, with a 365-day visa-free stay, food that rewires your standards, internet that holds up, and a culture that genuinely stretches how you see the world, Tbilisi offers a deal almost no other city can match. It is not the most convenient base. It might be the most rewarding one you ever pick. Browse more city guides on our blog to set Tbilisi against the rest of the field.

Frequently Asked Questions About Tbilisi, Georgia for Nomads

How much does it cost to live in Tbilisi as a digital nomad?

A comfortable nomad lifestyle in Tbilisi runs roughly $800 to $1,200 a month, covering a central apartment, eating out regularly, coworking, and weekends out. Budget-conscious nomads manage on $600 to $800 without feeling deprived, making it one of the cheapest capital cities for the quality of life on offer.

Do digital nomads need a visa for Georgia?

For most nomads, no. Citizens of 95 countries, including the US, UK, EU, Canada, and Australia, can enter Georgia and stay 365 days visa-free, with no application, fees, or income requirements. When the year is up, a quick border run to Armenia or Turkey resets the clock.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Tbilisi?

Yes. Most Tbilisi apartments have fiber offering 50 to 100 Mbps, and newer buildings reach 150 to 300 Mbps. Mobile data from Magti or Geocell is cheap and fast. If your work needs rock-solid connectivity, a coworking space is a smart backup.

Which Tbilisi neighborhoods are best for digital nomads?

Vera is the nomad neighborhood of choice, leafy and walkable with cafes and coworking nearby. Vake is a polished, slightly pricier upscale district. Old Town suits short stays surrounded by beauty, while Saburtalo offers the lowest rents and a more local, immersive feel.

How does Tbilisi compare to other top nomad cities in Europe?

Tbilisi undercuts most of Europe on cost while offering a 365-day visa-free stay that Schengen capitals cannot match. It trades polished infrastructure for raw character and novelty. To weigh it against pricier hubs, see our roundup of the best European cities for nomads.

What is the food and nightlife like in Tbilisi, Georgia?

Georgian cuisine is hearty and underrated: khinkali, khachapuri, and qvevri-made wine, with full meals around $5 to $8. Nightlife punches above its weight, from Fabrika's courtyard to world-class techno at Bassiani. Clubs rarely get going before 2 AM, so pace yourself.

Is Tbilisi the right base for your nomad style?

Tbilisi suits budget-minded, adventurous nomads who value visa simplicity, great food, and offbeat creative energy over polished infrastructure. It is less ideal if you need reliable systems or a large English-speaking network. Try our Nomad Taste Wheel or the full Tbilisi city profile to decide.
