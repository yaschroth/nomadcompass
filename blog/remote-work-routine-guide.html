Remote Work

Remote Work Routine: How to Plan Your Day and Week

Yannick Schroth February 2025 13 min read

Say it is 7 a.m. in Lisbon. The alarm went off at the same minute as yesterday, the 28 tram is rattling up through Graça outside, and nobody expects you online for another two hours. That quiet is the best and the worst thing about remote work. There is no commute to start the day and no office closing its doors to end it, so the only structure your day will have is the structure you give it.

The remote work routine that holds up is built around your body clock rather than your willpower. It has three fixed points: the same wake time every day, the hardest work in the first hours, and a hard stop in the evening. Everything between them can bend to meetings, errands and the city outside. Each of those three points has solid research behind it, and none of it needs an app.

Without them, the day spreads. When lockdowns pushed millions of people home in 2020, an analysis of meeting and email data from over three million users found the average workday grew by 48.5 minutes. At Microsoft, researchers noticed that about 30 percent of the employees they studied had developed a third burst of work in the hours before bed. A routine is how you get those evenings back.

A remote work routine starts with a fixed wake time

Start with the alarm, because everything else in the table hangs from it. A study of 60,977 UK Biobank participants, who wore wrist activity trackers for a week, scored how closely each person's sleep and wake times matched from day to day. Those with the most regular patterns had a 20 to 48 percent lower risk of death over the follow-up, and regularity predicted that risk better than how long people slept.

Length still matters. The CDC recommends seven or more hours a night for adults aged 18 to 60, and its first habit for better sleep is going to bed and getting up at the same time every day. So fix the wake time first, then count back seven and a half hours to find your bedtime. If 7:00 is the alarm, 11:30 p.m. is the latest you should be asleep, and the table above builds in a margin.

The weekend is where most routines leak. Sleeping in opens a gap between your body clock and your social clock that researchers call social jetlag, and in a large study it was linked to a higher body mass index regardless of sleep length. Keep the weekend alarm within an hour of the weekday one.

Morning light and coffee: setting the clock for the workday

Light is the signal that sets your internal clock, and when you get it matters. In 2022 an international panel of sleep and light researchers published recommendations for daytime, evening and night-time light. During the day they want at least 250 lux of melanopic light at the eye, a measure weighted for the cells that set the clock. In practice that means daylight: a walk before work or a desk facing a window.

That is why the inbox stays shut in the 7:15 slot. Twenty minutes outside gives your clock its bright signal and gets you to the desk with your own plan rather than someone else's requests.

Coffee needs a deadline. In a controlled study, 400 mg of caffeine, which the FDA equates to about two to three 12-ounce cups of coffee, taken six hours before bedtime still reduced sleep by more than an hour on objective measurement. The researchers concluded that substantial caffeine should stop at least six hours before bed. With a 10:30 p.m. bedtime, that makes 4:30 p.m. the cut-off, and earlier is better.

Deep work first: protect the morning of your remote workday

Cal Newport's 2016 book Deep Work rests on one argument: the ability to focus without distraction is becoming more and more valuable. For a remote worker the distraction is rarely a colleague at your shoulder. It is the notification, the Slack badge, the email that arrives while you are halfway through a hard paragraph.

What those interruptions cost is not what most people assume. In a lab study by researchers from UC Irvine and Humboldt University Berlin, 48 participants answered work emails while being interrupted by phone or chat. The interrupted groups actually finished faster, with no drop in quality, but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort, and the researchers saw that after only 20 minutes of interrupted work. You can keep up with a fragmented day. You pay for it in how the day feels.

So the first block of the morning gets the task that needs your whole head, and nothing else. Turn notifications off rather than to silent, and put chat and email into two or three fixed windows, the first of them after the block ends. No study sets the perfect block length; choose one you can hold without checking your phone, and lengthen it once that is easy.

Breaks in a remote work routine: short, often, and on your feet

At home nobody walks you to a meeting room, and whole mornings can pass without standing up. Breaks are worth planning for that reason alone. A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 22 study samples with 2,335 participants and found that micro-breaks of ten minutes or less raise vigour and reduce fatigue. Their effect on overall performance was small and not significant, and longer breaks helped performance more.

The same analysis found performance gains mainly on less demanding tasks, and the authors suggest that recovering from hard cognitive work may need more than ten minutes. Read together, that gives a simple pattern: short breaks throughout the day to stay fresh, and a real break, such as lunch away from the screen, after your heaviest block.

If you like a timer, the Pomodoro Technique, devised by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, works in 25-minute intervals with a short break after each and a longer one of 20 to 30 minutes after four. It suits admin better than long writing, where the bell can cut off good focus.

The body in a remote work routine: sitting, walking and the weekly minimum

The breaks do a second job. In a crossover trial, 19 overweight adults spent seven hours seated, either without a break or with two minutes of walking every 20 minutes. After a sugary drink, the walking breaks lowered their glucose and insulin responses compared with sitting straight through. A two-minute lap of the flat or the street is enough to count.

Short walks are not a workout, though. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines ask adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days. Put the workout right after the shutdown, where it also marks the end of the working day. In Lisbon that could be a run along the Tagus.

The afternoon dip: where calls and admin belong in the remote workday

The early afternoon slump has a cause. NIOSH, the US occupational safety institute, describes the afternoon, about 2 to 5 p.m., as one of the most vulnerable times for alertness: the body clock's push for wakefulness dips while the pressure to sleep has been building since morning.

Put that stretch to work instead of fighting it. Calls, where another person keeps you alert, belong here, along with replies, invoices and booking the next flat: tasks that need attention but not depth. With a team in other time zones, the overlap often falls in the afternoon anyway.

End the remote workday with a shutdown ritual

The hard stop is the fixed point people skip, and the one that keeps the evening from becoming that third burst of work. Cal Newport described his version in 2009: update the task lists, check the calendar for the next two weeks, review the weekly plan, then say a set phrase, "schedule shutdown, complete". Any work thought that surfaces later meets the fact that it is written down.

It sounds like a gimmick, and there is psychology under it. Unfinished goals keep pulling at the mind: in a series of studies, they caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task and worse performance on anagram puzzles. When participants wrote a specific plan for the unfinished goal, those effects disappeared. The list does the remembering, so your head can stop.

The same trick works at bedtime. In a sleep-lab study of 57 young adults, those who wrote a to-do list for the coming days fell asleep about nine minutes faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already finished, and the more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep. If work thoughts follow you to bed, keep a notebook on the nightstand.

Evening light and sleep: the other end of the routine

The morning sets the clock and the evening can undo it. The same light panel recommends no more than 10 lux of melanopic light at the eye from at least three hours before bed, and a bedroom as dark as possible, at most 1 lux. In a flat, that means a couple of warm lamps rather than the ceiling light, from about 7:30 p.m. if you sleep at 10:30.

The CDC adds the familiar habits: turn devices off at least 30 minutes before bed, and keep the bedroom quiet, relaxing and cool. In short-let flats with thin curtains and street noise, an eye mask and earplugs are the cheapest way to get there, and they travel in a jacket pocket.

None of this means staying in. The evening is when the city is yours: a sunset from a viewpoint, dinner with people from the coworking space. Just come home to low light.

The weekly rhythm of a remote work routine

A day plan without a week plan drifts. Once a week, on Friday afternoon or Sunday evening, look at the week ahead and put the deep work blocks into the calendar before anything else, so meetings have to fit around them rather than the other way round. It is the same move as the shutdown ritual, scaled up: decide in advance, write it down, stop carrying it in your head.

Then protect at least one day with no work at all. A 2017 synthesis of recovery research found that psychological detachment from work and physical exercise go with less exhaustion and more vigour, that recovery suffers when job stress is high, and that its benefits seem stronger in natural settings. A weekend that ends at the beach, on a trail or in a park is doing real work for Monday.

Keep the wake time through all of it, within that hour: five disciplined mornings are easily undone by two late ones.

Taking your remote work routine on the road

None of this depends on a particular cafe or gym, so it moves with you. Keep the skeleton and change the details: the same wake time, first block and shutdown, in a new flat with a new walk to the water. What shifts the clock times themselves is travel: time zones, jet lag, wifi that cannot carry a call. Frequently Asked Questions

What does a good daily remote work routine look like?

It has three fixed points: the same wake time every day, your hardest work in the first hours with notifications off, and a hard stop with a short shutdown ritual. Calls and admin go in the afternoon, exercise after work, and the evening stays dim before bed.

How long should deep work blocks be in a remote work routine?

No study sets an ideal length. Start with a block you can hold without checking your phone, even a short one, and extend it as that gets easy. What matters most is keeping it free of interruptions, which raise stress even when the work gets done.

How often should remote workers take breaks?

Take short breaks through the day and a longer one after demanding work. Micro-breaks of ten minutes or less raise energy and cut fatigue, and two minutes of walking every 20 minutes lowered blood sugar responses compared with sitting straight through.

When should you stop drinking coffee in a remote work routine?

At least six hours before bed. In a controlled study, 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bedtime still cut sleep by more than an hour. With a 10:30 p.m. bedtime, the last coffee should come by 4:30 p.m.

How do you stop remote work from taking over your evenings?

End every workday with a shutdown ritual: update your task list, check the next days in the calendar and write a specific plan for anything unfinished. Studies show a written plan stops unfinished goals from intruding on your thoughts, which is what lets the evening start.

Should a remote work routine change at the weekend?

Keep the wake time within about an hour of your weekday one. Sleeping in creates social jetlag, a mismatch between body clock and schedule, while regular sleep timing is linked to better health. Take at least one full day completely off work.
