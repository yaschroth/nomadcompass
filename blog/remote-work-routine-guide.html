Remote Work

How to Build a Remote Work Routine That Actually Works

Yannick Schroth February 2026 8 min read

Contents

  2. Designing Your Morning
  3. Your Workspace Strategy

  5. Async Communication
  6. Protecting Your Evenings

It's 11 a.m. on a Tuesday in a perfectly nice Bangkok apartment, and you've answered four Slack threads, scrolled two feeds, and started exactly nothing. Here's the paradox nobody warns you about: the more freedom you have, the more structure you need to use it. Strip away the office and your days dissolve into half-finished tasks, guilt about the temple you didn't visit, and the slow conviction that you're failing at work and travel both at once.

I've spent over two years working remotely from Southeast Asian cities, mostly out of Bangkok, and I've watched sharp, capable people burn out because they read "flexible schedule" as "no schedule." What follows is the system I built through trial, error, and far too many 2 a.m. deadline scrambles. It holds up in a Chiang Mai cafe and a Lisbon coworking space alike, and it survives the one thing that wrecks most routines: packing up for a new city.

Why Routine Matters More When You're Nomadic

In an office, the routine is built for you. The commute, the desk, the lunch break, the 5 PM stampede for the door: every one is a cue that shapes your day while you barely notice. Take them away and you're staring at a blank canvas most people have no idea how to fill. The freedom feels great for about a week, then starts to feel like quicksand.

Decision fatigue is the reason. Every choice draws down the same mental fuel you need for creative, high-quality work, and when you're nomadic the choices breed. Where do I work today? When do I start? When do I eat? Is now the moment for that temple, or after lunch, or never? A routine quietly settles dozens of these, so your best energy goes to the work that pays the rent, not the logistics around it.

Then there's the anchor effect. When the language, the food, and the street noise all shift under you every few weeks, your routine becomes the one fixed point, the thread stitching your days together across time zones. The nomads who last, in my experience, aren't the most adventurous ones. They're the ones carrying the most portable routine.

If you're struggling with this balance, our guide on staying productive while working abroad covers the foundational mindset shifts you'll need.

Designing Your Morning

Your morning sets the terms for everything after it. Get the first ninety minutes right and the rest of the day tends to fall into line. Get them wrong and you'll spend the afternoon chasing momentum you never built.

Anchor Rituals

An anchor ritual is one small, repeatable action that quietly announces the day has started. The action barely matters; the repetition is the whole point. Mine is a cup of chai, steam fogging the window while I write three priorities on a sticky note. Yes, the stereotype is real, and it earned its reputation. Some people journal. Some meditate. Some stretch for ten minutes. Pick one and keep it.

The catch is that the ritual has to be location-independent. "Coffee at my favorite cafe" collapses the moment that cafe is 4,000 miles behind you. "Tea and three priorities" travels in your carry-on and works in any kitchen.

Move Your Body

Exercise before work isn't a bonus round; it's the price of doing this for years instead of months. Eight hours a day folded into cafe chairs and coworking stools will quietly wreck your back, your hips, and your mood. I move for 30 minutes every morning, usually a run, sometimes yoga on a hostel floor, sometimes a slow walk through whatever neighborhood I've landed in. The form is negotiable. Showing up isn't.

The No-Phone First Hour

This is the single habit that changed the most for me. For the first hour after waking, Slack, email, and social media stay closed. No exceptions, not even a "quick glance." The logic is simple: the instant you open your inbox, you flip from running your day to reacting to someone else's. Their priorities become yours. Your sharpest hour gets spent dousing fires that would have waited until ten.

Spend that hour on the anchor ritual, the movement, and a quiet sketch of what the day needs from you. Sit down to work after that and you arrive with a clarity most people never feel, because they opened their eyes and immediately opened their email.

Your Workspace Strategy

The "work from anywhere" fantasy is always a laptop on a beach, screen invisible in the glare, sand in the ports. Real work needs a real strategy, and the one that holds up across a dozen cities is a rotation.

The Three-Place Rotation

I keep three kinds of workspace in play and pick by the task in front of me:

  • Coworking space: For deep work, video calls, and anything that dies without reliable internet. This is your professional base. If you're scouting Bali, our roundup of the best coworking spaces in Bali is a useful sense of what good looks like.
  • Cafe: For the lighter stuff: email, planning, loose brainstorming, first drafts. The low background hum genuinely helps creative work. Cap a session at 2-3 hours, buy something every 90 minutes, and don't be the person nursing one iced coffee until closing.
  • Home/apartment: For early mornings, late focus sessions, and anything you can do in a t-shirt and no plans. A folding laptop stand and a real pair of noise-cancelling headphones earn their place in the bag fast.

The rotation does two jobs: it kills monotony and matches the room to your energy. Hard focus goes to the coworking desk, creative drift to the cafe, whatever's left to home.

Scouting a New City

Give your first two days in a new city to scouting. Walk into three coworking spaces, sit in two or three cafes long enough to feel the wifi and the noise, and run a real speed test on your apartment's connection. By day three the rotation should be settled. In Bangkok, where I spend most of my year, it took a single afternoon, because the infrastructure there is genuinely that good. It's also gloriously cheap; our Bangkok budget guide has the full breakdown.

Structuring Deep Work Blocks

Cal Newport's Deep Work belongs on every remote worker's shelf. The core claim is short: the ability to focus without distraction on something cognitively hard is getting rarer and more valuable at once. Do it consistently and you've handed yourself an unfair advantage over everyone refreshing an inbox.

Time Blocking

I run my day in blocks, not lists. A task list says "write blog post" and lets you start whenever guilt finally wins. A time block says "9:00 to 11:00: write the blog post, nothing else exists." That second version is a promise with a start time attached, so there's nothing left to negotiate when nine arrives.

The shape I keep coming back to: two deep work blocks of 90 to 120 minutes each in the morning, split by a 15-minute break to refill the cup and move. Afternoons hold meetings, admin, and lighter stuff. That split isn't a personal quirk; it tracks the energy curve most people already ride whether they plan for it or not.

The Pomodoro Variation

If a 90-minute block feels like staring down a cliff, shrink it. The Pomodoro Technique runs 25 minutes of focus, a 5-minute break, repeat, then a longer 15 to 30 minute break after four rounds. I track the time with Toggl, and watching where the hours genuinely go, versus where I assumed they went, was a quietly humbling week.

Energy Mapping

Not all hours pull the same weight. Log your energy honestly for one week and your own pattern shows up in the data: for most people a sharp morning peak, a post-lunch trough around two, and a smaller second wind near dusk. Stack your hardest work on the peak and guard those hours like a dragon on gold. Admin, email, and meetings can live in the valleys.

"The key is not to prioritize what's on your schedule, but to schedule your priorities." (Stephen Covey)

Mastering Async Communication

If your team is spread across the map (and most nomads' teams are), async communication stops being a nice-to-have. It decides whether remote work is sustainable or a slow grind of midnight pings while your colleagues sleep eight time zones away.

Batch your responses. Check Slack and email at set times. Mine are 9 AM, 1 PM, and 5 PM, and between those windows the notifications are off, not muted, off. The first few days it feels like negligence, like the building is on fire and you're ignoring the alarm. It isn't. That's just withdrawal.

Write longer, less often. Ten quick messages spawn ten quick replies and a thread nobody can follow. One thorough message laying out the context, your reasoning, and the actual decision or question saves everyone the back-and-forth. It's also a kindness to the teammate who opens it six hours later, half a planet away.

Record video updates. A 3-minute Loom kills a 30-minute meeting and nobody mourns it. Use them for project updates, design reviews, anything where tone and a shared screen carry meaning that text drops. The colleague in a different time zone gets the full picture without setting a 6 AM alarm for it.

Set time zone expectations. Your current time zone goes in your Slack status, your working hours go in your email signature, and both get updated the day you land somewhere new, not the week after. Vague availability is where resentment grows, on both ends of the wire.

Protecting Your Evenings

Here's the dirty secret of remote work: with no boundary, work swells to fill every waking hour you'll let it have. You'll check Slack over dinner. You'll "just finish one more thing" until the clock says midnight. You'll feel a low hum of guilt every time you leave the apartment, because you could be working. That isn't dedication. It's a slow-motion collapse with good wifi.

The Shutdown Ritual

Borrow this one straight from Newport: build a deliberate end to the workday. Mine takes ten minutes. I skim what I got done, write tomorrow's top three, close every work app one by one, and say it out loud, yes, genuinely out loud: "shutdown complete." It sounds ridiculous the first time. It works anyway, because your brain needs an unmistakable signal that the day is closed, and a spoken sentence is harder to ignore than a vague intention.

Explore Your City

You did not fly to Bangkok or Lisbon or Medellin to spend your evenings staring at the same ceiling you could have stared at back home. After shutdown, get out. Walk a street you've never walked. Eat somewhere with no English menu. Wander a night market. Sign up for the cooking class. The evenings are where this lifestyle pays you back. Treat them as overflow work time and you'll burn out anyway, except now without a single decent story to show for the airfare.

For each city I keep a running list of evening adventures, three to five screen-free things I want to do that week. Some weeks I hit all five. Plenty of weeks I manage two and call it a win. The point was never the score; the list keeps me honest about choosing the city over the screen.

Adapting When You Move Cities

Every move breaks your routine. Accept it now and save yourself the disappointment later. The question was never whether the routine survives the flight, because it won't. It's how fast you can rebuild it on the far side.

Keep the skeleton, change the details. The morning anchor ritual stays. The deep work blocks stay. The shutdown ritual stays. All that swaps out is the where: a new coworking space, a new cafe, a new running route past unfamiliar shopfronts. The structure rides along in your head, and specifics are cheap.

Give yourself a transition buffer. The first two days in a new city are "setup days" on a deliberately light workload: finding the workspaces, cracking the grocery situation, locating a gym, learning which direction is home. Demanding full output while you're still jet-lagged and lost is just signing up for frustration.

Have a minimum viable routine. On the genuinely broken days, travel days, visa runs, apartment moves, I drop to a stripped-back version: one hour of deep work, a reply to anything truly urgent, done. It's not a productive day by any honest measure. It keeps the thread of consistency from snapping, which is the whole job that day.

Track what's working. Every Sunday I run a quick review: what landed, what didn't, what I want to tweak. Fifteen minutes, a coffee, a notebook. It's the cheap insurance that stops a small leak from becoming three lost weeks.

Your Routine Is Your Superpower

The nomads still at it in five years aren't the ones with the fastest laptops or the cheapest Airbnbs. They're the ones running a routine that travels with them, a portable operating system for the day that gives structure without rigidity, focus without burnout, and a rhythm that never tips into monotony.

Start absurdly small. Pick one thing from this guide, the no-phone first hour, the shutdown ritual, the three-place rotation, and run it for two weeks without negotiating. Then add the next. Inside a month you'll have a routine that out-produces any office you've worked in, with the perk of running it from a balcony in Chiang Mai if that's where you want to be.

The freedom to work from anywhere is the easy half. The discipline to work well from anywhere is what turns it into a life instead of an expensive burnout.

Frequently Asked Questions About Building a Remote Work Routine

Why does a remote work routine matter more for digital nomads?

In an office, the commute, desk, and 5 PM exit structure your day for you. Working remotely strips those cues away, so a routine replaces dozens of daily micro-decisions and becomes a psychological anchor that stays constant even as the language, food, and streets around you keep changing.

How do you build a remote work routine that survives moving cities?

Keep the skeleton and change only the details. Your morning anchor ritual, deep work blocks, and shutdown ritual stay identical, while the specific cafe, coworking space, and running route become local. Give yourself a two-day setup buffer and the foundational mindset from our guide to staying productive while working abroad.

What should a remote work morning routine include?

Start with a location-independent anchor ritual, like making tea and writing your top priorities, then move your body for around 30 minutes. Protect a no-phone first hour so you stay proactive instead of reacting to someone else's inbox before your clearest, most creative time of day.

How do you structure deep work into a remote work routine?

Block time rather than listing tasks, scheduling two ninety to 120 minute deep work sessions in the morning with a short break between. Map your energy for a week, guard your peak hours for demanding work, and push admin, email, and meetings into the natural afternoon dip.

How do you handle communication while working remotely across time zones?

Lean on async habits. Batch Slack and email into set windows, write longer messages less often, and record short Loom videos instead of holding meetings. Put your current time zone in your status and working hours in your signature, updating both immediately whenever you move cities.

How does a remote work routine protect your evenings?

A deliberate shutdown ritual draws the line: review your day, write tomorrow's top three priorities, close every work app, and signal that work is done. Without that boundary, work expands to fill every hour, so guarding evenings is what lets you actually explore the city you moved to.

What is the best workspace setup for a remote work routine?

Rotate between three places matched to the task. Use a coworking space for deep work and video calls, like the ones in our best coworking spaces in Bali roundup, a cafe for lighter creative work, and your apartment for early or late focus sessions. Scout each option in an affordable hub like the one in our Bangkok budget guide.
