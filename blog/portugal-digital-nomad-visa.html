How to Get a Portugal Digital Nomad Visa (Step by Step)

$4,196 a month over the last three months, a criminal record certificate with an apostille, and two separate applications, one at a consulate abroad and one at AIMA in Portugal, stand between you and legal residence in Portugal. That is the Portugal D8 Digital Nomad Visa in a sentence, and for non-EU remote workers it is the residence route built for them rather than borrowed from another purpose. Salaried remote employee, freelancer, or owner of a company abroad: the D8 is built for all three.

The visa launched in late 2022. The idea is simple and the execution is where applications stall: a precise document list, an income floor tied to the Portuguese minimum wage, and a tax position that changed in 2024 and that many older guides still describe wrongly. This guide follows the official sources from the first eligibility check to the residence card, with every fee and rule dated. Euro amounts are converted at the European Central Bank reference rate of 25 September 2026, $1.1403 per euro.

Overview: Portugal's D8 Digital Nomad Visa

Portugal added the D8 in late 2022 for people who want to live in the country while working remotely, instead of stretching one 90-day visa-free stay into the next. It is for non-EU/EEA citizens who work for employers or clients based outside Portugal.

The consulate issues a residence visa valid for two entries and four months, and within those four months you apply to AIMA for the residence permit itself. According to AIMA's page for the remote-work permit, that permit is valid for two years from issue and renews for successive three-year periods.

Think of it as the D7's purpose-built sibling. The older D7 is the residence visa for people living on pensions, rents or investment income, and before 2022 it was the nearest fit for remote workers. The D8 removes that workaround. Earn your living remotely from abroad and this is your lane: remote employees of foreign companies, freelancers and independent contractors with foreign clients, and partners in companies registered outside Portugal all qualify.

After five years on temporary permits you can apply for permanent residence, for which AIMA asks, among other documents, for proof of basic Portuguese (A2) and of your standing with the tax and social security authorities.

Citizenship is further away than it was. Since Lei Orgânica 1/2026 took effect on 19 May 2026, naturalisation needs ten years of legal residence for nationals of countries outside the EU and the Portuguese-speaking world, where the old rule was five. The D8 also brings no tax break of its own, a point covered in the tax section below.

Eligibility Requirements

The bar to qualify is lower than most people fear; the paperwork backing it up is where the consulates get strict. Start with citizenship. You must be a non-EU/EEA/Swiss citizen, because an EU passport already gives you the right to live and work in Portugal.

You also need a criminal record certificate from your country of origin, or from the country where you have lived for more than a year, with a Hague apostille or legalisation. AIMA later refuses the permit to anyone convicted of a crime punishable in Portugal by more than one year in prison.

Then comes the number everyone fixates on. The foreign ministry's document list asks for average monthly income from your employment or self-employment over the last three months of at least four times the Portuguese minimum monthly wage. The 2026 figure is 3,680 euros a month, four times the minimum wage of 920 euros set by Decreto-Lei 139/2025, which comes to $4,196 a month at the ECB exchange rate of 25 September 2026 ($1.1403 per euro).

The floor rises every January with the minimum wage, so a figure from an older guide is out of date.

The same list names the proof: an employment contract or an employer's declaration for employees; a company contract, a service contract or documents showing services provided for the self-employed. Savings do not replace it, because the ministry counts only means that come from those contracts. You also need travel insurance covering medical costs, urgent care and repatriation, and a document attesting your current tax residence.

One line decides the category: the work must be for people or companies domiciled outside Portugal, and AIMA asks for a sworn declaration from that employer or client. A job with a Portuguese employer is a different visa.

Required Documents

The D8 paperwork is long, and a single missing item sends the file back. The list below is the foreign ministry's base list for residence visas plus the items specific to the remote-work visa. Pull all of it together before you book the consulate appointment, not after:

  • Visa application form: The residence visa form, completed and signed.
  • Two identical recent passport photographs in good condition for identification.
  • Passport: Valid for at least three months beyond the planned stay, with a photocopy of the biographical page. If you apply outside your country of nationality, add proof of your legal status there.
  • Proof of income: Average monthly income of at least $4,196 over the last three months, with the employment contract, employer's declaration, company contract, service contract or record of services that produced it.
  • Criminal record certificate: From your country of origin or the country where you have lived for more than a year, with a Hague apostille or legalisation.
  • Travel insurance: Covering medical costs, including urgent care and repatriation. The ministry notes the requirement can fall away under a bilateral health agreement, such as the UK's S1.
  • Proof of tax residence: A document attesting where you are currently tax resident.
  • Extra documents your consulate asks for: The ministry states the list is a base and each consulate may request more. A Portuguese tax number (NIF) and proof of accommodation, such as a lease, are the usual additions.
  • NIF (Número de Identificação Fiscal): Your Portuguese tax number. You can request it in person at a tax office (Serviço de Finanças) or Loja do Cidadão, or remotely through the tax authority's e-balcão channel via a legal representative holding your power of attorney. The tax authority's leaflet stresses that a legal representative is a different role from a fiscal representative, and that no fiscal representative is required when the NIF is assigned.

The apostille is the step with its own clock, because it is issued in the country that issued the certificate. Documents in a foreign language filed with the tax authority, such as a NIF request, must be certified translations according to its leaflet. Consulates set their own language rules, so read your consulate's page before paying for translations. Step-by-Step Application Process

The D8 runs in two phases. Phase one is the visa application at the Portuguese consulate responsible for your nationality or legal residence. Phase two is the residence permit, filed with AIMA once you are physically in Portugal. Grasp that split early, because the permit application in Portugal starts from the visa, and you cannot do the whole process from inside the country.

Phase 1: Consulate Application

The foreign ministry's list of where to apply names, for each country, the consulate and whether its applications go through VFS Global, the ministry's external service provider. In the United States, the consulates in New York and San Francisco both work with VFS, and so does the consulate in London.

You submit the documents, give biometric data and pay the visa fee. The ministry sets the decision period for a residence visa at 60 days. Approval comes as a visa in your passport valid for two entries and four months, and inside that window you must enter Portugal and start phase two.

Phase 2: Residence Permit (AIMA Appointment)

In Portugal the permit is issued by AIMA (Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo), the agency that replaced SEF in October 2023. The application is made by appointment, with an online channel for residence-visa holders still being introduced, and is handed in person at any Loja AIMA, not only the one nearest your address. You bring your passport, the valid D8 visa, a sworn declaration from your employer or client abroad confirming the work, and a sworn declaration of your address in Portugal.

The address declaration needs backing: a land registry certificate if you own the home, or a declaration from the landlord or host stating on what basis you live there. Once granted, the permit runs for two years. The legal periods, 60 days for the visa and four months to reach AIMA, are the floor for your timeline; appointment availability at the consulate and at AIMA comes on top, so book both as early as the systems allow.

Costs & Fees

The government fees are the only costs with an official price. The national visa fee is $125, as of September 2026. AIMA charges two fees for a temporary residence permit under its fee table (Portaria 307/2023, table updated 26 February 2026): $114 to receive and analyse the application and $98 to grant the permit.

Those two AIMA amounts already include the 25% reduction the table applies to residence permits until the service moves fully online, so AIMA's share is $212. The government total for the visa and the first permit is $337. Renewing the temporary permit costs the same two AIMA fees again.

Everything else depends on your country and your choices: the apostille fee is set by the country that issues it, translators and insurers price freely, and no law requires a lawyer for the D8. If you want one, our directory lists English-speaking lawyers in Lisbon with the source for each language claim. What the fees buy is a two-year permit in an EU country with a defined path to permanent residence, not a tax regime.

Tax Implications of the D8: Residency, IFICI and the End of NHR

The visa does not make you a tax resident. Article 16 of the IRS code does: you are resident if you spend more than 183 days in Portugal in any 12-month period starting or ending in the tax year, or if you have a home there on any day of that period in conditions suggesting you intend to keep it as your habitual residence. You then count as resident from the first day of your stay.

A D8 holder who lives in Portugal meets one of those tests within the first year, and a resident is taxed on worldwide income. Article 18 of the same code treats employment income from work performed in Portugal as Portuguese-source, whoever pays it. A salary from a foreign employer earned at a desk in Lisbon is Portuguese income. It falls under the general rates, which article 68 sets from 12.5% up to 48% on taxable income above $98,800, as the code stood in September 2026.

The Non-Habitual Resident regime is closed. The tax authority's NHR FAQ states it was revoked from 1 January 2024. A transitional rule covered only people who became resident in 2024 and held one of six documents dated 2023, such as a job contract, a lease, a visa or a started immigration process, and the deadline for the full ten years was 31 March 2025. The same FAQ says a request from someone who became resident in 2025 is refused, so no new D8 holder can use NHR.

Its replacement, IFICI (article 58.º-A of the Tax Benefits Statute), taxes employment and self-employment income at a special 20% for ten years, but only income earned in the listed activities: research and higher-education teaching, qualified jobs at companies with Portuguese investment contracts or in eligible sectors, certified startups, and similar roles certified by FCT, AICEP, IAPMEI, ANI or Startup Portugal. Remote work for a foreign employer or foreign clients is not on the list.

You also must not have been resident in Portugal in the previous five years, or ever used NHR. For those who do qualify, article 81 of the IRS code exempts foreign employment, business, investment, rental and capital-gains income, while still counting it to set the rate on the rest.

Registration is due by 15 January of the year after you become resident. Common Mistakes & Pro Tips

The first mistake is timing the income proof wrong. The consulate looks at the last three months, so statements older than that do not count toward the average, and a contract that started last month cannot show three months yet. The criminal record certificate and its apostille come from another country's authorities on their own schedule, so order them first. A third trap is the passport: it must stay valid for three months beyond the stay you are applying for.

The income test is an average, which helps freelancers with uneven months. What the consulate checks is the total over the last three months, which must reach at least $12,589 (three times $4,196). Each payment in the bank statements should match an invoice, a service contract or the employer's declaration, because the ministry's list asks for documents that show where the income comes from. If you have several clients, document each one separately so the consulate can match every payment to its source.

Five more points to plan around:

  • Get your NIF early. Bank accounts, leases and employment contracts need it, according to the tax authority, consulates often ask for it, and the remote route runs through a legal representative who needs your power of attorney. A power of attorney given to a lawyer (advogado) or solicitor (solicitador) needs no signature recognition, according to the tax authority's leaflet.
  • Know when a fiscal representative is required. Not when the NIF is assigned. Once you have a tax relationship in Portugal, such as an employment contract, self-employment, a car or property, a resident outside the EU and EEA has 15 days to appoint a fiscal representative or sign up for the tax authority's electronic notifications, according to its representation FAQ. For EU and EEA residents both are optional. A self-employed resident of a third country with VAT activity must appoint a VAT representative before starting, whatever notifications they use.
  • Update your tax address when you become resident. A NIF requested from abroad is registered as non-resident, and the tax authority's leaflet says you must request the change of address once you meet the residence conditions.
  • Book the AIMA appointment as soon as the system allows. Any Loja AIMA can take the application, whether you settle in Lisbon or Porto. Our digital nomad guide to Lisbon covers neighbourhoods, coworking and costs for the months you wait.
  • Apply from abroad. AIMA's remote-work permit starts from the D8 residence visa, which only the consulate responsible for your nationality or residence issues, so a visa-free stay in Portugal is the time to prepare documents, not to file.

Is It Worth It?

Yes, with conditions attached. The visa earns its keep if you plan to spend real time in Portugal (a year at minimum), if you clear the income floor with room to spare, and if you would rather hold legal residence than keep counting visa-free days. It fits people who want to settle, because the residence permit, the tax residence and the path to permanent residence all assume that Portugal becomes your base.

What you get goes past the right to stay. The permit lasts two years and renews for three, and after five years it can become permanent residence, with naturalisation possible after ten years of legal residence for most D8 holders under the 2026 nationality law. It also lets you travel to other Schengen countries for up to 90 days in any 180, according to the European Commission's immigration portal, while your time in Portugal itself no longer counts against a visa-free limit.

The drawbacks are real too. AIMA can cancel a temporary permit if you are away without good reason for six consecutive months, or eight months in total, during its validity (article 85 of Lei 23/2007), so the D8 does not suit a life spent mostly elsewhere. Portuguese tax on worldwide income comes with residence, and neither NHR nor, for most remote workers, IFICI softens it.

If all you want is a quick three-month stint in Lisbon, the D8 is overkill, and you might prefer somewhere from our roundup of the best European cities for nomads where short stays are simpler. If Portugal is your base for the coming years, this is the visa built for it.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Portugal Digital Nomad Visa

What is the Portugal D8 digital nomad visa?

The D8 is Portugal's residence visa for non-EU citizens who work remotely for employers or clients based outside Portugal. Launched in late 2022, it is a four-month visa that leads to a two-year AIMA residence permit, renewable for three-year periods, and after five years to permanent residence.

Who is eligible for the Portugal D8 visa?

Non-EU, non-EEA and non-Swiss citizens who work remotely for people or companies domiciled outside Portugal. You need average monthly income over the last three months of at least four times the Portuguese minimum wage, $4,196 in 2026, plus a criminal record certificate, travel medical insurance and proof of your current tax residence.

How long does the Portugal D8 visa take to process?

Portugal's foreign ministry sets a 60-day decision period for residence visas. The visa is then valid for two entries and four months, and within that window you apply to AIMA for the residence permit, which runs for two years. Appointment waits at the consulate and at AIMA come on top of these periods.

How do you apply for the Portugal D8 visa step by step?

Apply at the Portuguese consulate or its VFS centre for your country of nationality or residence, pay the $125 visa fee and wait for the decision. Enter Portugal within the four-month visa, then file the residence permit application at any Loja AIMA by appointment, with your employer's or client's sworn declaration and proof of address.

How are taxes handled on the Portugal D8 visa?

The visa does not set your tax status: more than 183 days in any 12 months, or a habitual home, does. Remote work done in Portugal is then Portuguese-source income taxed at 12.5% to 48%. NHR is closed to new residents, and IFICI's 20% needs work for a qualifying Portuguese entity. For home-country rules, see our digital nomad tax guide.

How much does the Portugal D8 visa cost?

Government fees are $125 for the consular visa and $212 for the AIMA residence permit, which charges two fees after a 25% reduction, so $337 in total at the September 2026 exchange rate. Apostilles, translations, insurance and any lawyer come on top, and their prices depend on your country and provider.

Is the Portugal D8 visa worth it for digital nomads?

It suits you if you plan to live in Portugal for years rather than months, clear the income floor, and accept Portuguese tax on your worldwide income once resident. Long absences can cost you the permit. Permanent residence is possible after five years, and citizenship now needs ten years of legal residence for most D8 holders.
