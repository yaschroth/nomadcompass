Las Palmas Digital Nomad Guide: Costs, Visas, Districts

Yannick Schroth August 2026 11 min read

Las Palmas sits at 28 degrees north, closer to the Sahara than to Madrid, and it holds 20 to 25°C for twelve months of the year. It is also in the European Union, on the same clock as London for half the year, and outside the EU VAT area. Those three facts, taken together, explain why a Spanish port city of 380,000 people has one of the densest digital nomad communities in Europe. This guide covers what a month there costs, where to base yourself, and the two rules that make the Canaries different from the mainland.

Our own city data rates Las Palmas 9 out of 10 for climate, 9 for community and 9 for air quality. It rates it 5 for cost. That combination is the honest shape of the place: this is not a cheap destination by global standards, and anyone arriving from Southeast Asia will feel the difference in week one. What you buy instead is stability, and a lot of other remote workers.

Why Las Palmas works for digital nomads

Most island destinations force a trade. You get the weather and lose the infrastructure, or you get the infrastructure and pay Zurich prices for it. Las Palmas mostly refuses that trade, because it is not a resort. It is a working city with a container port, a university, two hospitals and a bus network, which happens to have an urban beach running three kilometres through the middle of it.

The practical consequence is that ordinary life works. Fibre reaches residential buildings at 300 to 600 Mbps. Pharmacies, dentists and banks are the same ones the 380,000 residents use. The 161 providers we list in Las Palmas who work in a language other than Spanish, 119 of them in English, exist because the demand has been there for years.

That is the difference between a nomad hub and a nomad backdrop. Canggu and Tulum grew a nomad economy on top of a beach. Las Palmas already had a city.

Cost of living in Las Palmas: what a nomad month costs

Budget around $1,580 a month as a solo remote worker. That is the figure our cost of living index carries for Las Palmas, built from local rent and a one-person basket rather than from a survey of what nomads say they spend.

A furnished one-bedroom runs $820 to $1,200 depending on how close you want to be to Las Canteras beach. Groceries land between $220 and $330 if you shop at Mercadona and the municipal markets rather than the tourist-facing supermarkets. A restaurant meal is $11 to $16, a coffee $1.60 to $2.20, and a city bus under $1.60. Coworking day passes run $16 to $27, with monthly desks considerably cheaper per day.

The number that surprises people is rent, and specifically how fast it moves in autumn. Las Palmas fills up from October, when northern Europe gets dark and the island does not. Book a first month before you arrive and find the longer lease once you are on the ground.

Las Palmas is cheaper than Madrid or Barcelona and considerably more expensive than Lisbon was five years ago. Treat it as mid-range Europe with better weather, not as a budget destination.

Where to live in Las Palmas as a remote worker

The city runs north to south along a narrow isthmus, which makes the choice unusually simple. Almost everything a digital nomad wants sits in five districts, and you can walk between most of them.

Las Canteras is the beachfront strip and the default first landing. You pay a premium for the promenade and you get sunset swims after work. Guanarteme sits one street back, quieter and noticeably cheaper, and is where a lot of people move for their second month. Triana is the commercial old town, good for cafes and shops, further from the water.

Vegueta is the historic quarter, all cobbles and colonial facades, lovely to walk and slightly dead at night. Santa Catalina, around the port, is the most mixed and the least polished. It has the transport links and the lowest rents of the five.

If you have one month and no local knowledge, take Guanarteme. It is close enough to the beach to swim before a standup and far enough back that you are not paying for a view you will stop noticing by week two.

Internet and coworking spaces in Las Palmas

Residential fibre delivers 300 to 600 Mbps, which is ahead of most of the cities remote workers compare this one against. Coworking spaces run gigabit. Mobile 4G and 5G coverage along the coast and through the central districts is strong enough that tethering is a real fallback rather than a theoretical one.

The established spaces are FICO, White Forest and Sky Coworking, all of which run events as well as desks. Cafes in Triana, Guanarteme and La Isleta are reliable for a few hours of focused work, though the island convention is that you buy something every couple of hours rather than nursing one coffee until closing.

We score Las Palmas 7 for wifi rather than 9. The reason is variance, not speed: the top of the range is excellent and the bottom of the range is an older building with a copper line and a landlord who describes it as fibre. Ask for a speed test screenshot before signing anything.

The Las Palmas time zone advantage for remote work

The Canary Islands run on Western European Time, which is UTC+0 in winter and UTC+1 in summer. That puts Las Palmas on London's clock and one hour behind Madrid, Berlin and Paris, despite being Spanish territory.

For anyone working British or Irish hours this is close to ideal. You are in the same working day as your team, in a place that is 22°C in February. For continental European hours you gain an hour in the morning, which most people convert into a swim. For US East Coast work the gap is four to five hours, so afternoons and evenings become the overlap and mornings are yours.

It is a small structural detail that shapes the whole day. Check it against your own calendar with our time zone finder before booking.

Tax in the Canary Islands: what differs from mainland Spain

The Canaries are politically Spanish and fiscally distinct. The islands sit outside the European Union VAT area and levy their own consumption tax, IGIC, at a general rate of 7 percent against mainland Spain's 21 percent IVA. On day-to-day spending you notice it on electronics and services more than on groceries.

There is also the Zona Especial Canaria, a low corporate tax regime at 4 percent for companies that meet investment and local hiring conditions. It is designed for businesses that genuinely relocate, not for a freelancer with a laptop, and the conditions are real. Treat it as context for why the island has an unusual number of small companies rather than as a plan.

Separately, Spain's Beckham Law lets qualifying new arrivals be taxed as non-residents at a flat 24 percent on Spanish-source income for up to six years. Whether you qualify depends on how you are employed and where your income arises, and that is a question for an adviser rather than a blog. Nothing here is tax advice.

Visas: how non-EU digital nomads stay in Las Palmas

EU and EEA citizens need nothing. Register locally once you are staying, and that is the extent of it.

Everyone else has two routes. The short one is the 90 days in any 180 that Schengen gives most Western passport holders, which is enough for a winter but not for a base. The long one is Spain's digital nomad visa, introduced under the 2023 Startup Law, which requires work performed mainly for clients or employers outside Spain, health insurance, and remote income of at least twice the Spanish minimum wage. It grants a year initially and is renewable toward five.

That floor is the part to check rather than to memorise. It is pegged to the minimum wage, so it rises most years: it sat near $2,350 a month when the scheme opened and is closer to $3,100 at the 2026 rate. Our nomad visa finder lists what we hold for Spain and 60 other countries, and the consulate you apply through is the source that counts.

Weather in Las Palmas: the year-round spring and the calima

Highs sit between 21°C in February and 26°C in September. Lows rarely drop under 15°C. Rain is scarce and concentrated in a handful of winter days. This is the most reliable climate of any city we rate in Europe, which is why it takes a 9.

The exception has a name. A calima is a Saharan dust event, carried west on the wind, and it turns the sky orange, raises temperatures sharply and drops visibility. Most last a day or two. A severe one closes the airport. They cluster in winter and early spring, and they are the single thing about the climate that nobody mentions in the brochures.

Air quality otherwise is excellent, at 9 out of 10 in our data, because the wind here comes off several thousand kilometres of open Atlantic.

The digital nomad community in Las Palmas

This is the strongest argument for the city and the reason it keeps appearing on shortlists. Las Palmas has been a remote-work destination since before the term went mainstream, and the infrastructure that grew around that is social rather than physical.

Nomad City, the annual festival, has run here for years and pulls in several hundred people. The main local Facebook group carries over 4,000 members, and Slack and WhatsApp groups organise beach meetups, surf sessions and language exchanges most weeks. FICO, White Forest and Sky Coworking all host events rather than just renting desks.

The practical effect is that arriving alone is not lonely for long. We rate community 9 out of 10 here, level with the highest scores anywhere in our data, and higher than any other European city we cover.

What Las Palmas is not good at

Food scores 6. Canarian cooking is honest and simple, built on papas arrugadas, fresh fish and goat cheese, but the restaurant range is narrow next to Lisbon, Valencia or Barcelona. Anyone who chooses a city by its dinner options will find this one thin.

Culture scores 6 for a similar reason. There is a cathedral, a Columbus museum and a decent concert hall, and there is not a great deal more. The island's draw is outdoors, not indoors.

Nightlife at 7 is real but tourist-weighted around Santa Catalina and the port. And Spanish matters more than the expat bubble suggests: English is fine in coworking spaces and on the beach strip, and it thins out fast at the pharmacy counter or the town hall. We rate English 6, which is a fair reflection of a city where most service workers manage it and most administration does not.

Who Las Palmas suits, and who should skip it

Come here if you want winter sun without leaving the EU, if you work UK or European hours, and if you would rather arrive somewhere with an existing community than build one. It suits people staying months rather than weeks, because the value is in the second month, once the beachfront premium stops being worth paying.

Skip it if your budget is built around Southeast Asian prices, if you need a deep restaurant or museum scene, or if you want to be able to reach a dozen other countries by train. It is an island in the Atlantic. That is the whole proposition, and it is also the whole limitation.

Two nearby alternatives are worth a look before you commit. Tenerife is the larger island next door, with a mountain and a colder winter in the north, and Fuerteventura trades the city for wind and empty beaches. If you would rather stay on the mainland, our ranking of the best Spanish cities for remote workers covers the alternatives, and the Lisbon guide makes the obvious Atlantic comparison.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a digital nomad need per month in Las Palmas?

Budget about $1,580 a month solo. That covers a furnished one-bedroom at $820 to $1,200, groceries around $220 to $330, and a coworking desk. Sharing a flat in Guanarteme or Santa Catalina rather than on the beachfront brings the total down by a few hundred dollars.

Is the internet in Las Palmas fast enough for video calls?

Yes. Residential fibre runs 300 to 600 Mbps and coworking spaces offer gigabit connections. Mobile 5G along the coast is strong enough to use as a backup. The variable is the building: older stock sometimes has a copper line, so ask for a speed test before you sign a lease.

What time zone is Las Palmas in for remote work?

Western European Time, UTC+0 in winter and UTC+1 in summer. That is London's clock and one hour behind mainland Spain. It suits anyone working British or Irish hours, and gives a four to five hour offset to the US East Coast, leaving mornings free.

Do digital nomads need a visa for Las Palmas?

EU and EEA citizens do not. Other Western passport holders get 90 days in any 180 under Schengen rules. For longer, Spain's digital nomad visa needs remote income from outside Spain of at least twice the minimum wage, about $3,100 a month at the 2026 rate, plus health insurance. It runs one year, renewable toward five.

Is Las Palmas cheaper than mainland Spain?

For most things, yes. Rent and eating out sit below Madrid and Barcelona, and the islands charge IGIC at 7 percent instead of 21 percent mainland VAT, which shows up on electronics and services. Imported goods can run the other way, since everything arrives by sea or air.

What is the best neighborhood in Las Palmas for digital nomads?

Guanarteme, for a first longer stay. It is one street back from Las Canteras beach, noticeably cheaper than the promenade, and walkable to the coworking spaces. Las Canteras itself is the easier first month, and Santa Catalina has the lowest rents and the best transport links.

What is a calima and how often does it hit Las Palmas?

A calima is a Saharan dust cloud blown west over the Canaries. It turns the sky orange, pushes temperatures up sharply and cuts visibility, occasionally closing the airport. Most last a day or two and they cluster in winter and early spring. Air quality is otherwise excellent year-round.

Is English enough to live in Las Palmas as a remote worker?

For the nomad-facing city, yes. Coworking spaces, the beach strip and most central restaurants operate in English, and we list 119 providers here who work in it. Administration, pharmacies and smaller shops run in Spanish, so basic phrases make daily errands considerably easier.

When is the best time of year to work remotely from Las Palmas?

Any month works, which is the point. October through March is the busiest stretch, because northern Europe is dark and the island is 21°C, so rents rise and rooms go early. September and May are the quieter shoulders with much the same weather.
