Gijon Digital Nomad Guide: Costs, Cider and Green Spain

Yannick Schroth August 2026 11 min read

Gijón is the Spain that does not appear in the brochures: green, rainy, industrial, and built on a beach that curves for a mile and a half through the middle of town. It is on the Bay of Biscay rather than the Mediterranean, which is why the summers are 22°C instead of 35°C and why almost no digital nomad has heard of it. A month here runs about $1,580, and the thing that will actually shape your stay is not the price. It is that hardly anybody speaks English.

Our data rates Gijón 8 for nature, 8 for safety and 8 for air quality. It rates English 4 and community 4. This guide is about who that combination fits.

Why Gijón suits a certain kind of remote worker

Asturias sells itself as Paraíso Natural, and the marketing is closer to accurate than most. The region is a strip of green mountains falling into the Atlantic, with the Picos de Europa an hour inland and a coastline of cliffs and small beaches running either side of the city.

Gijón itself is a working port and a former steel town of about 270,000 people, which is why it feels like a Spanish city rather than a Spanish destination. Prices are set by people who live here. The sidrerías are full on a Tuesday. Nothing is arranged around visitors, and for some remote workers that is precisely the appeal.

It also has a beach in the middle of it. Playa de San Lorenzo runs the length of the seafront, and the promenade above it is where the city walks in the evening.

Gijón cost of living: what a month costs

Budget around $1,580 a month as a solo remote worker, with a comfortable month landing between $1,525 and $2,070. Our cost of living index carries the middle figure.

A one-bedroom in the centre costs $820 to $980, and outer neighbourhoods start nearer $655. A restaurant meal is $13 to $16. A bottle of Asturian cider is under $4.40, which matters more than it sounds, because cider here is the social unit rather than beer or wine.

Home fibre is the standout: 600 Mbps plans cost $25 to $33 a month. That is a Spanish market quirk worth knowing, and it makes working from an apartment genuinely viable rather than a compromise.

Best neighbourhoods in Gijón for digital nomads

The city is compact and walkable, so the choice is about character rather than commute.

Cimavilla is the old fishermen's quarter on the headland, the oldest and most atmospheric part, dense with cider houses and busiest at night. Centro is the commercial core between the two beaches, the practical default: shops, transport, and everything within ten minutes.

Playa de San Lorenzo means the streets behind the seafront, quieter and more residential, with the promenade at the end of the road. La Laboral sits east around the enormous Universidad Laboral complex, greener and further out. El Natahoyo is the western, working-class and cheapest district.

For a first month, take Centro or the streets behind San Lorenzo. Cimavilla is wonderful and loud.

Gijón internet speeds and coworking

We rate wifi 6 out of 10, and the number hides a split. Home fibre is fast and cheap, at 600 Mbps for the price of two restaurant meals. Cafe wifi is inconsistent and public wifi in the plazas is patchy, so the Spanish cafe-working habit is weaker here than in Barcelona or Valencia.

Bri Coworking is the established space, running 300 Mbps shared fibre. For video calls and focused work, a coworking desk or your own apartment connection is the reliable answer, and given how cheap home fibre is, a longer lease with your own line is usually the better plan.

How much Spanish you need for Gijón

We score English 4 out of 10, and that is the single most important number on this page. Gijón is not a tourist city, so the usual pattern where hospitality staff switch to English does not hold. Outside hotels, tourist offices and a few restaurants, the working language is Spanish.

Anything administrative, dealing with a landlord, opening a bank account, arranging utilities, registering locally, will require at least functional Spanish or someone to help. Younger people around the university are more likely to have some English.

Asturianu, the regional language, appears in place names, signage and speech, though it will not affect your daily life. If your Spanish is weak and you have no appetite to improve it, this is the wrong city and there are better options on the Mediterranean coast.

Gijón's nomad community, and its absence

We rate community 4, and the honest description is that there is no nomad scene here. No hub, no regular meetups, no festival. The university gives the city a young, educated population, and there are expats, but they are not numerous and they are not organised.

Meeting other remote workers means the coworking space or online groups, and it takes deliberate effort. Anyone who needs a ready-made network within a week should go to Las Palmas or Valencia instead, and our Spanish cities ranking lays out the alternatives.

What Gijón offers instead is integration. It is small enough, and un-touristed enough, that learning Spanish and staying a few months actually makes you a local rather than a visitor.

Gijón weather: green for a reason

Gijón has an oceanic climate and we score it 6, which is a fair mark for weather that is mild all year and grey a lot of it. Summers sit around 22°C, which by Spanish standards is remarkable: while Seville reaches 40°C, the Asturian coast stays comfortable. Winters are cool at 8 to 14°C and it rarely freezes.

The cost is rain, spread across roughly 130 days a year, and the local orbayu, a fine persistent drizzle that is closer to weather-as-atmosphere than to actual rain. That is why Asturias is green while the rest of Spain is brown.

Air quality scores 8, helped by constant Atlantic wind, though the port and remaining heavy industry to the west mean it is not pristine. June through September is the season, and it is when Spaniards from Madrid come north to escape the heat.

Cider, seafood and the Asturian table

Food scores 7, and the regional cooking is a genuine reason to come. Fabada asturiana, the bean and pork stew, is the dish the region is known for. Cachopo is two veal fillets around ham and cheese, breaded and fried, and portions are aggressive.

Then there is sidra. Asturian cider is still, sour and poured from a height to aerate it, a few centimetres at a time, and you drink it in one go before it flattens. The pouring is the point, and the sidrerías along Cimavilla and Calle Gascona in nearby Oviedo are where the city socialises. It is cheap, it is everywhere, and it is unlike cider anywhere else.

Getting around Gijón and the rest of Asturias

The city is walkable end to end in about forty minutes, and the bus network covers the rest for a euro or so a ride. Almost nobody who lives centrally needs a car, and the seafront promenade doubles as the main north to south route on foot.

Asturias is where the transport matters. Oviedo, the regional capital, is 25 minutes by train and worth the trip for Calle Gascona alone, a street of nothing but cider houses. The narrow-gauge coastal line runs west toward Cudillero and the fishing villages, slowly and scenically. The Picos de Europa, one of Spain's best mountain ranges, is about ninety minutes inland.

Asturias Airport is 40 kilometres west with mostly domestic and a few European routes, so long-haul usually means connecting through Madrid or Barcelona. Trains to Madrid take under four hours since the high-speed line opened, which is a change worth knowing if you last looked at Asturias years ago.

Visas: staying in Gijón as a non-EU remote worker

EU and EEA citizens can live and work here indefinitely. Everyone else has the standard Schengen 90 days in any 180, or Spain's digital nomad visa.

That visa, introduced under the 2023 Startup Law, requires remote income of at least twice the Spanish minimum wage, earned primarily from clients or employers outside Spain, plus health insurance and a clean record. The floor is pegged to the minimum wage and so rises most years: it is around $3,100 a month at the 2026 rate. It grants up to three years.

Check the current figure with the consulate you apply through rather than relying on any published number, including ours. Our nomad visa finder tracks what we hold across 60 countries.

Who Gijón suits, and who should skip it

Come here if you speak some Spanish or intend to, if you would rather live in a real city than a destination, and if a green coastline and 22°C summers appeal more than heat and crowds. It is safe, cheap for Western Europe, genuinely beautiful, and almost entirely undiscovered by remote workers.

Skip it if you need English to work, if you want a nomad community waiting for you, or if grey skies wear you down. Gijón asks more of a newcomer than most Spanish cities and gives more back to the ones who stay.

Full scores and the current cost breakdown are on the Gijón city profile. Bilbao is the larger city along the same coast, and Las Palmas is the Spanish opposite: sun, English and a large nomad scene. Our Las Palmas guide covers that trade, and the comparison tool puts any two side by side.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to live in Gijón as a digital nomad?

About $1,580 a month solo, with a comfortable month between $1,525 and $2,070. A central one-bedroom is $820 to $980, outer areas from $655, and a restaurant meal $13 to $16. Home fibre at 600 Mbps costs only $25 to $33 monthly.

Do you need Spanish to live in Gijón?

Effectively yes. We rate English 4 out of 10 here. Gijón is not a tourist city, so hospitality staff do not routinely switch to English. Landlords, banks, utilities and any administration will need functional Spanish or someone to help you through it.

Is Gijón's internet good enough for remote work?

At home, yes: 600 Mbps fibre costs $25 to $33 a month. Cafe and plaza wifi is inconsistent, so the cafe-working habit is weaker here than in bigger Spanish cities. Bri Coworking runs 300 Mbps shared fibre and is the reliable option outside your flat.

What is the weather like in Gijón?

Mild and often grey. Summers hold around 22°C, cool for Spain, and winters sit at 8 to 14°C without freezing. Rain falls on roughly 130 days a year, including the local orbayu drizzle, which is why Asturias is green while much of Spain is dry.

Is Gijón safe for foreign remote workers?

Yes, we rate safety 8 out of 10. It is a mid-sized working city with low violent crime and streets that are comfortable at night, including the old Cimavilla quarter. Petty theft around the seafront in summer is the realistic concern rather than anything more serious.

Is there a digital nomad community in Gijón?

Barely. We score community 4: no hub, no regular meetups, no festival. Expats exist but are few and unorganised, and connections come through the coworking space or online groups. Las Palmas or Valencia are the better choices if an existing network matters to you.

Can non-EU citizens stay in Gijón long term?

Through Spain's digital nomad visa, which needs remote income of at least twice the Spanish minimum wage from non-Spanish sources, roughly $3,100 a month at the 2026 rate, plus insurance. It grants up to three years. Otherwise the Schengen limit of 90 days in any 180 applies.

What is Asturian cider and why does it matter in Asturias?

Sidra is a still, sour cider poured from arm's height to aerate it, a few centimetres at a time, drunk immediately before it flattens. It is the social unit here rather than beer or wine, costs under $4.40 a bottle, and the sidrerías are where the city actually meets.
