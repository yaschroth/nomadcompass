Gijón Digital Nomad Guide: Cider, Rain and Real Costs

Yannick Schroth August 2026 10 min read

In Spanish, escanciar simply means to pour or serve a drink, especially wine or cider. In an Asturian cider house it means something far more specific. The waiter raises the bottle above his head, holds the glass low at his waist and lets a narrow stream of cider fall the length of his body to smash against the inside of the rim. He stops when the glass is about a quarter full and hands it over, and you drink it in one go.

The height is the point. Asturian natural cider is still and tart, and the fall wakes the little gas it has. That small measure, the culín, loses its life within seconds, which is why nobody sips.

The ritual is now officially heritage. In December 2024 UNESCO added Asturian cider culture to its list of the intangible heritage of humanity, naming the orchards, the cider houses and the chigres, the region's cider bars, where it is passed on by imitation. And in August 2026, Gijón set a world record with 10,385 people pouring at once on Poniente beach.

The pour belongs to a particular coast. The apples grow on a wet, green strip between the mountains and the Bay of Biscay, under a grey sky for much of the year, with summer highs in the low twenties. Gijón is its port city, home to about 270,000 people, the largest in Asturias, with a 1.5-kilometre beach curving through the middle of town.

So here is the verdict. Gijón suits you if you speak some Spanish or mean to learn, want a real Spanish city rather than a resort, and want a cool summer by the sea, at about $1,580 a month on our one-person budget. It is the wrong choice if you need English for daily life, want a ready-made nomad community or need sun to function.

The green coast, and what its weather means

Gijón sits on the Bay of Biscay, and the Atlantic sets its moods. Rain falls on about 125 days a year and the annual total is close to a metre, much of it as orbayu, the Asturian word for the fine, persistent drizzle that hangs in the air rather than falls. It is the reason the region is green while much of Spain is brown, and the reason the cider exists.

It also keeps the temperature in a narrow band. August averages a high of about 23°C and January about 14°C. For a remote worker that makes summer the obvious season, warm enough for the beach and cool enough to sleep. The flip side is that a winter here is mild but grey, so if short wet days drag you down, come between June and September.

Late August is the liveliest time, when the Fiesta de la Sidra Natural ends with the mass pour on Poniente beach. Book a flat early if you want that month.

Where should you live?

The city is compact, and on our figures a central one-bedroom rents for about $920 a month against about $740 further out. With that small a gap, choose by the street you want to walk out onto.

Cimavilla: the old fishermen's quarter

Cimavilla is the original settlement, packed onto the headland between the city's two beaches. Its narrow lanes climb to the grassy Cerro de Santa Catalina, where there stands Eduardo Chillida's Elogio del Horizonte: ten metres of concrete facing the sea, which amplifies the wind and the waves when you stand inside its curve. It is the most atmospheric address in town, and the hilliest.

San Lorenzo and the Muro: the beach street

Playa de San Lorenzo runs for about 1,550 metres in a shell-shaped curve, and above it the Paseo del Muro is where the whole city walks in the evening. Stairways drop from the promenade to the sand; the grandest, La Escalerona, dates from 1933. The streets one or two blocks back are residential, with the sea at the end of the cross streets.

Centro and La Arena: the practical choice

Centro and La Arena make up the rest of the central districts, between the beaches and a short walk from the old town. For a first month with no local knowledge, this is the safe default.

Pumarín, La Calzada and the Laboral

Our city page puts the cheaper one-bedrooms in Pumarín and La Calzada, about ten minutes out. To the east, the Universidad Laboral, finished in 1955 and described as the largest building in Spain, now houses the LABoral art centre.

How much Spanish do you need?

More than in most Spanish cities a remote worker is likely to consider. The landlord, the bank, the utilities and the town hall all work in Spanish. Even professionals who work in English are scarce: our Gijón directory has found a single lawyer in the city who lists English among the languages they work in.

That cuts both ways. With intermediate Spanish, a few months here can feel like living in Spain rather than visiting it. With none, the paperwork will wear you down, and Valencia or the Canaries will be easier. For legal work in English, our guide to choosing between an abogado, a gestor and a notary in Spain explains who does what, wherever in the country you end up.

What working from Gijón is like

Home broadband is cheap in Spain, and our city page puts it at about $26 to $35 a month, so a flat with its own fibre line is the simplest office. Check that the line actually runs into the flat, not just past the building, before you sign.

For a desk among other people, Bri Coworking on Calle de la Muralla, a block from San Lorenzo, charges about $196 a month for a full-time desk, $150 for mornings only and about $8 for a single morning or afternoon. workINgijón, in the centre, offers round-the-clock access for freelancers who keep odd hours.

A day could look like this. Work through the morning, then walk the Muro at lunchtime. When the calls are done, climb Cimavilla to Chillida's sculpture for the view over the sea, then drop into a sidrería where the waiter pours from above his head and a bottle costs $3.50 to $4.50 on our figures. A city bus ride is about $1.70 if your feet give up.

Can you stay longer than 90 days?

With an EU or EEA passport, yes, indefinitely. On an American, British, Canadian or Australian one you get 90 days in any 180 across the Schengen area, which covers a summer but not a year.

For longer, Spain's digital nomad visa needs an income of twice Spain's minimum wage, which is about $3,280 a month when the 2026 wage is spread over twelve months. Some consulates count the wage over its fourteen yearly payments and state about $2,810 a month instead, so check the figure with the consulate you apply to. Each family member adds to the floor.

The other conditions decide whether you fit. You need a university degree or three years of professional experience, health insurance with no co-payments, and, if you are self-employed, no more than 20 percent of your work for Spanish clients. The visa runs for up to a year; apply from inside Spain instead and the residence permit runs for up to three. Our comparison of digital nomad visas sets Spain's floor against the other countries that offer one.

Weekends: Oviedo, the coast and the Picos

Asturias is small enough to cover by train. Oviedo, the regional capital, is about 25 minutes away on the C-1 commuter line. The C-4 line runs the other way along the coast to the fishing village of Cudillero, in a little over an hour and a half.

Inland, the limestone peaks of the Picos de Europa are a day trip by car. Spain's first national park was created here in 1918 around the glacial lakes of Covadonga.

Further afield, the high-speed line through the Pajares tunnels opened in November 2023, and the fastest train now reaches Madrid in 3 hours 39 minutes, down from about five.

Gijón, Bilbao or the Canaries?

Along the same coast, Bilbao is the bigger and costlier city, at about $2,030 a month on our budget. At the other end of Spain, our Las Palmas guide covers the opposite trade: year-round sun and a large nomad community, for about the same monthly budget. Our ranking of the best Spanish cities for remote workers sets out the rest.

If you do choose Gijón: come between June and September for your first stay, take a furnished flat in Centro or behind San Lorenzo, sign up for a few weeks at a coworking desk to meet people, and start a Spanish course on day one. Then learn to hold the glass low.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to live in Gijón as a digital nomad?

About $1,580 a month for one person with a central one-bedroom. A central one-bedroom rents for about $920 and one further out for about $740.

What does escanciar mean?

It means to pour or serve a drink. In Asturias it is the way cider is served: the bottle held above the head, the glass held low, so the stream hits the rim and wakes the cider. You drink the small measure in one go.

Do you need Spanish to live in Gijón?

In practice, yes. Landlords, banks and public offices work in Spanish, and professionals who work in English are rare. With some Spanish, the city is easy to live in.

What is the weather like in Gijón?

Mild and often grey. August highs average about 23°C and January highs about 14°C, with rain on about 125 days a year, much of it the fine drizzle Asturians call orbayu.

Can non-EU citizens stay in Gijón for more than 90 days?

Yes, with Spain's digital nomad visa. It needs an income of twice the minimum wage, about $3,280 a month over twelve months, a degree or three years' experience and health insurance.

Is there coworking in Gijón?

Yes. Bri Coworking, a block from San Lorenzo beach, charges about $196 a month for a full-time desk, and workINgijón in the centre offers round-the-clock access. Home fibre is cheap enough to work from a flat.

How long is the train from Gijón to Madrid?

The fastest train takes 3 hours 39 minutes, since the Pajares high-speed line opened in late 2023. Oviedo is a short commuter-train ride away.
