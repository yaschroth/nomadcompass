Digital Nomad Tax Guide 2026: Why 183 Days Is Not a Shield

Yannick Schroth January 2025 13 min read

Contents

  2. Taxed by passport
  3. Kept by your ties
  4. Countries that count days
  5. Taxing the work done there
  6. Low-tax countries
  7. What actually works
  8. FAQ

Every remote worker hears the rule sooner or later, usually from someone who has been travelling longer: never spend 183 days in one country, and no country can tax you. Keep moving and the bill never arrives. It is the most repeated idea in nomad tax, and for almost everyone who relies on it, it is wrong.

Say you spend 2026 like this: a hundred days in Lisbon, eighty in Mexico City, seventy in Bangkok, sixty in Tbilisi, and the rest back home for the holidays and a wedding. You never reach 183 days anywhere. The myth says you owe nothing. In practice, the country you left may still treat you as its resident, the countries where you sat and worked may tax the work, and the treaty that made "183 days" famous protects only people who are tax resident somewhere.

So the honest answer is the reverse of the myth. You cannot avoid having a tax home by moving fast; you can only choose one, on purpose, and then prove it. How hard that is depends on which kind of country you are dealing with, and there are five kinds.

Five kinds of country, five ways the myth fails

Where the 183 days actually come from

The number is real; it just does not mean what the myth says. The OECD's model tax treaty, the template its member countries are asked to follow, says in its article on employment income that a salary earned in another country is taxed only at home if three conditions hold together: you are there for no more than 183 days in any twelve months, your employer is not resident there, and no branch of your employer there bears the cost.

Read the first words of that article and the problem appears. It applies to "a resident of a Contracting State": someone who is already taxed as a resident by one of the two treaty countries. The 183 days are a shield that your home country's treaty lends you while you work abroad. A nomad who has deliberately stopped being resident anywhere has no home treaty to borrow it from.

Treaties also settle what happens when two countries both claim you, and the order of the tests says a lot about how tax authorities think. First comes where you have a permanent home available; then where your personal and economic ties are closer, the "centre of vital interests"; then where you habitually stay; and only then your nationality. Days come third. A flat, a partner and a bank account count for more.

Countries that tax you by passport

For Americans the myth fails on the first line. The United States taxes its citizens and green-card holders on worldwide income from all sources, wherever they live. Days abroad do not end that; they only unlock relief.

The main relief is the foreign earned income exclusion. It covers up to $132,900 of foreign earnings for tax year 2026. The test most nomads use is physical presence: 330 full days outside the United States in any 12 consecutive months. A full day runs from midnight to midnight, so travel days rarely count, and the IRS makes no exception for illness, family or a vacation: five weeks at home for Christmas can sink the year.

The exclusion also needs your tax home to be in a foreign country, which is one more reason to have one. And it leaves two things alone. It does not remove the duty to file a return, and it does not touch self-employment tax: the IRS's own worked example is a consultant abroad with $68,000 of net profit who must pay self-employment tax on all of it while qualifying for the exclusion.

Add the foreign bank account report, due once your accounts abroad together pass $10,000 at any point in the year, and an American nomad's paperwork grows rather than shrinks with every border.

Countries that keep you through your ties

Most other home countries tax by residence, not passport, which sounds better until you read how residence is defined. Britain's statutory residence test is the clearest example. If you were UK resident in any of the last three tax years, you are automatically non-resident only if you spend fewer than 16 days in the UK, or work full-time abroad, averaging at least 35 hours a week, and spend fewer than 91 days in the UK.

Miss those tests and your ties decide. HMRC counts five: family, accommodation, work, 90 days in the UK in either of the previous two years, and whether the UK is the country you spent most time in.

For a recent leaver, 16 to 45 days in the UK with four ties is enough to stay resident, and the more days you spend, the fewer ties it takes: over 120 days, one will do. A nomad who spends three months of the year in a flat they still rent, near a partner, has not left at all.

Germany is blunter still. You are fully taxable there if you have a Wohnsitz, a dwelling held in circumstances suggesting you will keep and use it, with no minimum number of days; a stay of more than six months creates a habitual abode on its own. Keeping your old flat, or a room you return to, can keep you resident while you spend the year abroad.

The lesson for anyone leaving a residence-based country is the same: leaving is an act, not an absence. Give up the home, move the ties you can, and find out what your country needs to see before it lets you go.

Countries that count your days, each in its own way

The host countries on your route have their own tests, and they do not count alike. Portugal makes you resident after more than 183 days in any 12-month period, or as soon as you have a home that looks like your habitual residence, and residence then runs from your first day. A year-long lease on a flat in Lisbon can do it with no day count at all.

Norway counts every whole or part calendar day, and adds a second line: more than 270 days in 36 months makes you resident too, which catches people who come back every summer. Thailand uses the calendar year, 180 days or more; the United States counts only full days. A rolling twelve-month window, like Portugal's, can also catch a stay that two calendar years would have split.

So the practical rule is to count per country, by that country's method, and to keep the evidence: boarding passes, leases, entry stamps or their digital records. If anyone ever asks where you were on a given night, a spreadsheet you kept at the time is worth more than a memory.

Countries that tax the work you do there

Residence decides who taxes your worldwide income. It does not stop a country taxing what is earned on its soil. Thailand taxes residents and non-residents alike on income from employment or business carried on in Thailand, wherever it is paid, and Portugal's code treats pay for work performed in Portugal as Portuguese income. Seventy days at a desk in Bangkok is work carried on in Thailand, even if the salary lands in London.

This is where the treaty shield earns its keep, for those who have one. A salaried nomad who is tax resident at home, works for a foreign employer with no branch in the host country and stays under 183 days is usually covered. For the self-employed, treaties tax business profits in the host country only through a permanent establishment, a fixed place of business, so months at one address are what to watch. Without a home residence, neither protection is yours to claim.

Thailand adds a second twist for people who do become resident there: foreign income earned from 1 January 2024 onwards is taxable when it is brought into Thailand, in the year it arrives or any later year. Moving savings into a Thai account can be a taxable event.

Countries that tax little or nothing

Then there are the places the myth is really about. The United Arab Emirates has no personal income tax. A 9 percent corporate tax applies to business profit above about $102,110 a year, and an individual's own business falls within it once its turnover passes about $272,300 in a calendar year. Our Dubai guide for digital nomads covers the remote-work visa that gives you residence there.

Georgia exempts its residents from tax on income without a Georgian source, and an individual entrepreneur with small business status pays 1 percent of turnover up to about $190,500 a year, and 3 percent above it.

There is a new condition. Since 1 March 2026, a foreigner also needs a right to work, Georgia's permit for the self-employed, before trading: about $76 to apply, and a fine of about $762 for working without it. Our guide to a year in Tbilisi walks through the steps.

Malaysia exempts most foreign-sourced income that resident individuals bring into the country until 31 December 2036, subject to conditions, and Paraguay's personal income tax reaches only income from Paraguayan sources. Portugal no longer belongs on this list: its Non-Habitual Resident regime was revoked from 1 January 2024, and its successor covers listed research, teaching and startup jobs at Portuguese entities, not remote work for a foreign employer.

None of these places can help until you are actually resident there, and actually gone from home. The UAE, for instance, treats you as resident after 183 days in a 12-month period, or after 90 days if you also hold a residence permit and meet further conditions. A zero-tax country is a tax home only if you live in it, and your old country will want to see proof, usually a certificate of tax residence, before it lets go.

What actually works

Replace the myth with a plan. Pick one country as your tax home, by where you really live and can prove it, and find out what your home country requires for you to leave: days, ties, a deregistration, a final return. Then count days in every country you work from, by that country's own method, and keep the records.

Next, check the source rules wherever you stay longer than a few weeks, especially as a freelancer, and whether a treaty between your tax home and that country covers you. Ask your tax home for a residence certificate once you qualify, because it is what a host country or your old one will ask for. And keep part of every payment aside until you know which country will tax it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I avoid tax if I stay under 183 days in every country?

Usually not. Your home country may still treat you as resident through your ties or your passport, countries where you work can tax income from work done there, and the treaty 183-day rule protects only people who are tax resident in one of the two treaty countries.

Do US citizens have to pay tax while living abroad as digital nomads?

They must file, because the US taxes citizens on worldwide income. The foreign earned income exclusion covers up to $132,900 of foreign earnings for 2026 if you spend 330 full days abroad in 12 months, but self-employment tax still applies.

How do I stop being UK tax resident as a digital nomad?

If you were resident in any of the last three tax years, you are automatically non-resident with fewer than 16 days in the UK, or with full-time work abroad and fewer than 91 days in the UK. Otherwise your UK ties decide: four ties keep you resident from just 16 days.

Can I be tax resident nowhere?

Rarely, and it helps less than it sounds. Home countries often keep you through ties, host countries can tax work done on their soil, and without a tax residence you cannot claim treaty protection or get a residence certificate.

Which countries tax digital nomads the least?

The UAE has no personal income tax, Georgia exempts residents' foreign-source income and taxes small businesses at 1 percent of turnover, and Malaysia exempts most foreign-sourced income until 2036. Each helps only once you are genuinely resident there.

Does a digital nomad visa change where I pay tax?

Not by itself. A visa decides whether you may stay; tax residence follows that country's own test. In Portugal, for example, more than 183 days or a habitual home makes a D8 holder resident, taxed at ordinary rates.

Is Portugal's NHR regime still open to new residents?

No. It was revoked from 1 January 2024, and its successor, IFICI, covers listed research, teaching and startup jobs at Portuguese entities, not remote work for a foreign employer.
