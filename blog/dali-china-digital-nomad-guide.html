Dali Digital Nomad Guide: China at $800 a Month

Yannick Schroth August 2026 13 min read

The first morning in Dali usually starts well. You wake in a courtyard guesthouse inside the old town walls, the Cangshan range rising straight behind the roofs, and carry a coffee to a table in the sun. The guesthouse wifi connects at once. Then you open Gmail, and nothing loads. Google Drive spins. WhatsApp shows a clock beside every message. The Slack call you set for nine is a grey window.

Nothing is wrong with the line. China's fixed broadband is fast, with a national median download of about 238 Mbps in Ookla's index, more than twice Germany's. What stops you is the filter on top of it, the Great Firewall, and in Dali that filter sits between you and almost every tool a Western remote job runs on. It is the fact that decides whether this town works for you, so it comes first here.

The verdict, then. Dali is one of the cheapest and most beautiful places in China to live for a month: a walled Bai town on a plain between a 4,000-metre mountain range and a lake 40 kilometres long, for about $800 a month. It suits you if your work can run through a tested workaround and your passport gets you in without a visa. It does not suit anyone whose income hangs on Google Workspace by the hour, or anyone hoping to stay for a season.

Getting online behind the Firewall

Start with what you will lose. The UK Foreign Office lists Google, Facebook, YouTube and X as permanently blocked in China, and WhatsApp and Instagram are blocked as well. Gmail, Drive, Meet and Google Maps go with Google. If your team lives in Slack threads and Google Docs, your day in Dali is a series of detours.

The usual detour is a VPN, and the order of operations matters. The same Foreign Office page notes that China's cybersecurity rules require products such as VPNs to be licensed by the government, so the apps people rely on are not ones you can comfortably find and download after landing. Install and test yours at home, on every device, before the flight.

The second detour is your phone plan. A foreign SIM or a roaming travel eSIM routes its data back through the issuing network, outside China, which is why Google and WhatsApp work on it without a VPN. That only covers mobile data, not the café or guesthouse wifi, and roaming data is metered. In practice many people use roaming data for the calls that matter and a local connection for everything else.

Then there is everything the Firewall does not block, because it is Chinese. WeChat and Alipay run daily life here: payments, taxis, food orders, messages with your landlord. Both now take foreign Visa and Mastercard cards, and payments worth less than about $30 carry no fee; above that, the fee is 3 percent. Set both up before you leave, while you can still receive the verification text at home.

Can you get in, and for how long?

Dali has no international border post, so the rules are China's. Citizens of 50 countries, including most of the EU, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, can stay up to 30 days without a visa for tourism, business or visits. Britain and Canada joined the list on 17 February 2026, and the waiver for both runs until 31 December 2026, the same end date as the rest of the scheme.

Americans are not on that list. The choice is a tourist L visa, applied for in advance, or the 240-hour visa-free transit, which needs an onward ticket to a third country. Kunming Changshui airport is one of its entry points, and the permitted area in Yunnan includes Dali and Lijiang. Fly in from the US and out to Bangkok, and you can spend most of ten days by the lake.

Two rules follow you once you are in. You must register your address with the local police within 24 hours of arrival; a hotel does it at check-in, and in a private rental it is up to you and the local police station. And neither entry route is a work permit. The immigration administration states that work requires prior approval and the right visa, and there is no visa for remote workers. Dali is a 30-day city for most passports, and a ten-day one for American ones.

Where to stay: the walls, the lake or the mountain

Dali is several places sharing a name, and the one you sleep in shapes every day. Dali Old Town sits about 10 kilometres north of Xiaguan, the modern prefecture seat that most trains, buses and official signs mean by "Dali". The two towns feel nothing alike, so check which one a listing is in.

The old town: walls, courtyards and cafés

The present old town was laid out in the late 14th century, after the Mongol conquest razed the earlier capital, and it keeps that grid: stone lanes, white-walled Bai houses, teahouses and craft shops inside the gates. It has the best concentration of laptop-friendly cafés and courtyard guesthouses, and the busiest lanes fill with tourists, especially on Chinese holidays. A private room or small studio runs about $200 to $450 a month. For a first month, live here.

The Erhai villages: water and bicycles

Along the lake, a string of Bai villages runs from Caicun, close to town, to Shuanglang on the far shore. Life here is guesthouses with lake views, cycling paths and boats, with fewer services and a longer ride to anything practical. Shuanglang draws its own crowds. Move out to the shore once you know how the buses and bikes work.

The Cangshan foothills: cooler and quieter

West of the walls the land climbs towards the mountain, with scattered guesthouses, retreats and trailheads. The air is cooler and the views over the lake are wide; the price is a steep walk for every errand. It suits someone who came for the mountain and works mostly offline.

Xiaguan: the working town

Xiaguan has the high-speed station, the long-distance buses, the big shops and the prefecture's hospitals, at cheaper local rents and with none of the old town's character. Few remote workers live there by choice, but it is where you go for paperwork, repairs and a doctor.

A working day between mountain and lake

Picture a weekday in October. You could start in a guesthouse courtyard, catching European emails before the town wakes: Dali runs on Beijing time, eight hours ahead of London in winter and seven in summer. Dedicated coworking is limited, so cafés and courtyards are the default desks, with your roaming data or a local SIM as the backup line for calls.

Lunch is the cheapest part of the day. A Bai or Yunnan meal at a local eatery costs about $2 to $5, and a coffee in a laptop-friendly café $3 to $5.

At five, you have a choice of two landscapes. Erhai, the lake below the town, covers about 250 square kilometres at 1,972 metres above sea level, and an e-bike along its western shore takes you through the villages to the water at sunset. Or take the chairlift up Cangshan, whose highest summit, Malong, reaches 4,122 metres, to the Jade-Cloud Road, a paved walkway along the mountainside that passes waterfalls and looks down over the lake and the old town.

On the way, the Three Pagodas of Chongsheng Temple stand about 1.5 kilometres north of the walls. The tallest, Qianxun, was built in the ninth century by a king of Nanzhao and rises 69.6 metres. This was the capital of two kingdoms, Nanzhao and then the Dali Kingdom, until the Mongols took it in 1253, and the Bai people whose capital it was still number about two million.

Language is the other daily texture. Mandarin runs everything outside guesthouses and the most tourist-facing cafés, and signage is almost entirely in Chinese. A translation app, WeChat's built-in translation and a few dozen phrases carry you through a month, but you will be beside the town's social life rather than in it unless you speak some Chinese.

What $800 a month buys

Our budget for Dali is about $800 a month for one person, within a range of $800 to $1,300. It is our own estimate rather than a measurement: no cost survey covers the town, which is why it is missing from our cost of living index of measured cities.

Rent is the line that moves. A guesthouse room or small studio in or near the old town sits at the low end; a lake-view flat in a popular village costs several times as much. Everything else is cheap: local food, buses and DiDi rides, a SIM at $10 to $20 a month. Cook at home and rent away from the busiest lanes and you can come in under $800; rent on the lake and eat in cafés and you will be nearer $1,300.

For comparison, our budget for Kunming, the provincial capital, is about $1,000, and Chiang Mai, the obvious alternative without a Firewall, about $840. Dali is not cheaper than northern Thailand, then. What the money buys here is different: a mountain, a lake and a medieval capital, in a part of China few foreigners settle in.

When to come

At this altitude and latitude the climate is mild most of the year. Spring and autumn are the best of it: in April, May and October the days are clear and comfortable, with May the warmest month at around 26°C by day. Locals sum up the scenery as "wind, flowers, snow and moon": the wind of Xiaguan, the flowers of Shangguan, the snow on Cangshan and the moon over Erhai.

Summer is the rainy season. Most of the year's rain falls between June and October, and August is the wettest month, with close to 300 millimetres; the lake and hills turn green and the domestic tourists arrive. Winter is dry and sunny by day and cold at night, with January lows around 4°C, so pack warm layers. Whatever the month, avoid Chinese public holidays if you dislike crowds in the old town.

Getting there, and the rest of Yunnan

Most people arrive by train from Kunming, about 250 kilometres to the south-east. A double-track line opened in 2018 raised speeds on the route from 60 to 200 km/h, and Dali's small airport has flights to Kunming. Inside town you walk; to the lake you cycle or ride an e-bike; for everything further, buses and DiDi, the ride-hailing app.

Dali is also the start of a route north. The railway to Lijiang takes about an hour and a half, against four hours by bus, and since 26 November 2023 a line has continued from Lijiang to Shangri-La on the edge of the Tibetan plateau. On a 30-day stay, that turns a month in one town into a base with weekends in two others.

Who Dali suits

Come if you are curious about China rather than nervous of it, if your work survives behind a tested VPN or on roaming data, and if a mountain, a lake and a 600-year-old walled town matter more to you than a meetup calendar. Come with a British, Canadian or Australian passport, or most European ones, and a 30-day plan, or an American one and either an L visa or a ticket onward from Kunming.

Do not come if your clients expect you live on Google Meet all day, if you need a long-stay visa, or if you want an English-speaking nomad community around you. Those are not inconveniences that a better café fixes; they are how China works. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to live in Dali, China?

About $800 a month for one person, within a range of $800 to $1,300. That is our own estimate, since no cost survey covers Dali. Rent moves the total most: a room or small studio near the old town runs about $200 to $450 a month.

Can digital nomads use Google and WhatsApp in Dali?

Not on a local connection. Google, YouTube, Facebook, X, WhatsApp and Instagram are blocked in China. A VPN installed and tested before arrival, or a roaming eSIM whose data leaves China through a foreign network, are the usual workarounds.

Do I need a visa to visit Dali?

Citizens of 50 countries, including the UK and Canada since 17 February 2026, can stay 30 days visa-free until 31 December 2026. US citizens need an L visa, or can use the 240-hour transit through Kunming with an onward ticket to a third country.

Does China have a digital nomad visa?

No. Visa-free entry and transit cover tourism, business visits and similar stays, and China's immigration administration states that work requires prior approval and the appropriate visa.

Where should a remote worker stay in Dali?

In the old town for a first month, for the cafés and courtyard guesthouses. The Erhai lakeshore villages are quieter and greener, the Cangshan foothills suit hikers, and Xiaguan, 10 kilometres south, is the practical modern town with the station and hospitals.

What is the best time of year to work from Dali?

April, May and October. Rain falls mostly from June to October, with August the wettest month, and winter nights drop to around 4°C.

Is Dali easy to get to?

Yes, through Kunming, about 250 kilometres south-east, by high-speed train or a short flight. From Dali the railway continues to Lijiang in about an hour and a half, and on to Shangri-La.
