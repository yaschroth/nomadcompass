English-Speaking Doctor in Medellín: Costs, EPS, Insurance

Finding an English-speaking doctor in Medellín is the easy half. The hard half is how you pay. On a tourist entry stamp you cannot join Colombia's public insurance, the EPS system, and Colombian law bars companies from selling you the private prepaid plans that sit on top of it. So for most short-stay nomads every consultation is private and paid on the spot, and your travel insurance is the only thing standing between you and the bill.

This guide covers both halves: where the lists of English-speaking doctors come from and what a private visit costs, then how the Colombian system works for a foreigner, from EPS enrolment on a visa to urgencias, the 123 emergency line, pharmacy rules and how to check a doctor's licence yourself. Prices are in US dollars at COP 3,329.61 per dollar, Colombia's official TRM exchange rate for 25 September 2026.

Where to find an English-speaking doctor in Medellín

The most useful starting point is a government list, not a review site. The UK Foreign Office publishes a list of medical facilities in Colombia, last updated 8 September 2025, with a column recording whether staff speak English. It names three Medellín hospitals. Two are private and marked Yes: the Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe and the Clínica Medellín. The third, the public Hospital General de Medellín, is marked Some.

Our directory of English-speaking doctors in Medellín starts from that list and adds providers that state their languages themselves. It holds six entries, last checked between 12 and 15 August 2026, and every card names the source its language claim came from. Three come from the Foreign Office list; the others are two English-language telemedicine and home-visit services and one hospital listed in a Spanish-language guide for English speakers.

Six is small, and it is small on purpose. It counts only doctors whose working language a published source states, not every doctor in the city who may speak some English. If you need a wider net, the doctors we list across Colombia add 13 more, among them seven in Bogotá that all come from official lists, six from the Foreign Office and one from the Austrian embassy.

What a private doctor visit in Medellín costs

Paying privately is called particular, and it is how a tourist sees a doctor here. The clearest published prices come from the English-speaking doctor services in our directory, because they sell to foreigners and post their rates. As of September 2026, one lists $100 for a 30-minute video consultation, $150 for a 30-minute office visit and $225 for a 45-minute home visit, all priced in dollars.

A second service sets its prices in pesos, which convert to $63 for a 30-minute video call, $95 for a 45-minute home visit in El Poblado or Laureles, and $105 for a video consultation with an internal medicine specialist. Both are English-first services built for expats, so treat their prices as the top of the range for a routine visit rather than the norm.

The large hospitals do not publish a consultation price list. They quote on request. The Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe, for example, runs a separate booking channel for appointments paid privately, by an insurance policy or by medicina prepagada. For procedures, its international office states that payment is due in full before treatment and that the final invoice can differ from the initial budget. Ask for the quote in writing before you agree to anything larger than a consultation.

How Colombia's health system works in Medellín: EPS and IPS

Two acronyms explain almost everything. An EPS (Entidad Promotora de Salud) is the insurer: you enrol with one, it collects your contributions and it decides which providers you use. An IPS (Institución Prestadora de Servicios de Salud) is the provider: the clinic, hospital or laboratory that actually treats you. Every IPS has to be registered in the national register of providers, the REPS.

The EPS system has two regimes. The contributory regime is for people who pay: employees, pensioners and the self-employed. The subsidised regime is for people without the means to pay and is means-tested through the Sisbén survey. A foreign remote worker earning abroad belongs in the contributory regime if they belong anywhere.

What an EPS member gets is the national benefits plan, the PBS, which runs from preventive care to high-complexity treatment. Members owe moderating fees and co-payments under Article 187 of Ley 100, and they are treated in the IPS network their EPS has contracted. That network is the catch if you want an English-speaking doctor: you cannot pick one unless they happen to work inside your EPS's network.

Who can join a Medellín EPS as a foreigner

The answer turns on one card, the cédula de extranjería. Colombia's Foreign Ministry says in its visa FAQ that everyone aged 7 or over who holds a visa valid for more than three months must request one, and must register with Migración Colombia within 15 calendar days of arriving or of the visa being issued. A tourist on an entry stamp holds no visa and gets no cédula.

The enrolment rules then close the loop. Decreto 780 of 2016, the consolidated health regulation, lists the identity documents valid for enrolment, and for foreigners they are the cédula de extranjería, a passport, a diplomatic card or a safe-conduct pass, "as applicable". The Ministry of Health's guide to migrant enrolment, written for Venezuelan migrants, accepts a passport only for children under 7. For an adult, the cédula is the document that works.

The same decree places in the contributory regime "all persons resident in the country" with no employer whose monthly income is at least one minimum wage. So the practical sequence for a nomad is fixed: obtain a visa longer than three months, register with Migración Colombia inside 15 days, receive the cédula, then enrol with an EPS of your choice. On a 90-day stamp, none of those steps is open to you.

What EPS membership costs a self-employed foreigner

The contribution is 12.5% of your contribution base, set by Article 204 of Ley 100 of 1993, and the base can never be lower than the minimum wage. An employee splits that with the employer; a self-employed person pays all of it. The 2026 minimum wage is set in pesos and converts to $526 a month, the figure restated by Decreto 0159 of 19 February 2026 after the Consejo de Estado suspended the original December decree.

That gives a floor of about $66 a month, converted from pesos, for a self-employed member declaring the minimum. Declare more income and the base rises with it. Independent workers pay monthly in arrears through the PILA, the integrated contributions form, based on what they earned the previous month.

Two rules work in your favour. An EPS may not ask about your health as a condition of enrolment, which means no exclusions for conditions you already have. And Decreto 780 gives a member access to the benefits plan from the date of enrolment. For a nomad with a chronic condition, that is the difference between the EPS and every private option below.

Medellín's medicina prepagada: the private layer on top of an EPS

Medicina prepagada is private health cover sold by prepaid-medicine companies, one of the voluntary plans Decreto 780 allows on top of the public benefits. It gives access to the private clinics and specialists in the company's own network, which is where a foreigner with a cédula gets the widest choice of English-speaking doctors. It is not an alternative to the EPS, though. Article 2.2.4.4 of Decreto 780 says these plans can be sold or renewed only to people enrolled in the contributory regime, as contributors or beneficiaries.

So prepagada is a second layer, open only to foreigners who already have a cédula and an EPS. The rules on it differ from the EPS in ways that matter. The company may run an entry examination and exclude conditions you already have. At renewal it cannot add new exclusions for illnesses that started after you signed, and it must renew the contract unless you breach it.

Once you hold both, you choose at the moment of use whether to go through your EPS or your plan, and the company cannot make you use the EPS first. Every plan's content, exclusions and prices must be filed with the Superintendencia Nacional de Salud, the health regulator, 30 days before it goes on sale. The same Superintendencia investigates and sanctions an EPS that breaks the enrolment rules.

Travel insurance for Medellín, and the cover the nomad visa requires

On a tourist stamp, international insurance is the whole plan. Colombia lets visa-exempt nationals stay 90 days, extendable to a maximum of 180 days in a calendar year, and during that time no EPS or prepagada is open to you. The UK Foreign Office's health advice for Colombia tells travellers to carry insurance for local treatment and medical evacuation, and to call the insurer quickly if they are referred to a facility.

The digital nomad visa, the V Nómada Digital, makes insurance a condition. The Foreign Ministry's visa requirements ask for "a health policy with coverage in national territory against all risks in case of accident, illness, maternity, disability, hospitalisation, death or repatriation, for the time planned for the stay". The requirement names the risks and the territory, not the issuer, so check your policy wording against each of those seven risks before you apply.

The same visa asks for income of three minimum wages over the last three months, a floor set in pesos that converts to about $1,578 a month at the 2026 wage and the September rate. It runs up to two years, and once it is issued for more than three months, it brings the cédula and with it the EPS route above. Our nomad visa finder sets Colombia's rule beside the other programmes.

Need a name before you land? The Medellín services directory lists doctors, dentists and lawyers by the language they work in, and each card links the source that states it, so you can check the claim yourself before you book.

Urgencias or consulta prioritaria: which door to use in Medellín

Colombian law gives everyone, insured or not, a right to initial emergency care. Article 168 of Ley 100 makes it compulsory for every public and private provider, "regardless of the ability to pay", and says it needs no contract or prior order. Decreto 780 adds that checking your insurance must come after triage and can never be a reason to delay that first stabilising care.

Triage sorts you into one of five categories set by Resolución 5596 of 2015. Category I is immediate, category II must be seen within 30 minutes, and category III needs tests or treatment in the emergency department. Categories IV and V are conditions with no evident risk to life or limb. Those are the patients who wait longest in an urgencias department.

For them the Ministry of Health points to outpatient care instead, naming consulta prioritaria alongside general and specialist consultations. For a fever, an ear infection or a sprain, a priority appointment at a clinic or a video consultation is the route the Ministry intends, and it spares you a queue that always serves category I and II patients first.

Know what the right to emergency care does not cover. "Initial" means stabilisation, an initial diagnosis and a decision on where you go next. Everything after that is billed, and without an EPS the bill is yours or your insurer's. Nor is an English-speaking doctor guaranteed on the night. The Hospital General de Medellín, the public hospital on the Foreign Office list, runs a high-complexity emergency department open 24 hours a day, every day of the year.

Medellín's emergency number 123 and ambulances

Call 123 and ask for an ambulance. That is the UK Foreign Office's instruction for Colombia, and 123 is also the emergency number on our Medellín city page. Have your address in Spanish ready, with the street format (Calle or Carrera plus the numbers).

Carry your passport, or your cédula once you have one, because the hospital will register you by document number. Put your insurer's emergency line in your phone before you need it; most policies require you to notify them before or soon after admission, and the Foreign Office advice repeats that.

One case works differently. If you are hurt in a traffic accident, as a pedestrian or a passenger, your treatment is billed to the SOAT of the vehicle involved, the compulsory traffic accident insurance for motor vehicles. Decreto 780 lists what it covers, from emergency care and surgery to medicines and rehabilitation. Get the vehicle's plate and its SOAT details at the scene if you can.

Medellín pharmacies and the prescription rules

Pharmacies are called droguerías, carry a cross on the sign, and most of them deliver. The rule that matters is on the box. Any medicine labelled "Venta bajo fórmula médica" may be dispensed only against a prescription; Decreto 780 makes demanding one an obligation of the person behind the counter, and forbids them from recommending medicines or swapping the one prescribed.

A Colombian prescription has a set form. It must be written in Spanish, use the generic name, give doses in the metric system and carry the prescriber's name, signature and professional registration number. A prescription from home does not meet that standard, which is why the Foreign Office warns that you may need a local doctor's prescription to get some medicines. If you see an English-speaking doctor for a repeat prescription, bring the packaging or your home prescription so they can write the generic name.

Medicines under special control, the medicamentos de control especial, follow a separate regime under Resolución 1478 of 2006, which Decreto 780 cross-references. If you take one, bring enough for the whole trip in its original packaging with a copy of your prescription, in your carry-on bag, as the Foreign Office advises. Then book a local consultation early rather than when you run out.

How to check a Medellín doctor's registration in ReTHUS

Colombia keeps one national register of licensed health workers, the ReTHUS (Registro Único Nacional del Talento Humano en Salud). Under Ley 1164 of 2007, doctors, dentists, nurses and other health professionals must be inscribed in it to practise, and the register also records sanctions handed down by the medical ethics tribunals.

The Ministry of Health describes the public search as the place where "citizens can verify whether health personnel have met the requirements to practise", including whether a doctor, dentist or nurse has been sanctioned. The query runs on the Ministry's SISPRO portal and takes either an identity document number or a first name and surname, and it tells you whether the person is registered and for which profession.

Run the check before you book an English-speaking doctor you found through an advert or a social media group rather than an official list. Two details make it practical. The professional registration number has to be printed on every prescription, so the paper you are handed already gives you something to check. And a doctor who trained abroad must have the degree formally recognised in Colombia, a process called convalidación, before registering. A foreign-trained doctor practising here should therefore still appear in the register.

Why English is more common in some Medellín clinics than others

English in the city's medicine follows the money that arrives from abroad. The Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe's international office says it has served patients from other countries for more than 20 years, advertises multilingual staff, and lists executive check-ups, cosmetic and bariatric surgery, transplants and oncology among its services. The Clínica Medellín takes international enquiries through a bilingual Spanish and English form.

Dentistry shows the pattern more sharply. Half of the eight entries in our list of English-speaking dentists in Medellín come from a dental directory that lists clinics treating international patients, and two of the others describe their own services in terms of international patients, translation and transport. Where a clinic sells treatment to people flying in, English is part of the product, and that is where an English-speaking doctor is easiest to find.

The Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe's definition of an international patient explains who that office is for: someone who has lived outside Colombia for at least six months, or someone living in Colombia with international medical insurance. A nomad with an international policy fits the second case, which makes that office, with its advertised multilingual staff, the one to contact. At the public hospital, the Foreign Office records English as "Some", so bring a translation app or a Spanish speaker.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find an English-speaking doctor in Medellín?

Start with the UK Foreign Office list of medical facilities in Colombia, which marks English as "Yes" at the Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe and the Clínica Medellín, and "Some" at the public Hospital General. Our Medellín doctors directory adds services that state their languages themselves, each with the source named.

How much does an English-speaking doctor cost in Medellín?

English-speaking doctor services in Medellín publish their rates: one charges $150 for a 30-minute office visit and $225 for a home visit, another $95 for a home visit and $63 for a video call, as of September 2026. Large hospitals quote private consultation prices on request.

Can a tourist join an EPS in Medellín?

No. Adult foreigners enrol with a cédula de extranjería, which is issued only to holders of a visa valid for more than three months. A tourist entry stamp gives neither. Medicina prepagada is closed too, because Decreto 780 lets companies sell it only to people already enrolled in the contributory regime.

What health insurance does Colombia's digital nomad visa require?

The Foreign Ministry asks for a health policy covering Colombian territory against accident, illness, maternity, disability, hospitalisation, death and repatriation, for the whole planned stay. Check each risk against your policy wording. The visa also requires income of three minimum wages, about $1,578 a month in 2026.

Will a Medellín hospital treat me in an emergency without insurance?

Yes. Ley 100 obliges every public and private provider to give initial emergency care regardless of ability to pay, with no prior order, and insurance checks must wait until after triage. That covers stabilisation and a first diagnosis. Treatment after that is billed to you or your insurer.

What is the emergency number in Medellín for an ambulance?

Dial 123 and ask for an ambulance, as the UK Foreign Office advises for all of Colombia. Have your address in Spanish ready with its Calle or Carrera numbers, carry your passport, and call your insurer's emergency line as soon as you are admitted.

How can I check that a doctor in Medellín is licensed?

Search the ReTHUS, Colombia's national register of health professionals, on the Ministry of Health's SISPRO portal by identity number or by first name and surname, including any English-speaking doctor you found online. It shows whether the person is registered to practise and any sanction from the ethics tribunals. Every Colombian prescription carries the prescriber's registration number.

Can I buy prescription medicine at a Medellín pharmacy without a prescription?

Not legally, for anything labelled "Venta bajo fórmula médica". Decreto 780 obliges the pharmacist to require a prescription written in Spanish, with the generic name and the prescriber's registration number. A prescription from home does not meet that format, so book a local consultation early.
