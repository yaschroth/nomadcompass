Tax Adviser in Portugal: NIF, IFICI and IRS for Nomads

Since 2009, remote workers have passed round a story about Portugal. Move to Lisbon, register as a Non-Habitual Resident, pay a flat 20 percent on your salary and nothing on your foreign income for ten years. It still turns up in forums and in guides written before 2024, and for anyone arriving now, almost none of it holds.

The story was never the only reason people came. Lisbon gets about 2,875 hours of sunshine a year, among the most in Europe, and its old centre is built across steep hills above the Tagus. The No. 28 tram leaves Praça Martim Moniz, climbs to Graça and rattles down through Alfama, ringing its bell past the Miradouro de Santa Luzia, where tiled panels show Praça do Comércio as it looked before the 1755 earthquake.

From the Miradouro da Senhora do Monte, above Graça, the castle stands to the left, and the river and its estuary glow behind the rooftops of Baixa. The light and the hills were the first pull. Then Web Summit moved to Lisbon in 2016 and later committed to stay for ten years, and since October 2022 Portugal has issued visas built for people who work remotely for employers and clients abroad.

The tax story sat on top of all that, and it is the part that has changed most. The law now tells a plainer story. The Non-Habitual Resident regime closed to new arrivals on 1 January 2024. Its successor keeps a 20 percent rate only for listed jobs certified by Portuguese bodies, and a remote job for a foreign employer is not one of them. A salary earned at a desk in Graça is Portuguese income, whoever pays it, taxed at the ordinary rates once you are resident.

So the real work is less glamorous: a tax number, the day residency starts, the first return and, for freelancers, registrations with dates attached. A remote employee of one foreign company can do most of it alone and may want help only with the first, split year. A freelancer should hire an adviser from the start. Anyone a Portuguese employer or startup might hire should talk to one before the 15 January after arriving, because that deadline cannot be recovered.

What happened to the 20 percent deal

The Non-Habitual Resident regime was created in 2009 and revoked from 1 January 2024 by that year's budget law. Even at its height it was narrower than the story: the flat 20 percent applied to work done in Portugal in a list of high-value activities, while the famous exemptions covered foreign dividends, rents and gains taxed elsewhere. "Twenty percent on everything" was never the deal.

A transitional rule let in a last group who became resident in 2024 on the strength of a contract, lease, visa or immigration process dated 2023, and they had until 31 March 2025 to register. Those already inside keep NHR until their ten years run out, so you may still meet people in Lisbon paying 20 percent. Their bill is no guide to yours, and an adviser's website still selling NHR to new clients has not been checked in a while.

The new 20 percent, and why it rarely reaches a laptop

The replacement is IFICI, the tax incentive for scientific research and innovation, often sold as "NHR 2.0". It taxes qualifying employment and self-employment income at a special 20 percent for ten consecutive years, and foreign-source income is generally exempt, though it still counts when the rate on your other income is set. Pensions are left out of that exemption, and dividends from blacklisted jurisdictions are taxed at 35 percent.

You must become tax resident, must not have been resident in the previous five years, and must never have used NHR or the returning-residents regime; it can be used once. Then comes the list that excludes most nomads: research and university teaching, qualified jobs at companies that export at least half their turnover, R&D staff and jobs at certified startups, each certified by a Portuguese body such as the science foundation FCT, AICEP or IAPMEI.

Every item is defined by the entity you work for, and for a highly qualified profession the company must prove by 15 March that you meet the conditions. A foreign employer with no presence in Portugal has nothing to prove it with. The D8 visa is built on income from outside Portugal and IFICI on work for a qualifying entity inside it, so the two meet only when a Portuguese employer or startup hires you.

If that is you, the date matters more than anything. You apply on the Portal das Finanças by 15 January of the year after you become resident, so resident from 2026 means applying by 15 January 2027. A late registration takes effect only from the year it is filed, and the missed years come off the ten.

The day Lisbon starts taxing you

Your visa does not make you tax resident; the IRS code does. You are resident for a year if you spend more than 183 days in Portugal, consecutive or not, in any 12-month period starting or ending in that year, or if at any point you have a home you appear to intend to keep as your habitual residence. The second test needs no day count at all.

Residency begins on the first day you are here. Say you land on 1 March with a D8 and move into a flat in Graça: January and February are non-resident months, and from March Portugal taxes your worldwide income. It works the other way too. A year-long lease on a flat you furnish and keep while you travel looks like a habitual residence whatever your day count says, so if you mean to stay under the line, ask an adviser before you sign it.

What the D8 changes, and what it does not

Portugal's consulates issue two visas for remote work: a residency visa, the D8, and a shorter temporary stay visa. The steps and documents are in our step-by-step guide to the Portugal D8 visa. The income floor is four times the Portuguese minimum wage, about $4,196 a month for 2026; our comparison of digital nomad visa income requirements has the rest.

The visa does not flip your tax status, but a D8 holder who actually lives here meets a residency test within the first year. Then comes the rule the old story left out: pay for work performed in Portugal is Portuguese-source income, whoever pays it. A salary from New York earned on a terrace above the Tagus is taxed at the general rates of 12.5 to 48 percent, with the top rate above $98,800. Any relief comes from a treaty credit for tax paid elsewhere, not from a special regime.

The first errand: a NIF

Everything starts with the NIF, the número de identificação fiscal: nine digits that stay the same whether you are recorded as resident or not. A landlord in Alfama or a bank in Baixa will ask for it long before any tax office does, and you need it for a job and social security too.

There are two official routes to a NIF for a foreign citizen. In person, you book a Serviço de Finanças or a Loja do Cidadão, online or on +351 217 206 707, and bring your passport and proof of your foreign address. Remotely, a legal representative with your power of attorney files through the e-balcão service. A power of attorney given to an advogado or a solicitador needs no signature certification, which is why remote NIF services are so often law offices.

The number is issued to you as a non-resident at your foreign address. When you become resident, you must request the change of address yourself; nothing updates on its own.

The fiscal representative you probably do not need

Much of the advice online is out of date here. No fiscal representative is required when the NIF is assigned, and for residents of the EU, Norway, Iceland or Liechtenstein a representative is always optional. Living anywhere else, you have 15 days after forming a tax link with Portugal, such as property, a car, a Portuguese job or self-employed work here, to appoint one or sign up for electronic notifications.

One exception survives: a non-resident from outside the EU working self-employed in Portugal must still appoint a VAT representative resident here. All of these rules are about non-residents. Once you live in Lisbon and have moved your tax address, the question falls away, and an adviser still billing you for representation is charging for nothing.

Freelancing from Lisbon: green receipts, coefficients and VAT

Freelancers are the group for whom an adviser most often pays for itself, because the duties start before the work does. You file a declaration of start of activity before your first job, then issue an invoice or receipt for every payment through the tax authority's own application. These are the recibos verdes, the green receipts.

Most freelancers fall under the simplified regime, open while the previous year's self-employed income stays under $228,000. It taxes a fixed share of what you bill: 75 percent for professions on the official activity table, 35 percent for other services, cut by half in your first year and by a quarter in the second if you have no salary or pension that year.

The 75 percent has a catch: the code expects documented expenses of at least 15 percent of fees and adds any shortfall back, so keep every receipt with your NIF on it.

VAT runs on its own track. You are exempt if your turnover in Portugal stayed under $17,100 the year before and you made no exports. Whether fees from a client in London or Austin count towards that is decided service by service, a question for an adviser before the first invoice goes out.

The second bill: social security

Income tax is only half of what a freelancer owes, and social security for the self-employed runs on its own clock: you declare earnings each quarter and pay monthly, between the 10th and the 20th of the following month. The contribution is 21.4 percent of "relevant income", which for services is 70 percent of what you invoiced. Bill $4,000 a month and you pay about $599.

New freelancers get a long start, since a first registration only takes effect on the first day of the twelfth month after activity begins. The quarterly declaration after that month is the one people forget, so put it in your calendar, or your adviser's.

A Lisbon tax year, season by season

The tax year is the calendar year. January in Lisbon is mild, with daytime highs of 14 to 19°C, and brings the first quarterly social security declaration and, for anyone who became resident the year before, the IFICI deadline on the 15th.

On 1 April the annual return, the Modelo 3, opens online, with foreign income on Annex J beside the tax paid abroad. It closes on 30 June, at the end of the month when Alfama, Graça and Mouraria hang coloured garlands for the Santo António festivities, the neighbourhood marches parade down Avenida da Liberdade on the evening of 12 June, and the old streets smell of grilled sardines. File before the festas and the rest of June is yours.

Tax due is paid, and refunds arrive, by 31 August. Your first return covers the arrival year split at the arrival date, and getting that split right is the most common reason a new resident pays an adviser for one year and then files alone.

Two countries, one income

Portugal has 78 double tax conventions in force, each deciding which country may tax which income and which one gives credit. Two matter most to English-speaking nomads. The US treaty dates from 1994, but the US taxes its citizens wherever they live, so an American in Lisbon still files a US return; our guide to how digital nomads are taxed covers the US side. A new UK convention entered into force on 29 December 2025 and applies to UK income tax from 6 April 2026.

A treaty never removes the Portuguese filing duty. As a resident you declare worldwide income, with the foreign part and the tax already paid on Annex J, which is how the credit is claimed. Leaving it off because "it was already taxed" is the mistake a first-year review exists to catch.

Who you can hire, and what only they may do

Portugal protects professional titles more tightly than the advice itself. Legal consultation is a competence of advogados and solicitadores but not reserved to them, so tax advice is open to more people than the titles are. Court work is reserved, and the tax courts require a legal representative: a dispute that leaves the tax office needs a lawyer.

The contabilista certificado, a certified accountant, has one exclusive act: signing the accounts and returns of anyone with organised accounting, where your freelance income lands once it outgrows the simplified regime. In practice, a contabilista handles returns, green receipts, social security and IFICI filings, and an advogado handles disputes and the legal side of residency. Anyone else calling themselves a "tax consultant" holds no protected title at all.

Checking an adviser before you pay

The Ordem dos Contabilistas Certificados keeps a public register of certified accountants showing whether each registration is active or suspended. Ask for the name of the person who will sign your returns and look it up: the register lists individuals, not firms. For an advogado or solicitador, ask for the professional number and confirm it with the Ordem dos Advogados or the Ordem dos Solicitadores e dos Agentes de Execução.

Read any power of attorney before you sign it, then get the scope in writing: which returns, whether the quarterly social security declarations are included, and who files for IFICI. When the fee is worth it

The old story promised a rate. What Lisbon actually hands a newcomer is a sequence: a NIF before the lease, a residency date that splits the first year, a start-of-activity declaration before the first invoice, 15 January if IFICI could apply, and a return between April and June. An adviser earns the fee on the judgement calls in that sequence, not on the forms.

Pay for the first year if you are a remote employee, since the split year and the treaty credit are where mistakes cost money. Pay throughout if you freelance, at least until the coefficients, VAT and social security run on autopilot. Then close the laptop, take the 28 up to Graça and watch the estuary light up the rooftops of Baixa: that part of the story was always true.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the NHR tax regime still available in Portugal?

Not to new arrivals. NHR was revoked from 1 January 2024, and the last transitional registrations closed on 31 March 2025. People already registered keep it until their ten years end.

Can a digital nomad get the 20% IFICI tax rate in Portugal?

Rarely. IFICI covers listed activities certified by a Portuguese body, such as research, university teaching, certified startups and qualifying jobs at exporting companies. A remote job for a foreign employer has no entity in Portugal to certify it.

What is the IFICI application deadline?

15 January of the year after you become tax resident, on the Portal das Finanças. A late registration only takes effect from the year it is made, so the earlier years are lost from the ten.

When do I become a tax resident in Portugal?

After more than 183 days in any 12-month period starting or ending in the tax year, or as soon as you hold a home you appear to intend as your habitual residence. The arrival year is split at your first day there.

Is a remote salary from a foreign employer taxed in Portugal?

Yes, once you are resident. Pay for work performed in Portugal is Portuguese-source income whoever pays it, taxed at 12.5% to 48%. A treaty can give credit for tax paid abroad, claimed on Annex J.

How much social security does a freelancer pay in Portugal?

21.4% of relevant income, which for services is 70% of what you invoiced. Billing $4,000 a month means about $599 a month. First-time self-employed workers start paying on the first day of the twelfth month after opening their activity.

Do I need a tax adviser in Portugal as a digital nomad?

Not by law, unless your freelance income moves you into organised accounting, which a certified accountant must sign, or you end up in tax court, which requires a lawyer. Freelancers and anyone who might qualify for IFICI gain most from one.
