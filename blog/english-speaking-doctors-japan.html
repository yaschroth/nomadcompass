English-Speaking Doctors in Japan: How to Find and Pay

Finding an English-speaking doctor in Japan is a solved problem that almost nobody explains. Every hospital and clinic in the country must report its services to its prefecture, including the foreign languages it treats patients in, and since April 2024 the health ministry has published all of it in one national search tool. In Tokyo alone, 1,966 English-speaking doctors on our list come from that register. The harder questions are what the language flag actually promises, and what a visit costs if you are not in Japan's public insurance.

This guide answers both. It covers how to read the ministry's register of English-speaking doctors, the separate list of hospitals for foreign visitors, National Health Insurance and its 30% co-payment, what hospitals charge uninsured patients, the insurance rule on the digital nomad visa, and the emergency lines that answer in English. Prices are in US dollars at $1 = ¥158.54, the rate of 1 August 2026; amounts that Japan sets in yen are converted at that rate.

Medical Information Net: Japan's official register of English-speaking doctors

The system is called Iryō Jōhō Netto (医療情報ネット, "Medical Information Net"), nicknamed Navii. It rests on a duty created by the 2006 revision of the Medical Care Act: hospitals, clinics and dental clinics must report their services to the prefectural governor, who publishes them. Until 2024 each prefecture ran its own website. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) merged them into one national system that went live in April 2024, and pharmacies report into it as well.

For an English speaker, three features matter. The search screen has a dedicated foreign-language filter, next to filters for clinics open right now and for night and holiday care. The interface is offered in English, simplified and traditional Chinese, and Korean, but through machine translation of the Japanese pages, so facility names and free-text notes can come out garbled. And each facility's page carries a block headed 対応することができる外国語の種類, "foreign languages that can be handled", which is where the language claim lives.

Start at the Medical Information Net search page, choose 外国語 (foreign language) in the detailed search, then narrow by ward and by department. The Japanese interface under your browser's own translation is often easier to use than the built-in English one. It keeps the facility's Japanese name intact, which is what your map app and a taxi driver will recognise.

What the English flag on a Japanese clinic listing means

The language block holds more than a yes or no. For each language, a facility can state the days and hours it is available, the departments it covers, a phone number for that language, and a level on a four-step scale: native-like (母国語なみ), everyday conversation (日常会話程度), a few words (片言) and none. A separate field says whether the facility uses a multilingual voice-translation device, and a free-text box holds anything else it wants to add.

Tokyo's form adds the field that matters most. It is called 対応可否区分, and it records whether patients in that language are accepted outright (受入可能, ukeire kanō) or only after consultation (要相談, yō sōdan). None of the other seven prefectures we read for our directory fill it in. A Tokyo clinic marked 要相談 for English is asking you to phone first, which is the one call an English speaker is least able to make.

Now the limits. The flag is the facility's own report, published by the ministry, not a test anyone sat. It does not say that every doctor speaks the language, or that reception does. The level fields exist, and on the thousands of pages we read they were blank. The hours field sometimes confines English to one doctor's sessions. And a listed language can rest entirely on a translation app, which the facility discloses only in its notes, in Japanese. Read the notes before you go.

How we built a list of English-speaking doctors in Tokyo from the register

Our directory uses the register as its source and turns those doubts into rules. A language is dropped when the facility's own notes say it relies on a translation device or an interpreter, asks patients to call first, or offers only a few words. In Tokyo, where the form asks, only languages marked 受入可能 survive.

Facilities reporting more than six languages are refused whole, because one clinic claiming a dozen is likelier to be an optimistic form than a multilingual staff. Company clinics that treat only one employer's workers are left out. The filter is strict on purpose, and a language that survives it is one the facility has stated without qualification.

The result is 2,111 doctors whose working language we can trace to a published source: 2,101 from the MHLW register, 8 from the UK Foreign Office's list of medical facilities in Japan and 2 from a Korean consulate. 1,966 are listed in English, 293 in Chinese, 111 in Korean and 35 in French, across 19 languages, last checked between 15 August and 24 September 2026.

Every card links to the facility's own page on the register, so you can read the fields yourself rather than trust our summary. Beyond the capital, the national list of doctors by language holds 5,915 in 15 cities, and Osaka's English-speaking doctors are the second largest group, with 962 of the city's 1,014. Outside Tokyo the acceptance field is left empty, so there a listed language stands unless the facility's own notes hedge it.

Japan's list of hospitals that accept foreign patients

The second official source works differently. MHLW and the Japan Tourism Agency jointly publish a list of medical institutions that accept foreign patients, and here the prefecture decides who goes on it: a facility must be willing to treat foreign patients and be judged suitable by the prefectural government. The current edition was published on 30 June 2026 and amended on 6 August 2026, with Tokyo among the prefectures whose entries changed.

Tokyo's sheet names 505 institutions, and more than 450 of them name English. Category 1 hubs can admit emergency patients who need a bed, and every prefecture must have at least one. Category 2 hubs, including clinics and dentists, accept foreign patients in each secondary care area. Tokyo has 35 in Category 1 and 280 in Category 2. The list also records the credit cards each one takes, whether it runs 24-hour care, and JMIP accreditation, a third-party certification of a hospital's services for international patients.

The JNTO medical institution search presents the same data in English, Chinese and Korean, searchable by prefecture, language, department, card brand and JMIP status. For the June 2026 edition the ministry said JNTO's copy would be updated later, so the Excel file on the MHLW site can be the newer of the two. Use this list for hospitals, where a prefecture's judgement is worth having, and the register for an English-speaking doctor around the corner.

Clinic or hospital in Japan: where to see a doctor first

Japan runs on free access: the ministry lists the patient's freedom to choose any medical institution as a founding feature of the system. In October 2023 there were 104,894 clinics and 8,122 hospitals, plus 66,818 dental clinics. For a cold, a rash or a repeat prescription, an English-speaking doctor at a local clinic (クリニック or 医院, iin) is the right door. It is often a single doctor with the specialty painted on the sign.

Large hospitals push back with a surcharge. Since 1 October 2022, specified function hospitals (mostly university hospitals) and regional medical support hospitals and referral-focused hospitals with 200 or more beds must charge patients who arrive without a referral letter at least $44 on a first visit and at least $19 on a return visit. For dental care the floors are $32 and $12. The fee sits on top of the bill, and emergencies are exempt.

That figure is a floor, not a price. The Japanese Red Cross Medical Center in Tokyo's Shibuya ward charges $69 for a first visit without a referral. Seeing a clinic first and asking it for a referral letter (紹介状, shōkaijō) is the cheaper route into a big hospital, and the letter tells the specialist what the clinic already found.

National Health Insurance in Japan: who joins and what the 30% covers

Everyone living in Japan is expected to belong to a public health scheme. Employees join their employer's, and everyone else with an address in a municipality joins National Health Insurance (kokumin kenkō hoken), run by the city or ward. Foreign residents join on the same terms. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's rules exclude four foreign groups: people whose period of stay is three months or less, people under designated activities for medical treatment or for sightseeing and recreation, diplomats, and people staying illegally.

You enrol at the ward office within 14 days of moving in. Premiums are set by each ward or city, which quotes yours when you register. The co-payment is set by age: 30% from school age to 69, 20% before compulsory schooling, 20% from 70 to 74 and 10% from 75, with higher earners over 70 paying 30%. Insured prices come from a national fee schedule counted in points, each worth about 6 US cents, so the clinic does not set them.

A monthly cap keeps the 30% from becoming ruinous. The ministry's own worked example is a $6,308 hospital bill: 30% is $1,892 at the counter, and the high-cost medical expense benefit (kōgaku ryōyōhi) returns everything above the cap. The caps rose in August 2026. For the middle income band the monthly cap is now $541 plus 1% of costs above $1,804, which brings that bill to about $586, and a new annual limit of $3,343 applies.

What uninsured visitors pay to see a doctor in Japan

If you are not in a Japanese scheme, you pay the whole bill for an English-speaking doctor or any other, and the price is not fixed. Treating an uninsured visitor is free-practice medicine (自由診療, jiyū shinryō), so each hospital sets its own rate. The ministry's 2023 survey of hospitals, published in July 2024, found that more than nine in ten price it off the national fee schedule, multiplying the points by a rate of their own choosing.

Of 5,424 hospitals that used the schedule, 85.4% charged the insured rate of about 6 cents a point, or less. Another 7.3% charged up to one and a half times that rate, 4.8% up to double and 2.5% more than double.

The pattern flips at the hospitals most set up for foreigners: among the 81 accredited under JMIP or the Japan International Hospitals scheme, 71.6% charged above the insured rate and 39.5% charged more than double. The $6,308 stay above costs exactly that at an insured-rate hospital and more than $12,615 at one charging over double.

Interpreters can be a separate line on the bill. Only 2.2% of hospitals charged for one, against 23.5% of the accredited ones, and the survey notes that hospitals may charge the fee to insured patients too. The full figures are in the MHLW survey of hospitals treating foreign patients.

Unpaid bills follow you home and back. Since 10 May 2021 the ministry has collected hospital reports of visitors who leave bills unpaid above a set amount and shared them with the Immigration Services Agency for use at later entry checks. Since October 2022 a hospital no longer needs the patient's consent to report. The ministry's advice to visitors is to buy private insurance with enough cover before arriving.

Need a clinic this week? Our English-speaking doctors in Tokyo page sorts the register by ward and language, drops the hedged entries, and links every card to the ministry page it came from.

Japan's digital nomad visa and its health insurance rule

Japan's route for remote workers is a designated activities status, open since 2024 to nationals of 51 countries and regions that have both a visa exemption and a tax treaty with Japan. It lasts six months, cannot be extended, and once it ends you wait six months before applying again. Applicants need an annual income of at least $63,080, a threshold Japan sets in yen and converted here at the rate above, and a spouse or child can come along on a matching status.

The insurance clause is exact. The Immigration Services Agency's page for the status requires private medical travel insurance covering death, injury and illness for the whole stay, with at least $63,080 of cover for treatment of injury and illness, again a yen figure converted at our stated rate. A credit card's built-in travel cover is accepted if its terms meet that standard and you document them. An accompanying spouse or child needs the same cover, through their own policy or family cover under yours.

The status comes with no residence card, and National Health Insurance enrols people registered at an address in a municipality. For a nomad-visa holder, then, the private policy is the insurance you will actually use, and every visit to an English-speaking doctor is billed as an uninsured patient's. Ask your insurer whether it can pay a Japanese hospital directly, because otherwise the bill, at the hospital's own rate, goes on your card first. Emergencies in Tokyo: 119, #7119 and helplines in English

For an ambulance anywhere in Japan, dial 119, say it is a medical emergency (救急, kyūkyū), then give the address. In Tokyo the dispatcher can bring an interpreter into the call in English, Mandarin, Korean, Portuguese or Spanish, according to the Tokyo Metropolitan Government.

For the grey zone between waiting until morning and calling an ambulance, Tokyo has #7119, the Fire Department's emergency consultation centre, staffed around the clock by doctors, nurses and former ambulance crews. Press 1 for hospitals open now, 2 to describe symptoms. From a phone that cannot dial #7119, the numbers are 03-3212-2323 in the 23 wards and 042-521-2323 in Tama. The Fire Department's page for the service lists no foreign-language option.

Two lines answer in English by design. Himawari, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government's medical information service on 03-5285-8181, runs every day from 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. in English, Chinese, Korean, Thai and Spanish. It finds English-speaking doctors and clinics in other languages near you, and explains how the system works, free apart from the call. The Japan Visitor Hotline run by JNTO, on 050-3816-2787, answers 24 hours a day in English, Chinese and Korean.

One name to be careful with. Older guides send readers to the AMDA International Medical Information Center at amda-imic.com, which in 2019 ran a free phone interpreting line for patients. That domain has changed hands: web archives show it serving football streams in 2024, and the site now at the address calls itself an independent publisher affiliated with no provider. Treat any phone number printed next to that name as unverified.

Pharmacies in Japan: prescriptions and English-speaking staff

Japan separates prescribing from dispensing. Most clinics hand you a prescription to take to a dispensing pharmacy (調剤薬局, chōzai yakkyoku), usually a few doors away, and there were 62,828 pharmacies in March 2024. Pharmacies bill through the same public insurance as clinics, so an insured patient pays the same co-payment share at the counter.

Pharmacies report into Medical Information Net too, with a field for whether a language needs contact in advance. Our English-speaking pharmacies in Tokyo list holds 175 pharmacies whose language the register records, 155 of them in English, and counts a language only where the pharmacy serves customers in it without prior contact. The same register gives the city 1,084 dentists, and our English-speaking dentists in Tokyo page lists 999 of them in English.

Bringing medication into Japan: the Yunyu Kakunin-sho

Japan limits medicine by quantity. Without paperwork you may bring up to one month's supply of prescription medicine, up to two months of other medicines, and up to 24 units per item of topical products such as creams and eye drops. Pre-filled syringes and self-injection kits are allowed up to a month. Above those limits you need an import confirmation certificate, the Yunyu Kakunin-sho (輸入確認証), obtained before you leave home and shown at customs. Part of the application can now be made online.

Controlled drugs follow stricter rules. Narcotics such as morphine and fentanyl, and stimulant raw materials such as lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse), need prior permission from the director of a Regional Bureau of Health and Welfare. Psychotropics such as diazepam need a certificate above a month's supply or as injections. Amphetamine and methamphetamine are banned outright, and amphetamine is the active ingredient of Adderall.

The UK Foreign Office adds that some ordinary pharmacy products are illegal to carry, naming Vicks inhalers, cold and allergy medicines containing pseudoephedrine, and painkillers with codeine. The MHLW page for travellers bringing medicines gives the full limits in English and names the Kanto-Shin'etsu Regional Bureau as the contact for arrivals at Narita and Haneda. Six months on a nomad visa is well past the one-month allowance, so a regular prescription needs either the certificate or a Japanese prescription from an English-speaking doctor here.

Seeing a doctor in Japan: a first-week checklist for English speakers

  1. Work out which insurance you are in. With a residence card and an address, enrol in National Health Insurance at the ward office within 14 days. On the nomad visa or as a visitor, carry your private policy's details and its claims number.
  2. Save four numbers: 119 for an ambulance, #7119 for medical advice in Tokyo, Himawari on 03-5285-8181 for a clinic in your language, and the Japan Visitor Hotline on 050-3816-2787 for anything at night.
  3. Pick an English-speaking doctor near home before you need one. Filter the register by English, then read the facility's language notes and the days English is offered.
  4. Go to a clinic first and ask for a referral letter if you need a hospital, which avoids the surcharge of $44 or more.
  5. Check payment before the visit. The hospital list records which card brands each institution accepts; for a clinic off that list, ask when you book.
  6. Declare any medicine above the one-month or two-month limits before you fly, not at the airport.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find an English-speaking doctor in Japan?

Use the health ministry's Medical Information Net and filter by foreign language, or the JNTO search for hospitals that accept foreign patients. In Tokyo, prefer entries marked 受入可能, meaning English patients are accepted without phoning first. Our directory lists 1,966 English-speaking doctors in Tokyo drawn from that register.

How much does a doctor visit in Japan cost without insurance?

The full bill, at a rate the hospital chooses. In the ministry's 2023 survey, 85.4% of hospitals charged the insured rate of about 6 cents per fee-schedule point, or less. Among 81 hospitals accredited for international patients, 71.6% charged more, and 39.5% charged over double. Interpreter fees can be extra.

Can foreigners join National Health Insurance in Japan?

Yes, if you live in Japan with an address in a municipality and are not covered by an employer's scheme. Tokyo's rules exclude people with a period of stay of three months or less, medical or sightseeing designated activities, diplomats and illegal stays. You enrol within 14 days and pay a 30% co-payment from school age to 69.

Does Japan's digital nomad visa require health insurance?

Yes. The Immigration Services Agency requires private insurance covering death, injury and illness for the whole stay, with at least $63,080 for medical treatment. Card-linked travel cover counts if its terms meet that standard. The status gives no residence card, so the private policy is your working insurance in Japan.

Will the ambulance number in Japan answer in English?

Dial 119 and say it is a medical emergency, then give the address. Tokyo's dispatchers can add an interpreter in English, Mandarin, Korean, Portuguese or Spanish. For non-emergency advice, Himawari on 03-5285-8181 answers in English daily from 9 a.m. to 8 p.m., and the Japan Visitor Hotline runs 24 hours.

Do I need a referral to see a doctor at a hospital in Japan?

No, but large hospitals charge extra without one. University hospitals and hospitals with 200 or more beds in the relevant categories must charge at least $44 on a first visit and $19 on a return visit. The Japanese Red Cross Medical Center in Tokyo charges $69. Emergencies are exempt.

Can I bring my prescription medication into Japan?

Up to one month's supply of prescription medicine, two months of other medicines, without paperwork. Above that you need a Yunyu Kakunin-sho import certificate before you travel. Narcotics and stimulant raw materials such as lisdexamfetamine need prior permission, and amphetamine, the active ingredient of Adderall, is banned.

Are there English-speaking pharmacies in Tokyo?

Yes. The health ministry's register records languages for pharmacies too, and our Tokyo list holds 175 whose language it records, 155 in English, counting only those that serve customers in that language without prior contact. Most clinics send you to a dispensing pharmacy nearby with a paper prescription.
