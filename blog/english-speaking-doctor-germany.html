English-Speaking Doctor in Germany: Find, Book and Pay

The quickest route to an English-speaking doctor in Germany is an official search, not an expat forum. The Arztsuche at 116117.de, run by the national association of statutory-insurance doctors, lets you filter practices by the foreign languages they report, and its English page links straight to a pre-filtered search for English. Two catches decide how useful that is to you. The 116117 phone line itself is German only. And the appointment service behind it books for statutory patients, not for the privately insured.

So the practical question is less "where is a doctor who speaks English" than "which system am I in". A statutory patient shows a card and pays nothing at the practice. A private patient gets an invoice under a federal fee schedule: a standard consultation plus a symptom-focused examination comes to $24.45 at the usual multiplier. Every dollar figure in this guide is converted at the European Central Bank exchange rate of 25 September 2026, $1.1403 per euro.

How to find an English-speaking doctor in Germany: the official search

The 116117 Arzt- und Psychotherapeutensuche is operated by the Kassenärztliche Bundesvereinigung (KBV) and filled with data from the regional Kassenärztliche Vereinigungen (KVs), the associations of doctors who treat statutory patients. Its search has a Fremdsprache field, and each practice card shows a "Fremdsprachen" line listing the languages that practice reports. The KVs collect those details from the practices themselves, so a listed language is the practice's own statement, not a tested certificate.

You do not need to navigate the German interface to use it. The 116117 English page links a ready-made English search for 14 specialisations, from general practitioner and gynaecologist to psychotherapist and radiologist. Enter a town or postcode in the field marked "Ort oder PLZ eingeben" and press "Suchen". The page's own advice is to phone for an appointment, because walking in without one means a longer wait.

Filter by specialty first and language second. A practice that lists English in Kreuzberg does you no good if you need a dermatologist and it is a general practice. Then read the card: opening hours, whether it treats statutory patients, and a phone number. Opening the call with "Sprechen Sie Englisch?" before booking costs thirty seconds and settles what the listing only claims.

What our English-speaking doctor directory holds for Germany

Our own list of English-speaking doctors in Germany holds 3,603 entries across five cities, and 3,580 of them are in Hamburg. That lopsided number has one cause: the Kassenärztliche Vereinigung Hamburg publishes the foreign languages each registered practice reports, and 3,577 of our German entries come from that list. Every card links to the KV Hamburg entry it came from, so you can check the listing at source before you ring.

Outside Hamburg our coverage is thin, and we say so on the pages. We list 8 English-speaking doctors in Berlin and 5 in Munich, drawn from consular lists that embassies publish for their own citizens: the UK Foreign Office's travel advice, the French, Austrian and Italian missions, and the medical guide Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs keeps for each country. Those lists are useful because a consulate has vetted them for its nationals. They are not a census.

The honest division of labour, then: for doctors in Hamburg our directory mirrors the official KV data with a language filter already applied. For doctors in Berlin and doctors in Munich, start with our consular-sourced entries and then run the 116117 search, which covers every statutory practice in both cities.

Statutory or private health insurance in Germany: the choice that decides your doctor visit

Anyone who lives in Germany must hold health insurance. Section 193(3) of the Insurance Contract Act (VVG) requires every resident to carry cover for outpatient and inpatient treatment, with any deductible capped at $5,702 a year. Members of the statutory system (GKV) satisfy the rule automatically. Everyone else needs a private policy (PKV) that meets it.

Which system you land in depends mostly on how you earn. Employees below the 2026 compulsory insurance threshold of $88,259 a year, or about $7,355 a month, are insured in the GKV through their employer, according to the federal government's figures for 2026. Most self-employed people are not compulsorily insured: they join the GKV voluntarily or buy private cover.

Voluntary GKV membership has a gate. Under Section 9 of Social Code Book V (SGB V), you must have been insured in the statutory system for at least 24 months in the last five years, or for 12 months without a break immediately before that insurance ended. A freelancer arriving from outside the EU with no German insurance history does not meet that, which leaves private insurance as the working option.

If you do join the GKV as a self-employed member, the contribution is a percentage of income with a floor. The Federal Ministry of Health lists a general rate of 14.6%, or 14.0% without sick pay, plus each fund's own add-on, which averages 2.9% in 2026. Contributions are charged on at least about $1,503 a month even if you earn less.

Adding long-term care insurance at 3.6% plus the 0.6% surcharge for people without children, the minimum for a childless freelancer without sick pay works out at about $317 a month at the average add-on rate. The ceiling for health cover alone is $1,160 a month with sick pay, again at the average add-on.

The difference at the practice is stark. A statutory patient hands over the insurance card and the practice bills the KV. A private patient receives an invoice, pays it, and claims it back from the insurer. That second model is where the fee schedule below matters.

What a private patient pays a doctor in Germany: the GOÄ fee schedule

Private medical fees in Germany follow the Gebührenordnung für Ärzte (GOÄ), a federal ordinance that assigns every service a number of points. Section 5 fixes the point value at about 6.65 US cents (the law sets it in euro cents) and lets a doctor multiply the resulting fee by 1 to 3.5 depending on difficulty and time. Above 2.3 the multiplier needs special justification, and Section 12 requires the invoice to explain it in writing. For technical services the ceiling is 2.5 and the threshold 1.8; for laboratory work, 1.3 and 1.15.

In practice 2.3 is the multiplier you will see on most invoices, because it is the highest a doctor can charge without writing a reason. Worked from the point values in the GOÄ schedule, the common GP items cost this at 2.3:

  • No. 1, consultation (80 points): $12.22
  • No. 5, symptom-focused examination (80 points): $12.22
  • No. 3, extended consultation, at least 10 minutes (150 points): $22.93
  • No. 7, full examination of one organ system (160 points): $24.46
  • No. 50, home visit including consultation and examination (320 points): $48.92
  • No. 70, short certificate or sick note (40 points): $6.11

A routine visit billed as No. 1 plus No. 5 therefore costs $24.45. The same two items at the 3.5 maximum come to $37.22. Blood tests, ultrasound and other procedures are billed on top as separate numbers, so a first visit with lab work runs well past the consultation figure. A reformed GOÄ has been drafted and passed to the Federal Ministry of Health, but as of September 2026 the version on the federal law portal is still the one in force.

The same schedule applies to anyone paying out of pocket, including a visitor on travel insurance. Section 1 of the GOÄ also limits billing to medically necessary care, unless you ask for more. Keep every invoice: the item numbers and multipliers on it are what your insurer checks before paying you back.

Start with a Hausarzt: how German doctor care is organised

Germany expects you to have a family doctor. Section 76(3) SGB V says it plainly: the insured person chooses a Hausarzt. The same section gives statutory patients free choice among doctors admitted to statutory care, asks them not to switch doctors within a calendar quarter without good reason, and allows other doctors only in emergencies. Choosing a practice farther away than the nearest suitable one, without a compelling reason, can leave you paying the extra cost.

For an English speaker the family doctor is worth more than the rules suggest. A GP who speaks English becomes your translator into the rest of the system: they write the referrals, read the specialist letters, and issue the sick notes an employer or client may ask for. Spending effort on finding one English-speaking GP pays back every time you need anyone else.

Bring your insurance card to every visit if you are statutory, or your policy details if you are private. Registering with one practice keeps your referrals, test results and specialist letters in a single file, which matters more when the letters are in German.

The Überweisung: when you need a referral to a specialist doctor in Germany

A referral, the Überweisung, is a form your GP issues to send you to a specialist. Its most important job for a newcomer is unlocking the appointment service. Under Section 75(1a) SGB V, the Terminservice will only find you a specialist appointment if you hold a referral, and according to the KBV that referral must carry a Vermittlungscode, a booking code your doctor adds when the appointment is urgent.

Some specialists need no referral for that route. The law exempts ophthalmologists and gynaecologists, and the KBV's description of the 116117 service adds family doctors, paediatricians and the first psychotherapy consultation. For those you can book directly through the Terminservice or ring the practice yourself.

The practical sequence for anything beyond a cold is short. See your English-speaking GP, ask for a referral with a Vermittlungscode if the problem is urgent, then either call the specialist or let 116117 find the slot.

Getting a doctor's appointment in Germany through the 116117 Terminservice

Long waits for specialists are the complaint every newcomer hears, and the law has a direct answer for statutory patients. Section 75(1a) SGB V obliges the KVs to run appointment service points reachable 24 hours a day on a single national number, and sets the maximum: the wait for an appointment they arrange must not exceed four weeks. For acute psychotherapy the limit is two weeks. The distance to the practice they offer must be reasonable.

You book by phone on 116117, online at 116117-termine.de, or through the 116117 app. The catch for an English speaker is the same as before: the phone service runs in German. The online booking is the easier route, since a form can be translated in the browser and a phone call cannot.

What you give up is choice. The law guarantees the waiting time and a reasonable distance, not a particular doctor, and nothing in it promises a doctor who speaks English. If language matters more than speed, use the Arztsuche to find an English-speaking practice and book it yourself.

Looking for a named practice rather than a slot? Our directory of English-speaking doctors in Germany groups every entry by city and links each one to the official list it came from.

A doctor at night or on the weekend: 116117 versus 112

Germany separates urgent care into two numbers, and mixing them up wastes time. 116117 is the medical on-call service, the ärztlicher Bereitschaftsdienst. It covers evenings, nights, weekends and public holidays, for problems you would normally take to a GP but which cannot wait until the next day. It directs you to the nearest on-call practice and arranges home visits when needed. The call is free with a German phone contract.

112 is the emergency number for the fire service and ambulance. The 116117 English page draws the line clearly: if a situation is life-threatening or there is a risk of permanent injury, call 112. The on-call service is for when practices are closed, you need help today, and the situation is not life-threatening.

The line is busy and it triages by software. A study by the Central Research Institute of Ambulatory Health Care (Zi), published in Deutsches Ärzteblatt International in January 2026, counted 6,067,734 telephone assessments and 897,232 online self-assessments on 116117 between 2022 and 2024. Because the phone line is in German, the online on-call practice finder on 116117.de, searched by postcode, is the better first step for an English speaker who can still walk.

Health insurance for the German freelance visa

Germany has no dedicated digital nomad visa. The long-stay route for most remote workers is the residence permit for freelance work under Section 21(5) of the Residence Act, which can be issued for up to three years. Our nomad visa finder lists it as the Freiberufler visa, and the rule on insurance is where applications most often come undone.

The Berlin immigration office's page for the freelance permit sets the standard. You must be insured in Germany, either in the statutory system or in a comparable private policy, and it states that foreign health insurance is generally not sufficient. Statutory members show a current confirmation from their fund with the contribution due, or proof of registration with the Künstlersozialkasse, the social fund for self-employed artists and publicists. Private members show their insurer's certificate under Section 257(2a) SGB V, which states the scope and cost of the cover.

That certificate is the practical filter. Section 257(2a) describes insurers that, among other conditions, offer the basic Basistarif and operate on the life-insurance model. Together with Berlin's statement that foreign insurance is generally not enough, that rules out most travel policies. Applicants over 45 face a second check: since 1 July 2025 Berlin expects either a projected pension of $1,839 a month for at least 12 years or assets of $264,782 by age 67. The permit itself costs $64 when issued as a label.

Get the insurance sorted before the appointment, not after. It is also what turns you from a cash patient into someone who can use the system described above. Our Dresden guide walks through the same freelance route for a cheaper German city.

EU citizens and the European Health Insurance Card at a German doctor

If you are insured in another EU or EEA country, or in Switzerland, the European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) covers treatment in Germany that cannot wait until you get home. The EU's official guidance says you have the same rights to care as people insured in the country you are staying in. In Germany that means the doctors admitted to statutory care, the same practices the 116117 search lists.

Three limits matter. The EHIC does not cover private healthcare, so a private-only practice will bill you under the GOÄ. It does not cover planned treatment that you travel for. And it is built for temporary stays: the EU's guidance says people who move their residence should arrange cover in their new country. An EU citizen who settles in Berlin therefore falls under the German insurance duty in Section 193 VVG like anyone else.

Prescriptions and the Apotheke after your doctor visit

Prescription medicines come only from a pharmacy, the Apotheke. Since 1 January 2024 the electronic prescription, the e-Rezept, has been mandatory for prescription drugs in the statutory system, according to the Federal Ministry of Health. You redeem it with your insurance card at the pharmacy counter, through the official E-Rezept app, or with a paper printout the practice gives you.

Statutory patients aged 18 and over pay a co-payment on each item: under Section 61 SGB V it is 10% of the price, at least $5.70 and at most $11.40, and never more than the medicine itself costs. Section 62 caps your total co-payments at 2% of gross annual income, or 1% for the chronically ill in ongoing treatment. A private patient pays under the terms of their own policy instead.

At night and on Sundays, pharmacies take turns on emergency duty. The pharmacists' portal aponet.de finds the nearest duty pharmacy by postcode, and those pharmacies may add an emergency fee of $2.85 between 8 pm and 6 am on weekdays and all day on Sundays and holidays.

How to check a doctor in Germany is properly registered

The German Medical Association (Bundesärztekammer) says that the state medical chambers and the KVs are the only institutions holding valid information about the qualifications of doctors working in Germany. It runs no national register of its own; its doctor search points you to the regional searches instead. For a statutory practice, the 116117 listing already tells you the doctor is admitted to statutory care and in which specialty.

Do not expect the chamber to recommend anyone. The Ärztekammer Berlin states that it represents all Berlin doctors and therefore cannot single out some of them. It also warns that doctor-rating portals reflect the impressions of a selection of patients, rarely measure clinical skill, and are not secure against manipulation. That is the same reason our directory records only where a language claim comes from, never a verdict on quality.

Seeing an English-speaking doctor in Berlin

Our ratings give Berlin 9 out of 10 for English, one of its strongest scores, next to a 3 for cost. A high English score describes daily life in the city, not the paperwork: insurance letters, referral forms and the 116117 phone line stay in German whatever the practice speaks.

The workable Berlin method combines the pieces above. Run the 116117 English search with a Berlin postcode near where you live, since Section 76 lets the insurer charge extra for a practice farther than necessary without a compelling reason. Ring two or three practices from the results, and register with the one that answers in English. Then keep 116117's online on-call finder bookmarked for evenings, and 112 for anything that cannot wait.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find an English-speaking doctor in Germany?

Use the official 116117 Arztsuche. Its English page at 116117.de links a search already filtered for practices that report English, across 14 specialisations. Enter a town or postcode, pick a practice near you, and phone to confirm. In Hamburg, our directory mirrors the KV Hamburg language data for 3,580 doctors.

Is the 116117 doctor service in Germany available in English?

The phone line is not. The 116117 English page states that the telephone service is only available in German. The website is usable in English: it has an English information page, an English-filtered doctor search, and an online finder for on-call practices that works by postcode.

How much does a doctor visit cost in Germany for a private patient?

Under the GOÄ fee schedule, a consultation plus a symptom-focused examination costs $24.45 at the standard 2.3 multiplier and $37.22 at the 3.5 maximum. Tests and procedures are billed separately. Statutory patients pay nothing at the practice; they show their insurance card.

Do I need a referral to see a specialist doctor in Germany?

To get a specialist appointment through the 116117 Terminservice, yes: Section 75 SGB V requires a referral, and the KBV says it must carry a Vermittlungscode. Ophthalmologists, gynaecologists, paediatricians, family doctors and a first psychotherapy consultation are exempt from that requirement.

What health insurance do I need for the German freelance visa?

Berlin's immigration office requires insurance in Germany: statutory cover, or a comparable private policy proved by the insurer's certificate under Section 257(2a) SGB V. It states that foreign health insurance is generally not sufficient, so a travel or expat policy usually will not pass.

Can EU citizens use the EHIC at a doctor in Germany?

Yes, for treatment that cannot wait until you return home, at doctors in the statutory system, on the same terms as locally insured patients. The card does not cover private practices or planned treatment, and it is meant for temporary stays. Once you live in Germany, you need German cover.

How do I find an English-speaking doctor in Berlin?

Run the 116117 English search with your Berlin postcode, then ring a few practices near you and register with one that answers in English. Our Berlin doctors page adds entries from embassy and consular lists. The 116117 phone line itself runs only in German.

Which number do I call for a doctor at night in Germany?

Call 116117 for the on-call medical service when practices are closed and the problem cannot wait but is not life-threatening. It sends you to an on-call practice or arranges a home visit. Call 112 for life-threatening situations or a risk of permanent injury. The 116117 line runs in German.
