Budapest: Europe's Most Underrated Nomad Destination

Yannick Schroth January 2026 8 min read

Contents

  2. Cost of Living
  3. Best Districts
  4. Coworking Spaces
  5. Thermal Bath Culture
  6. Ruin Bars & Nightlife
  7. Final Verdict

Picture this: it is January, snow is settling on the lampposts, and you are shoulder-deep in 38-degree thermal water in an outdoor pool, your standup call finished an hour ago, a coffee that cost two euros going cold on the ledge beside you. That is a Tuesday in Budapest. The Hungarian capital runs at roughly half the cost of Lisbon or Barcelona, carries fiber faster than most of Western Europe, and hides a 2,000-year bathing habit under its Art Nouveau facades.

I was born here. Then I left, chasing the laptop life through Southeast Asia, Latin America, and a string of pricey Western European cities, before it dawned on me that the place I grew up quietly outscored most of them. This guide is for nomads tired of the usual shortlist, the ones willing to look at Central Europe and see what everyone else keeps overlooking.

Why Budapest?

You know the feeling. You loved Lisbon or Barcelona, then you saw the rent. Budapest is the city that gives you the second part without the wince. It straddles the Danube, the hilly Buda side on one bank and flat, busy Pest on the other, and the architecture stops you mid-stride: Ottoman bath houses, century-old apartment blocks with iron balconies, a parliament that looks borrowed from a fairy tale. The food punches at the weight of cities twice its size.

Hungary is in the EU, so you get Schengen access and infrastructure that actually works. Ferenc Liszt airport connects to nearly every major European city, and with Wizz Air and Ryanair based nearby, the fares stay almost comically low. Vienna, Prague, and Krakow are each a one-to-two hour train ride or a 30-euro flight, close enough that a spontaneous weekend abroad stops feeling like an event. No surprise it keeps landing on our roundup of the best European cities for nomads.

The internet is the quiet brag here. Hungary sits in the global top 15 for broadband speed, and most apartments ship with fiber running 300 to 1000 Mbps straight out of the wall. Cafes and coworking floors hold up under back-to-back calls. For somewhere this cheap, the bandwidth feels almost out of place, like finding a sports engine in an old sedan.

Cost of Living in HUF and USD/EUR

This is where the city earns its keep. As I write, a euro buys you roughly 380-400 HUF and a dollar around 360-380 HUF. If your salary lands in pounds, euros, or dollars, those exchange rates quietly turn ordinary days into bargains. The math works in your favor before you have even ordered a coffee.

Accommodation

A one-bedroom in a central Pest district (V, VI, or VII) runs 180,000-280,000 HUF/month (roughly 450-700 EUR or 480-750 USD) short-term. Slide one district out to IX or XIII, still very walkable, still very livable, and you drop to 140,000-200,000 HUF/month (350-500 EUR). Sign for six months or more and landlords routinely shave another 15-20% off, because a tenant who stays beats a calendar of turnover.

For the first week or two, do not lock yourself into anything. Furnished short-term rentals on Flatio or local Facebook groups (search "Budapest Apartments for Rent") buy you a base while you walk the streets and view places in person. The good apartments here are found on foot, not on a map.

Food & Coffee

A two-course lunch with a drink at a local "etterem" lands at 2,500-4,000 HUF (6-10 EUR), the kind of midday menu that ends with you loosening your belt. A proper third-wave coffee is 800-1,200 HUF (2-3 EUR). A week of groceries from Lidl, Aldi, or the Great Market Hall averages 15,000-25,000 HUF (40-65 EUR). Cook most nights, eat out a few, and the whole month of food sits around 100,000-150,000 HUF (250-380 EUR).

Transport

The BKK network (metro, tram, bus) goes everywhere and shows up on time. A monthly pass is 9,500 HUF (about 25 EUR), which would barely cover a few days in London or Paris. The old yellow trams along the river earn their fame: they rattle, they screech, and they are still one of the prettiest commutes in Europe. Need a door-to-door ride? Bolt, the local Uber, runs most cross-town trips at 1,500-3,500 HUF (4-9 EUR).

Bottom line: Budget 300,000-450,000 HUF per month (750-1,150 EUR / 800-1,200 USD) and you live well. That is about half of what the same life costs in Lisbon, and right in line with Tbilisi. The difference shows up at the end of the month, in money you did not have to spend.

Best Districts to Stay In

On paper Budapest splits into 23 districts (kerulet). As a nomad you will care about maybe five of them. Here is how the ones that matter actually feel on the ground:

District VII (Erzsebetvaros / Jewish Quarter): The beating heart of nomad Budapest, where the cafes, coworking floors, ruin bars, and restaurants pile up block after block. Everything is within walking distance, and there is always one more courtyard to wander into. The catch is the noise. On weekend nights the ruin bar crowds spill into the streets, and your apartment windows will know it. Pick VII if you draw energy from a city that refuses to sit still.

District V (Belvaros / Downtown): The postcard version of the city, all Parliament domes, the Chain Bridge, and that long sweep of Danube embankment at dusk. It is more polished and more touristy than VII, but the evenings settle down sooner. You pay more in rent, and in return you wake up to some of the best architecture in the country. Choose V if you want elegance and a river view from your morning coffee.

District IX (Ferencvaros): Every city has its up-and-coming district. Budapest's is already good. The Central Market Hall anchors it, the food along Raday utca keeps getting better, and young professionals have quietly moved in. Rents undercut V and VII, and it still feels like a place where locals, not tour groups, set the pace.

District XI (Buda side): Cross the river and the whole tempo changes. Buda is hilly, green, residential, the kind of place where you hear birds before traffic. Gellert Hill and the Buda Hills put real trails minutes from your door, yet a single tram drops you back into Pest's noise whenever you want it. The Gellert Baths sit right here. Choose XI if calm and open air matter more than being in the thick of things.

District XIII: The one people forget to mention. This northern stretch of Pest hugs the Danube and balances cheap rent, local character, and easy transit better than its reputation suggests. Margaret Island, the city's green lung, is a five-minute stroll away. The cafe scene is thin but thickening, and the trams reach everything.

Coworking Spaces

A few years ago the coworking options here were thin. Not anymore. Finding a good desk is easy now, and the monthly rate will read like a typo if you are coming from London or Amsterdam.

  • Loffice: One of Budapest's original coworking spaces, located near the Danube in District V. Beautiful design, solid community, and regular events. Around 60,000 HUF/month (150 EUR) for a flexible desk.
  • Kaptar: Located in the heart of District VII, Kaptar ("Beehive" in Hungarian) is beloved for its warm community and affordable pricing. Day passes around 4,000 HUF (10 EUR), monthly from 50,000 HUF (125 EUR).
  • Impact Hub Budapest: Part of the global Impact Hub network, this space attracts social entrepreneurs and mission-driven professionals. Great for networking. Located in District VIII, near the National Museum.
  • Prezi House: Run by the team behind the presentation software Prezi (which was founded in Budapest), this space has a strong tech community feel. Premium facilities with a startup energy.

Most spaces sell day passes at 3,000-6,000 HUF (8-15 EUR), so you can test a room before you commit a month to it. And when you would rather not commit at all, the cafes carry you. Espresso Embassy, Madal, and Fekete pour serious coffee and will let you nurse one flat white through three hours of deep work without a sideways glance. Curious how that compares somewhere hotter? The Thermal Bath Culture

This is the part no other European nomad city can match. Budapest sits on more than 120 natural hot springs, and people have been soaking in them since the Romans, then the Ottomans, laid the first stones. Close the laptop after a brutal day, walk ten minutes, and lower yourself into 38-degree water inside a bathhouse that has been steaming since before your country had its current borders. Your shoulders drop. The deadline stops mattering quite so much.

Szechenyi Baths: The biggest medicinal bath complex in Europe and the one on every postcard. It sits in City Park on the Pest side, a vast lemon-yellow neo-Baroque palace wrapped around 18 pools, several of them open-air and open all year. So yes, you can sit chest-deep in steaming water while snow lands on your hair and dissolves. Entry runs 8,000-10,000 HUF (20-25 EUR), depending on whether you take a locker or a cabin.

Gellert Baths: The prettiest of them all. The Art Nouveau interior, mosaic tiles, stained glass, columns the color of sea glass, looks like a set built for a Wes Anderson film. You will find it on the Buda side at the foot of Gellert Hill. It costs a touch more than Szechenyi, and the ceiling alone justifies the difference.

Rudas Baths: An Ottoman bathhouse from the 16th century, capped with a rooftop pool that floats you out over the Danube with the whole city laid out below. Come Friday or Saturday night, when it stays open until 4am, and the soak turns into something closer to a ritual you will not find anywhere else.

"I have worked remotely from 30+ countries, and Budapest is the only city where my post-work routine involves soaking in thermal water inside a building older than most nations. It never gets old."

Plenty of nomads pencil the baths into their week the way other people block out the gym. A 10-visit pass at Szechenyi is about 30,000 HUF (75 EUR), which works out to 3,000 HUF a soak. Hard to think of a cheaper way to reset your nervous system on a Wednesday.

Ruin Bars & Nightlife

The ruin bar was born here, in the early 2000s, when someone looked at a derelict Jewish Quarter courtyard and decided to serve beer in it instead of demolishing it. The idea has since been cloned across the planet, and the copies never quite land. The originals are still the real thing: crumbling walls hung with bicycles and bathtubs, mismatched chairs no two of which match, graffiti layered like sediment, an atmosphere that swings between art squat and someone's wild living room.

Szimpla Kert started it all and is now the most famous ruin bar anywhere. Touristy, sure. Still a good night, especially midweek when the crowds thin and you can actually hear the person across the table. Come Sunday morning and the same courtyard turns into a farmers market, jars of honey where the beer taps were, which is the gentler way in if a 2am crush is not your scene.

Past Szimpla, the city keeps going. Instant-Fogas sprawls across rooms and floors until you lose your friends in it, Ellato Kert keeps things scruffier, and Mazel Tov dresses the formula up with Middle Eastern plates and a glass roof. A beer runs 800-1,200 HUF (2-3 EUR), and a cocktail rarely tops 3,000 HUF (7-8 EUR), so a full night out costs what one round does back home.

For nomads, these places are less about the drinking than the bumping-into. This is where you meet the other remote workers, trade notes on which city to try next, and somehow end up in a stranger's District VII flat at 2am on a Tuesday, talking about everything. Just remember the standup call waiting for you in the morning. The morning always wins eventually.

Final Verdict

Budapest belongs to that short list of cities that hand you almost everything at a price that feels like a glitch. Fast internet. Food that fills you up for pocket change. Streets worth getting lost in. Bath culture nobody can rival, nightlife that became an export, and a monthly total low enough that you put money away while living better than you did at home. Most cities make you trade one of those for another. This one mostly does not.

Perfect? No. Hungarian is one of the hardest languages on the planet, so park any dream of picking it up over a few months. The winters are long, cold, and grey, and if you run on sunlight they will test you. Visa and residency paperwork can grind your patience down. Younger locals speak good English, but step into an older neighborhood or a government office and the language wall goes back up. For anyone weighing visa options across Europe, Hungary's White Card is newer and clunkier than Portugal's D8, though it gets a little smoother each year.

Best for: Budget-minded nomads who want a European base with real culture behind it, not just a desk and a flat white. People who would take a thermal pool over a beach any day. Anyone hunting for a city with depth that the remote-work wave has not yet washed flat.

Not ideal for: Sun-chasers who need warmth all year round (point yourself at Lisbon or Bali instead). Nomads who lean on English for every single interaction. And anyone whose stomach cannot make peace with a serious paprika habit.

Frequently Asked Questions About Living in Budapest

How much does it cost to live in Budapest as a digital nomad?

Budapest is one of Europe's most affordable nomad bases, with a cost of living roughly half that of Western European capitals. Budget around 300,000 to 450,000 HUF per month (750 to 1,150 EUR) for a comfortable lifestyle covering a central apartment, food, transport, and going out.

How much is rent for an apartment in Budapest?

A one-bedroom apartment in a central Pest district (V, VI, or VII) runs about 180,000 to 280,000 HUF a month. Slightly less central neighborhoods like IX or XIII are cheaper, and long-term leases of six months or more often drop prices by another 15 to 20 percent.

Which Budapest districts are best for digital nomads?

District VII, the Jewish Quarter, is the heart of nomad Budapest, packed with cafes, coworking, and ruin bars. District V offers river views and elegance, District IX is up-and-coming and affordable, and the Buda side around District XI is calmer and greener. See the full Budapest city profile.

Is the internet fast enough for remote work in Budapest?

Yes. Hungary consistently ranks among the top 15 countries globally for broadband speed, and most Budapest apartments come with fiber offering 300 to 1000 Mbps. Cafes and coworking spaces rarely disappoint, so video calls and heavy uploads are not a problem for remote workers based here.

Are there good coworking spaces in Budapest?

Plenty, and at a fraction of Western European prices. Established spaces like Loffice in District V and Kaptar in District VII offer flexible desks from around 50,000 HUF a month, with day passes near 3,000 to 6,000 HUF. Laptop-friendly cafes fill the gaps nicely.

What makes Budapest's thermal baths worth it for nomads?

Budapest sits on over 120 natural hot springs, with a bathing culture dating to Roman and Ottoman times. Szechenyi is the largest, Gellert the most beautiful, and Rudas has a Danube-view rooftop pool. Many nomads build weekly bath visits into their routine like gym sessions.

Is Budapest a good city for digital nomads overall?

Budapest suits budget-conscious nomads who want genuine European culture, thermal baths over beaches, and a city with real depth that is not yet overrun. It is less ideal if you need year-round sun or rely heavily on English everywhere. Use our Nomad Taste Wheel to compare it against other cities.
