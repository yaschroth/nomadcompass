Budapest Digital Nomad Guide: Buda, Pest and the Danube

Yannick Schroth January 2025 12 min read

Open a map of Budapest and you see two cities facing each other across a river. On the west bank, Buda climbs straight from the water onto Castle Hill and Gellért Hill, and behind them wooded hills rise to János Hill, at 527 metres the highest point in the capital. On the east bank, Pest runs out flat across a sand plain at the edge of the Great Hungarian Plain, a grid of boulevards, apartment blocks and courtyards that is far larger than Buda. Between them the Danube narrows to 230 metres.

They were separate towns until 1873, when Buda, Óbuda and Pest merged. The Chain Bridge, opened in 1849 as the first permanent crossing, had already tied them together, and today a string of bridges does. The river still organises the city. Budapest's 23 districts are numbered in semicircles: District I is the Castle Quarter in Buda, the count jumps the river to District V in the heart of Pest, then loops back through Pest to Buda again.

For anyone moving here, the map is the first decision. Pest is flat, dense and walkable, and it holds most of the cafés, ruin bars, coworking desks and flats you will be offered. Buda is steeper, greener and quieter, with Ottoman baths at the foot of its hills and forest a tram ride away. Trams run along both banks and the metro runs under the river, so crossing is easy; the question is which bank you want to wake up on.

Either way, Budapest is a European capital at a Central European price: our one-person budget with a central flat comes to about $1,550 a month. It suits EU citizens, who can simply move, and non-EU remote workers with a salary high enough for Hungary's White Card. Skip it if you need winter sun, or if you are a non-EU freelancer with no employer or company behind you.

Can you stay, and on what?

Hungary is in Schengen, so an American, British, Canadian or Australian passport gets 90 days in any 180, shared with every other Schengen country. Since 10 April 2026 the Entry/Exit System records your face, fingerprints and every crossing in place of passport stamps, so the count is exact.

For longer, Hungary has something most of its neighbours lack: a permit written for remote workers. The White Card asks for a net income of about $3,450 a month for the six months before you arrive and for as long as you stay. The floor is a fixed sum set in euros rather than a multiple of a Hungarian wage, so its dollar value moves only with the exchange rate.

Read the eligibility closely. You must either work for an employer outside Hungary or own a share of a profitable company abroad, and you may not earn anything in Hungary or hold a share in a Hungarian company. A freelancer invoicing clients in their own name fits neither box neatly. The card lasts a year, can be extended once for a second, and family members cannot join you on it.

You apply at a Hungarian consulate or, on a visa-free passport, from inside Hungary through the Enter Hungary portal, and the decision is due within 30 days. Our digital nomad visa comparison sets it against the other European schemes, and our guide to Portugal's nomad visa covers the better-known alternative.

EU and EEA citizens need no permit. If you stay beyond three months, register your address with the immigration office by the 93rd day after arrival; the fee is about $3, and the certificate never expires.

When does Hungary tax you?

Hungary taxes its residents on their worldwide income, and residence turns on where your only permanent home is before it turns on days. If your only home is a Budapest flat, you are resident. If you keep homes in two countries, the test moves to your centre of vital interests, and only if that settles nothing does the count of 183 days in a calendar year decide.

EU citizens with a registration certificate become resident once they spend 183 days here in a year. The practical lesson for a White Card holder: keep the home abroad if you want to stay outside Hungary's net, and count the days anyway.

Pest: the flat bank

Most of what a remote worker needs is in Pest, in the inner districts inside and around the Grand Boulevard, the tram-lined semicircle that runs from Margaret Bridge to Petőfi Bridge and is usually taken as the edge of the centre. Our figures put a one-bedroom in the centre at $880 a month, and one outside it at about $670.

District V: Parliament and the embankment

Belváros-Lipótváros is the Pest riverfront: the Parliament, the third-largest parliament building in the world, and the Danube promenade facing Castle Hill. Tram 2 runs along the embankment from Jászai Mari tér past the Parliament and the Chain Bridge, one of the finest commutes in any city.

District VI: Andrássy út and the Opera

Terézváros is built around Andrássy út, the 2.5 kilometre tree-lined avenue from the centre to Heroes' Square and City Park, listed by UNESCO with the riverbanks and the Castle Quarter. The Opera is on it, and beneath it runs the M1, opened in 1896 and the oldest underground railway in continental Europe.

District VII: the old Jewish Quarter

Erzsébetváros is where the ruin bar was born. Szimpla Kert opened in 2002 and now fills a former stove factory on Kazinczy utca with bars, film screenings and live music, and on Sunday mornings it turns into a farmers' market. A few streets away stands the Dohány Street Synagogue, the largest in Europe. Nights on the bar streets are loud, so take a flat facing a courtyard, not the street.

District IX: the market and the river

Ferencváros begins at the Pest end of Liberty Bridge, where the Great Market Hall, opened in 1897, sells paprika, sausage, cheese and vegetables from more than 180 stalls, every day but Sunday. It is the place to shop if you cook.

District XIII: facing Margaret Island

North of the Parliament, Újlipótváros has some of the best modernist apartment houses in Hungary, the finest of them facing the river around Szent István Park. Its back garden is Margaret Island, a park in the middle of the Danube, where a rubberised running track of about 5.3 kilometres loops past medieval ruins and a swimming complex. If you run, this is the district to live in.

Buda: the hilly bank

Cross any bridge westwards and the tempo drops. A flat in Buda trades nightlife for gardens, a view and trails a short ride from the door.

District I: the Castle Quarter

On the ridge above the river, the Castle Quarter keeps its medieval and Baroque streets, Matthias Church and the white towers of Fisherman's Bastion, looking across the water at the Parliament. A funicular climbs to it from the Chain Bridge.

District XI: Gellért Hill and the baths

Újbuda wraps around Gellért Hill, and at its foot, squeezed between hill and river, is Rudas, a Turkish bath from the Ottoman 16th century whose octagonal pool sits under a 10 metre dome. The Art Nouveau Gellért Baths, at the Buda end of Liberty Bridge, closed on 1 October 2025 for a renovation due to finish in 2028, so do not plan a winter around them.

Districts II and XII: the Buda Hills

Behind the castle, the hills climb into protected forest. From Városmajor the cogwheel railway, tram 60 on the network, hauls itself up to Széchenyi-hegy, where the Children's Railway, 11.2 kilometres of narrow gauge staffed by schoolchildren aged 10 to 14, winds through the woods towards Hűvösvölgy. It passes János Hill, where a lookout tower tops the city's highest point. Choose these districts if trails matter more to you than bars.

A working day between the banks

The river is also your commute. A monthly pass, about $28, covers the metro, trams and buses, and the lines follow the map: tram 2 on the Pest bank, the metro under the river, and trams 4 and 6 round the Grand Boulevard, where tram 6 is the only tram line that runs all night.

Say you live in District VI. In the morning you could walk to KAPTÁR on Révay köz, where a weekday desk costs about $178 a month before VAT, or about $21 for a single day. Lunch could be a counter in the Great Market Hall or a small restaurant near the Boulevard, where an inexpensive meal averages about $14.

At five, the city's oldest habit takes over. Budapest sits on about 125 thermal springs, and Széchenyi, a neo-Baroque palace in City Park, is the largest medicinal bath in Europe, with outdoor pools kept at up to 38°C. A weekday ticket costs about $42, or $33 if you arrive before nine.

On Friday and Saturday nights Rudas opens from 10pm to 3am, with the pools, the Turkish bath and a bar on the roof above the Danube. If you want one ritual that no other European base can give you, it is that one.

When should you go?

Budapest has real seasons. Winter, from November into early March, is cold and short of sunshine, with January highs under 5°C, and it is when the thermal pools make the most sense. Spring swings quickly from chilly to pleasant, and April days are often warm with cool evenings.

Summer runs from May to mid-September and is warm to hot, with July highs near 29°C and sudden heavy showers in May and June. In August the Sziget festival takes over Óbuda Island, this year from 11 to 15 August.

Autumn, from mid-September to late October, brings little rain and long sunny days, and it is the best time to arrive. May and early June run it close.

If you get sick

With a European Health Insurance Card you are treated on the same terms as Hungarians, but only by doctors and hospitals under contract to NEAK, the national health fund, so ask before you are seen. From outside the EU you need insurance; the White Card requires proof of it anyway.

In an emergency, call 112. For routine care in English or another language, our list of doctors in Budapest by the language they speak shows the source for each one.

Budapest, Vienna or Prague?

Of the three Central European capitals, Budapest is the cheapest. On our one-person budgets, Prague costs about $420 a month more and Vienna about $950 more. For a non-EU remote worker, Budapest also has the White Card, while all three draw on the same Schengen allowance for visitors.

So pick Budapest if you want a big European city with its baths, its river and its courtyards, and can live with grey winters and a language almost nobody learns in a season. Choose your bank first: Pest for the life on the street, Buda for the hills. Arrive in autumn or late spring, rent for a month before signing anything longer, and if you need the White Card, check the employment condition before you check the income. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Hungary have a digital nomad visa?

Yes, the White Card. It needs a net income of about $3,450 a month, a fixed sum set in euros, from an employer or a profitable company abroad, lasts one year and can be extended once. Family members cannot join on it.

How much does it cost to live in Budapest as a digital nomad?

About $1,550 a month for one person with a central one-bedroom, on our Numbeo-based budget. A central one-bedroom averages $880, one outside the centre $670, and other living costs about $670.

Should I live in Buda or Pest?

Pest, for most remote workers: it is flat and dense, with the cafés, coworking spaces and ruin bars in Districts V, VI and VII. Buda is hillier, greener and quieter, with the Rudas baths and the Buda Hills.

Are the Gellért Baths open?

No. The Gellért Baths closed on 1 October 2025 for renovation, with reopening planned for 2028. Széchenyi in City Park and Rudas at the foot of Gellért Hill are open.

How long can I stay in Budapest without a visa?

On a US, UK, Canadian or Australian passport, 90 days in any 180, shared with the rest of Schengen. EU and EEA citizens can stay freely and must register their address by the 93rd day.

When is the best time to work from Budapest?

Mid-September to late October, when rain is scarce and days are long and sunny, or May and early June. January highs stay under 5°C, and July highs reach about 29°C.
