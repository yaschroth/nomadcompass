Santa Teresa Digital Nomad Guide: Costs and Wifi

Santa Teresa is a surf town with one road, and that road is not paved. It scores 9 for nature in our index and 4 for community is the number people quote at you, but the community score is the one that undersells it: the scene here is small, not thin. What actually decides whether you can work from Santa Teresa is the wifi, and the honest answer is that it is fine until it rains.

Budget $1,200 to $2,200 a month. This is not a cheap Central American beach town and has not been one for years. What follows is what the money buys, where the connection holds, and the season that decides both.

Cost of living in Santa Teresa: the expensive end of Costa Rica

Our Santa Teresa city page puts a comfortable month at $1,400, with a realistic range of $1,200 to $2,200. That is more than Tamarindo, more than most of the Nicoya Peninsula, and roughly double what the same lifestyle costs inland.

The reason is the road. Everything arrives over a rough unpaved stretch that punishes vehicles and adds a delivery premium to groceries, building materials and imports. A supermarket basket here costs noticeably more than the same basket in Nicoya town, an hour inland. Prices did not creep up; they climbed hard through the post-2020 remote-work boom and have not come back down.

You can cut it. A room in a shared house, cooking most nights, and eating at sodas, the small family-run local restaurants, will hold a month near the bottom of that range. Beach clubs and restaurant dinners are what take you to the top of it.

Wifi in Santa Teresa: good enough, until the rain

We rate wifi 6 out of 10, which is decent for a beach town and not the same as reliable. Everyday work, email and most video calls are fine. What is not guaranteed is uptime during heavy rain, a power blip or peak evening demand, and in green season all three happen in the same week.

Plan for it rather than hope. Carry a local SIM with a real data allowance so you can hotspot the moment the house connection wobbles. Nomads who treat mobile data as their backup rather than their emergency have a much calmer time here.

Do not schedule a board presentation from a jungle rental in October. That is not pessimism, it is the same advice anyone who has worked a green season here will give you.

Where to work in Santa Teresa

Three places carry the working day, and they are genuinely different from each other.

Nomad Coworking Santa Teresa

In Playa Carmen, and the one to book if the work matters. A dedicated coworking space built for remote workers: stable wifi, air conditioning, and desks meant for a focused day rather than a laptop balanced on a lunch table.

Selina Santa Teresa

A hostel and coworking hub on Santa Teresa Beach with dedicated work areas. The trade is the usual one: more social, more interruption. If your first problem is meeting people rather than shipping work, start here.

Product C

An airy seafood cafe and market that doubles as a laptop spot, with coffee and good food. Fine for a few hours and a change of scene. Not where you put a deadline.

Eating in Santa Teresa: two price worlds

Food scores 7 out of 10 here, which is high for a town of this size and comes from the split. There is a genuine restaurant scene, and there is the local one, and the gap between the two bills is wide.

The Bakery is the long-standing breakfast and coffee institution. Product C does seafood and doubles as a market. Katana Sushi and Burger Rancho cover the evening end. These are the places visitors name, and they are priced for visitors.

The other world is the soda: a small family kitchen serving a casado, rice, beans, plantain, salad and a protein, for a fraction of the restaurant price. Groceries at the local pulperia and the weekly farmers market beat the supermarket on produce by a distance. Cooking three or four nights a week is the single biggest lever on your monthly total.

Where to stay in Santa Teresa: four stretches of one road

The town is a single coastal road, and the names people use are stretches of it rather than districts.

Playa Carmen is the southern end and the practical one: the crossroads, the supermarket, the coworking. Santa Teresa Beach is the middle and the busiest, most restaurants and most nightlife. Playa Hermosa sits north, quieter and better for beginner surf. Malpais is south of Carmen, the fishing-village end, calmest of the four.

Distance matters more than it looks on a map, because the road is slow. Living in Hermosa and working in Carmen is a daily commitment, not a stroll.

Getting around Santa Teresa without a car

Most people rent a scooter or a quad. The road is dust in dry season and mud in green season, and neither is forgiving of inexperience. If you have never ridden, this is not the place to learn on your first day.

Walking works within a stretch. Between stretches it is a long, hot, dusty push. Taxis exist and are not cheap by local standards.

Surfing and the 9 out of 10 for nature

Nature is the highest score Santa Teresa carries and it is the honest reason to come, and a 9 puts it near the top of our nature and outdoors ranking. The waves break consistently along the whole beach, which is unusual: most surf towns have one good peak and a crowd on it. Here the swell spreads out.

Playa Hermosa is the gentler option for learning. The main beach is more consistent and more crowded. Sunsets face directly west along the sand, which is why every evening the whole town walks down to the water.

The wellness side is not decoration either. Yoga is woven through the town rather than bolted onto it, and it is one of the main ways people meet each other.

The community score is 4, and that is misleading

Four out of ten reads like a warning. In practice Santa Teresa is one of the easiest places we cover to meet people, because the scene is small and overlapping: the same faces turn up at the coworking, the surf break and the yoga class inside a week.

What the low score reflects is scale. There is no large permanent nomad population, no weekly meetup calendar, no critical mass of the kind Lisbon or Medellin has. If you want a professional network you can hire from, this is the wrong town. If you want to know people within a fortnight, it is an easy one.

Safety: the sea is the real risk

Safety scores 7 out of 10. Violent crime is not the daily concern here. Opportunistic theft is: unlocked rentals, bags left on the sand while you paddle out, scooters parked overnight on the road. Almost everything that happens to nomads in Santa Teresa is of that kind, and almost all of it is preventable.

The two things that actually put people in hospital are the road and the water. Scooter and quad accidents on loose dirt are common, and rip currents on this coast are strong. Learn to spot one before you swim out past your depth, and treat the helmet as non-negotiable rather than optional.

Medical care is basic in town and serious cases go to Nicoya or San Jose, hours away over that road. Travel insurance is not a formality on this stretch of coast.

Visas: 90 days, and the border run is getting harder

Most nationalities enter Costa Rica visa-free for up to 90 days, which comfortably covers a surf-and-work season. That is what nearly everyone here is using.

The traditional reset is a border run to Nicaragua or Panama. It still happens, and it is under more scrutiny than it used to be. Re-entry is at the officer's discretion, and people are turned back. Do not build a year around the assumption that it always works.

Costa Rica also runs a remote-worker visa with an income threshold for stays beyond the tourist window. Thresholds and paperwork move, so check the current requirement against an official source rather than a blog, including this one. Best time to go: dry season, green season, and the gap between them

December to April is dry season. Reliable sun, reliable internet, the most people, the highest prices, and accommodation that needs booking well ahead.

May to November is green season. Rain arrives in heavy afternoon bursts rather than all day, the landscape turns properly green, prices fall and the town empties out. September and October are the wettest and the quietest, and they are when the road and the connection are worst.

The sweet spot most long-stayers land on is the shoulder: May, or late November into early December. Prices have not peaked, the rain is manageable, and the town has people in it.

Who Santa Teresa is for

It suits someone whose work is asynchronous, who surfs or wants to learn, and who is content with a small social world rather than a large one. Take the morning in the water, work through the middle of the day, and be back on the beach for sunset.

It does not suit anyone with a call-heavy calendar in green season, anyone who needs a city, or anyone expecting Central American prices. On that last point the gap between expectation and reality is the single most common complaint about this town.

If the surf matters less than the budget, our Latin America ranking and the wider city index have cheaper options at the same latitude.

Frequently asked questions about Santa Teresa

Is the wifi in Santa Teresa good enough for remote work?

For everyday work and most video calls, yes. We rate it 6 out of 10. It is not reliable enough to promise flawless uptime in green season, when rain and power blips take connections down. A local SIM as a hotspot backup solves most of it.

How much does a month in Santa Teresa cost?

Between $1,200 and $2,200 for one person, with $1,400 a fair middle. Rent, restaurant meals and beach clubs drive the range. Shared housing and cooking at home hold it near the bottom.

Is Santa Teresa cheaper than the rest of Costa Rica?

No, it is one of the more expensive places in the country. Everything trucks in over a rough unpaved road, and years of steady price rises have pushed it well above inland towns an hour away.

Do I need a car or scooter in Santa Teresa?

Effectively yes, unless you stay within one stretch of the road. Most people rent a scooter or quad. The road is dusty in dry season and muddy in green season, so it is not a good place to ride for the first time.

How long can I stay in Santa Teresa on a tourist entry?

Commonly up to 90 days for most nationalities. Border runs to reset it are common but increasingly scrutinised and never guaranteed. For longer, Costa Rica has a remote-worker visa with an income requirement.

When is the best time to work from Santa Teresa?

December to April for reliability and sun, at peak prices. May or late November for the best balance of weather, cost and company. September and October are wettest, quietest and hardest on the internet.

Is Santa Teresa good for beginner surfers?

Yes. Playa Hermosa to the north is the gentler stretch and where most lessons happen. The main beach is more consistent and busier. Waves break along the length of the coast, so crowding is less of a problem than in most surf towns.
