The Rise of Coliving: Best Spaces for Digital Nomads in 2025

Yannick Schroth January 2025 10 min read

On my third night in a Lisbon coliving, fourteen strangers crammed around a kitchen table built for eight, passing a pan of someone's questionable paella and arguing about whether Bali had peaked. By dessert I had two coworking buddies and a hiking plan for Saturday. That table is the whole pitch for coliving: a private room when you want the door shut, and a built-in crowd when you don't. In 2025, there are more of these spaces, in more cities, than ever.

Over the past four years I've slept in more than a dozen of them across Southeast Asia, Europe, and Latin America. A few rewired how I travel. A handful were glorified hostels with a coworking room bolted on and a premium invoice to match. This guide separates the two: what coliving actually is, why it's spreading so fast, and which spaces earn the money.

What Is Coliving?

Strip away the marketing and coliving is shared housing engineered for community. You get a private bedroom, sometimes with an ensuite, and you share the kitchen, the living room, the coworking floor, and usually a terrace or pool. The operator handles utilities, cleaning, WiFi, and furniture. You arrive with a suitcase and a laptop, and by that evening you're living there.

Think of it as the flat share that grew up and got a job. What sets it apart from a normal rental is intent. The residents are vetted, the events are planned, and the whole space is laid out to push people into the same room at the same time. The good ones feel less like renting and more like being dropped into a small, temporary tribe.

For nomads, the part that matters is the work setup: real desks, fast fiber, a meeting room, sometimes a phone booth for calls. That folds your coworking membership into the rent and quietly saves you $150-300/month. Anyone who has tried to lead a client call from a hostel common room, dodging the 11am checkout crowd and a blender going off behind them, knows exactly why that's worth paying for.

Why Coliving Is Exploding

The money makes the trend obvious. The global coliving market was worth roughly $15 billion in 2023 and is on track to clear $30 billion by 2028. The interesting question is why, and the answer is four trends arriving at once:

Remote work is permanent now. Nobody is debating return-to-office anymore. Spotify, Airbnb, and Shopify went remote or hybrid and stayed there, and millions of people now wake up able to work from any time zone with WiFi. Coliving hands those people a landing pad in a strange city: no apartment hunt, no utility deposits, no Sunday afternoon lost to assembling a flat-pack desk.

Loneliness is the nomad's real problem. Ask anyone who has done this for a couple of years what actually breaks people, and they won't say visas or time zones. They'll say the empty studio at 8pm. Coliving aims straight at that. It drops you among twelve people in the exact same boat, so dinner is a group thing and Saturday already has a plan before you've unpacked.

Housing markets are broken. In cities like Lisbon, a top destination for digital nomads, short-let prices have gone vertical, and a furnished one-bed can swallow a month's budget before you've added the bills. Tally up rent, utilities, internet, cleaning, and a coworking desk separately, and a single coliving invoice often comes out ahead.

The product grew up. The first wave of coliving was duct tape and good intentions. Today's operators are funded and run like hotels with a community team. Brands like Selina and Outsite deliver professional management, a real events calendar, and quality you can roughly predict before you book.

Top Coliving Spaces Worldwide

These are the spaces I point friends toward, drawn from stays of my own and a lot of comparing notes with people I trust. The filter is simple: WiFi that holds up on a video call, a community that actually forms, and a price that matches what you get.

1. Selina

Selina is the closest thing coliving has to a chain you can navigate on autopilot, with properties scattered across Latin America, Europe, and Asia. Each one mixes hostel-grade social energy with private rooms and a proper coworking floor. The draw is predictability: the lobby in Lisbon, Medellín, and Bali rhymes, so you book without bracing for a surprise.

Best for: Nomads who move frequently and want a reliable brand they can hop between. Locations: 100+ worldwide. Price: $600-1,500/month depending on location and room type.

2. Outsite

Outsite plays at the premium end, in addresses you'd actually want a postcard from: Lisbon, Bali, the California coast. These are designed houses, not converted hotels, so they feel lived-in rather than checked-into. The desks are genuinely good, and the crowd skews a little older, a little further into their careers, more founders than fresh-out-of-college backpackers.

Best for: Established remote workers who want comfort and quality. Locations: 30+ across Europe, Americas, and Asia. Price: $1,000-2,000/month.

3. Sun and Co (Javea, Spain)

Sun and Co runs one house, and that's the point. It sits in a villa in Javea, a Spanish coastal town most nomads can't pronounce until they've been, and it has quietly built a cult following. Tight community, a real coworking room, lemon trees and a sea breeze doing half the work. The weekly community dinner and the skill-share nights are the kind of thing people still talk about a year after they leave.

Best for: Nomads seeking a slower pace and deep community bonds. Location: Javea, Spain. Price: $800-1,200/month.

4. Roam

Roam was one of the originals, and its Bali and Miami houses still hold up. The design is the kind you photograph without meaning to, the units are fully private with their own kitchenettes, and the coworking area would shame a few startup offices I've worked from. If the thing keeping you out of coliving is the fear of losing your own four walls, Roam is the version that keeps them.

Best for: Those who want privacy with optional community. Locations: Bali, Miami, Tokyo. Price: $1,500-2,500/month.

5. Tribe (Bali)

Tribe has become the default in Canggu, the corner of Bali where the nomad scene is about as dense as it gets anywhere on earth. It pairs affordable private rooms with a social calendar that rarely has a quiet night and a coworking space that ranks among the island's best. If Bali is on your route, also raid our guide to the best coworking spaces in Bali for places to work beyond the front door.

Best for: Budget-conscious nomads who want community in Bali. Location: Canggu, Bali. Price: $500-900/month.

6. Sende (Spain & Portugal)

Sende sits in rural Galicia, and it's built as the deliberate opposite of the city grind. If you've been running on too many flights and too much caffeine, a month here, with forest on one side and mountains on the other, resets something. The group stays small, usually 10-15 people, which is exactly why it bonds. By week two you're not housemates, you're a unit that cooks together.

Best for: Nomads seeking nature, quiet, and deep work. Location: Galicia, Spain. Price: $700-1,000/month.

7. Nine Coliving (Tenerife)

Nine Coliving in Tenerife went from unknown to constantly recommended in the space of a couple of years. The Canary Islands sell themselves: warm in February, cheaper than mainland Europe, and on a clock that still lines up with a Berlin standup. Nine gives you modern private rooms, coworking that holds together, and a calendar where a Tuesday might mean a surf lesson and a Thursday a volcano hike before dinner.

Best for: Nomads who want European time zones with tropical weather. Location: Tenerife, Canary Islands. Price: $800-1,300/month.

8. Chateau Coliving (France)

Yes, you can answer Slack messages from a real French chateau. Chateau Coliving runs month-long stays in restored chateaux out in the countryside, with coworking rooms in rooms that used to host counts, long communal dinners, and the occasional wine tasting that bleeds into the evening. It's proof that coliving doesn't have to mean a white-walled apartment and an IKEA desk.

Best for: Nomads who want a once-in-a-lifetime experience. Location: Various chateaux in France. Price: $1,200-1,800/month.

9. Outpost (Bali & Cambodia)

Outpost started as a coworking-coliving hybrid in Bali and pushed on into Phnom Penh. Its Canggu and Ubud houses have been nomad fixtures for years, pairing serious workspaces with rooms you'll actually sleep well in. The Cambodian spot is the move for stretching a budget across Southeast Asia, the same geoarbitrage logic behind our Bangkok budget guide, where the rent stays low and the productivity stays high.

Best for: Southeast Asia-focused nomads who prioritize workspace quality. Locations: Bali, Cambodia. Price: $600-1,200/month.

10. Anceu Coliving (Spain)

Anceu is the other Galician option, and the concept stays with you. It runs out of a restored village that had been emptying out for decades, and residents are part of bringing it back: you live among neighbors, hills, and fiber that somehow reaches the middle of nowhere. The people it pulls in tend to be the thoughtful, sustainability-minded sort, the kind who compost without being asked.

Best for: Nomads interested in sustainability and rural living. Location: Galicia, Spain. Price: $500-800/month.

What Does Coliving Cost?

What you pay swings wildly with location, operator, and how private your room is. Here's the honest spread for 2025, region by region:

Southeast Asia (Bali, Thailand, Cambodia): $500-1,200/month. This is where the maths gets silly in your favor. A private room, coworking, an events calendar, and often a pool, all for less than a bare studio costs in most Western cities.

Southern Europe (Spain, Portugal, Canary Islands): $700-1,500/month. The sweet spot, and the reason so many nomads orbit this band of coastline. You get European infrastructure and a slow lunch culture without watching your savings evaporate.

Western Europe & Americas (France, US, Mexico): $1,000-2,500/month. The expensive end. Premium addresses charge premium rates, though once you add up what's bundled in, the gap to a plain rental narrows fast.

Sticker price lies, though. Take that $1,000/month coliving and unbundle it: utilities ($100), fast internet ($50), a coworking desk ($200), cleaning ($100), the events ($50). Strip those out and the room itself is running you about $500. Do the same arithmetic on a furnished rental, where every one of those lines is a separate bill and a separate errand, and coliving usually wins on what actually leaves your account.

Pros & Cons of Coliving

Four years of bouncing between colivings, Airbnbs, and plain old rentals taught me where this model shines and where it grates. Here's the unvarnished version:

The Pros

  • Instant community: The headline feature. You drop your bag and you already have people to eat with, work beside, and split a scooter rental with on Sunday. It fixes the single hardest part of nomad life.
  • Zero setup friction: No flat hunt, no utility deposits, no afternoon lost decoding a Spanish washing machine dial. You walk in, unpack, and you're living.
  • Built-in coworking: A real desk thirty seconds from your bed beats hunting for a cafe that has both an outlet and WiFi that survives the lunch rush. Your commute is a flight of stairs.
  • All-inclusive pricing: One invoice, everything on it. No surprise power bill, no WiFi dying because a landlord forgot to pay the provider on the day you have three calls.
  • Networking that doesn't feel like networking: Residents skew entrepreneurial and well-traveled, and the best conversations happen over breakfast, not at a name-tag event. A few of mine turned into clients.

The Cons

  • Less privacy: A private room still means a shared everything-else. If you recharge by being alone, the constant low hum of other people gets tiring by week three.
  • Variable quality: The boom pulled in plenty of operators who saw a trend and not a community. Some are excellent. Some are a hostel with a "coworking" sign. Do the homework.
  • Community dependency: Your month rides almost entirely on who else booked it. A great cohort makes the place glow; a flat one leaves you paying a premium to feel lonely in company.
  • Minimum stays: Two weeks or a month is the usual floor, so it's far less nimble than a hotel if your plans shift on three days' notice.
  • Can feel institutional: Scale up far enough and the warmth thins out into processes and Slack channels. Real community is organic, and that stubbornly refuses to franchise.

"The best coliving experience I've had was at a 12-person space in Spain where everyone genuinely cared about the community. The worst was a 60-bed operation that was basically a hostel with a coworking room bolted on. Size matters, and smaller is almost always better."

How to Choose a Coliving Space

With hundreds of spaces fighting for your booking, picking the right one comes down to homework. This is the checklist I run every time, refined by a few stays that taught me the hard way:

1. Check the internet speed. For a remote worker this is the whole ballgame. Spaces that publish their actual speed test results are telling you they've got nothing to hide. Aim for 50 Mbps down at the floor, 100+ if you can get it, and ask whether there's a backup line. One dead router shouldn't end your workday and your client relationship in the same afternoon.

2. Read recent reviews, not old ones. A coliving can change character overnight when management turns over or the operator doubles the bed count. Weight the last six months and discount the rest. Cross-check Google Maps, Nomad List, and Coliving.com so one glowing outlier doesn't fool you.

3. Consider the community size. The 8-20 band tends to gel best. Drop below eight and a single clash of personalities can sour the whole place. Push past thirty and it stops being a community and starts being a building, with the intimacy that makes coliving worth it quietly draining out.

4. Look at the coworking setup. A laptop perched on a dining table is not coworking. You want dedicated desks, chairs that won't wreck your back over a month, a phone booth or call room, and outlets that aren't a daisy-chain of extension cords. If calls are part of your week, confirm there's somewhere private to take them before you pay.

5. Evaluate the location. A gorgeous house in the wrong spot gets old by day four. Can you walk to dinner, a grocery run, a decent coffee? Is there a bus or train when you want to leave? Is there a gym, a pool, a beach, anything that gets you off the screen and out the door?

6. Ask about the vibe. Some houses are sunrise-surf-and-sourdough; others are tequila until 3am. Neither is wrong, but booking the wrong one is a long month. Scroll their Instagram for a few minutes and the culture announces itself.

7. Start with a shorter stay. If there's a two-week option, take it. A month is a long sentence in a place that turns out to be a bad fit. Weekly rates usually cost a little more per night, and that premium is just insurance with an exit door attached.

Is Coliving Right for You?

Coliving isn't for everyone, and pretending otherwise does nobody any favors. If you guard your privacy, need a silent room to do your best work, or want to dissolve into a local neighborhood and learn the butcher's name, a normal rental wins. There's no medal for forcing yourself into a group house.

But if the social side of this life is the part that wears you down, if you're sick of the setup chores in every new city, or if you just want to meet sharp people without the name-tag theater of a networking event, try it once. For a lot of nomads, that first stay quietly becomes the default way they travel from then on.

Best for: Nomads who value community, convenience, and structured social environments. Those new to the nomad lifestyle who want a soft landing. Remote workers who want reliable coworking without a separate membership. Anyone who's tired of eating dinner alone.

Not ideal for: Deep introverts who need extended alone time. Couples or families (most spaces are designed for solo travelers). Budget nomads who can find cheaper accommodation independently. Long-term residents who want to put down roots in a single neighborhood.

So here's my actual advice. Pick a well-reviewed space in a city you already want to see, commit to two weeks and no more, and walk in expecting nothing. Worst case, you spent a fortnight in a nice room with fast WiFi. Best case, you walk out with the people who'll show up at the next three cities too.

Frequently Asked Questions About Coliving for Digital Nomads

What is coliving for digital nomads?

Coliving is shared housing built for community. You get a private bedroom and share kitchens, living rooms, and coworking areas, while the operator handles utilities, cleaning, WiFi, and furnishing. It works like a curated, professional flat share, with vetted residents and organized events that encourage genuine connection.

How much does coliving cost for digital nomads?

Pricing depends on location, operator, and room type. Southeast Asia runs roughly $500 to $1,200 a month, Southern Europe sits around $700 to $1,500, and Western Europe and the Americas reach $1,000 to $2,500. Most rates bundle utilities, internet, coworking, and cleaning into one bill.

Is coliving cheaper than renting an apartment?

Often, once you count what is included. A $1,000 coliving stay that covers utilities, fast internet, coworking, cleaning, and events can leave the room costing closer to $500. Compared with a furnished rental where you pay those extras separately, coliving frequently wins on real value.

Which coliving spaces are best for digital nomads?

Strong options include Selina for reliable brand consistency, Outsite for premium design, Sun and Co for tight community, and Tribe for affordable stays in Bali. Pair a stay with the right workspace using our guide to the best coworking spaces in Bali.

What are the main pros and cons of coliving?

The upside is instant community, zero setup friction, built-in coworking, and all-inclusive pricing. The downside is less privacy, variable quality between operators, dependence on the current cohort, and minimum stays of two weeks or a month. Smaller spaces almost always feel more genuine than large ones.

How do I choose a coliving space as a remote worker?

Check the internet speed first, then read reviews from the past six months. Favor communities of roughly 8 to 20 residents, inspect the coworking setup, and weigh the location and vibe. A coliving stay supports a steady remote work routine, so start with a shorter trial.

Is coliving worth it for first-time digital nomads?

For many, yes. Coliving offers a soft landing if you struggle with the social side of nomad life or the logistics of settling into a new city. If you need complete privacy or quiet, a traditional rental fits better. Browse our digital nomad city guides to find your next base.

Where can I find coliving destinations to base myself?

Coliving clusters around established nomad hubs in Southeast Asia, Southern Europe, and Latin America, from Bali and Lisbon to rural Spain and the Canary Islands. To match a destination to your budget and priorities, explore our city guides before committing to a stay.
