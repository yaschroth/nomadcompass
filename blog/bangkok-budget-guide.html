Bangkok on a Budget: What It Costs to Live There in 2026

Yannick Schroth March 2025 12 min read

Contents

  6. Visas and tax

A plate of kaphrao, minced pork stir-fried with holy basil and chilli, spooned over rice at a stall on a Bangkok soi, costs about $1.80. It was $1.50 until recently: in a government survey of one-dish meals this year, the most common price rise took a plate from $1.50 to $1.80. In the office lanes of Silom, the average meal has more than doubled since 2012 and now comes to about $1.95.

That one price explains a lot about living here. Three stall plates a day for a month come to about $160, so eating out every meal costs less than a quarter of the rent on a central one-bedroom. In Bangkok, what you eat barely moves your budget. Where you sleep decides it. The plate also shows the direction of travel: stall prices are rising 15 and 30 cents at a time, and the city that feeds you so cheaply is slowly clearing its pavements of the stalls that do it.

So here is the budget verdict. On our one-person figures, priced from Numbeo, you can live in Bangkok on about $1,150 a month with a one-bedroom in the centre, or about $800 in a cheaper district, and that buys a capital with a metro, fibre broadband and one of the world's great street kitchens. It is a poor choice if you cannot take heat, if your lungs object to smog from December to March, or if you want months without a long-stay visa: the visa-free stay is now 30 days.

Where a Bangkok budget really goes

Rent is the whole game. On our figures, a one-bedroom in the centre costs about $700 a month and one outside the centre about $350, while everything else a single person spends in a month, groceries, some meals out, utilities, a phone plan and transport, comes to about $450. The $350 difference between those two flats is bigger than your entire food bill could be if you ate at stalls.

That is why the cheap version of Bangkok is not about eating less. It is about living one or two train stops further out, which is how our lean budget reaches about $800. A couple sharing a central one-bedroom comes to about $1,510, which works out cheaper per head than living alone.

The restaurant side has a floor too. A meal in an inexpensive sit-down restaurant costs about $3 on our figures, and what the extra $1.20 buys is mostly a seat indoors. Eat at stalls for most meals and the food line of your budget stays small whichever district you choose.

The pavement kitchen is also a regulated one. City Hall allows vending only in designated spots, and every Monday is a non-trading day for the city's street vendors, meant for cleaning. The number of approved spots has fallen sharply: 321 regular vending points with 9,920 vendors in 2025, against 741 points with 16,838 vendors in 2022, plus a set of more lenient zones. If your soi has a good stall, it may not be there forever, and on Mondays you will be cooking or eating indoors.

Pick a soi near a station

Bangkok is huge, its traffic is notorious, and the trains run above and below it. So choose a station first and a flat second. Each of these areas sits on the BTS or MRT, and each changes the budget in its own way.

Ari: cafés and government offices

Around Soi Ari, off Phahonyothin Road by BTS Ari, stylish cafés and bakeries share the streets with government offices, including the Ministry of Finance. It is two stops north of Victory Monument, and it feels like a Thai neighbourhood that works office hours rather than a district built for visitors.

Thong Lo: the expensive end of Sukhumvit

Thong Lo is officially Sukhumvit Soi 55, reached from BTS Thong Lo. Bars and restaurants sprang up here in the early 2000s, and a large Japanese community lives around it. It is the place for evenings out, and our neighbourhood notes put it among the city's priciest areas, so it suits the top of the budget.

On Nut: further out on the same line

On Nut is a BTS station three stops beyond Thong Lo on the same Sukhumvit line, where three districts meet, with a walkway into a big Lotus's supermarket. Addresses like this, outside the centre but on the line, are where to look for the $350 one-bedroom.

Silom and Sathorn: the business district and the park

Silom Road, sometimes called the Wall Street of Thailand, is where BTS Sala Daeng meets the MRT at Si Lom. Across Rama IV Road lies Lumphini Park, 57.6 hectares of lawns and lakes laid out in 1925 and known for the water monitors that live around its lakes. Rents here are central rents; what you gain is the park for a run before the heat and both train lines at the door.

For food rather than a flat, Yaowarat Road in Chinatown is where to go: about 1.5 kilometres of shops that at night turns into one long food street for tourists and locals alike.

Moving around for about a dollar

The trains are what make a cheap Bangkok life comfortable. A BTS Skytrain trip costs 51 cents to $1.41, depending on distance, and the underground MRT Blue Line charges 51 cents to $1.32. The MRT takes contactless Visa and Mastercard cards at the gate, and since June 2026 its old stored-value cards no longer work; the BTS still runs on its own Rabbit card and single tickets.

You may read about a flat fare of about 60 cents. It applied only on the Purple and Red lines and ended in 2025; those two lines now cap what you pay at about $1.20 a day. A common fare capped at about $1.35 across all lines has been approved for January 2027, which would help most on trips that change between the BTS and the MRT.

The water is cheaper still. The Chao Phraya Express Boat's orange-flag line costs about 54 cents along the Chao Phraya; the canal boats on Khlong Saen Saep cut east to west across the city for 45 to 75 cents. Both fares move with the price of diesel. For everything else there is Grab or Bolt, and from Suvarnabhumi Airport the Airport Rail Link reaches Phaya Thai in about 27 minutes for $1.35.

Where you would work

The connection will not be the problem. Thailand's median fixed broadband runs at about 299 Mbps, among the ten fastest in the world, and its mobile data at about 134 Mbps, so a condo with fibre and a phone hotspot as backup covers most working days.

The cheapest desk in Bangkok costs nothing. The C asean Samyan CO-OP, on the second floor of the Samyan Mitrtown mall by MRT Sam Yan, is a reading and working space of about 500 seats, free to the public and open 24 hours; you register at the door. Upstairs in the same complex, JustCo runs paid coworking floors in the office tower.

If you want a community and a fixed desk, The Hive Thonglor on Sukhumvit Soi 49 occupies seven floors with a rooftop café and terrace, and a hot desk there starts at about $120 a month. Near the Asoke interchange, Glowfish is more of a serviced office, for when you need a private room. For a comparison with the other big Southeast Asian base, see our guide to coworking spaces in Bali.

Heat, rain and the haze months

Bangkok is hot all year and hottest in April, when daytime highs average almost 36°C and nights about 27°C. The rain comes from about June to October, heaviest in September. The rain costs you nothing; the heat decides how much of the city you will walk and how high your electricity bill runs.

The air is the real cost. From about December to March, traffic fumes and smoke from farm burning in the surrounding provinces build up over the city, and in mid-January 2026 Bangkok's average PM2.5 reached 58.1 micrograms per cubic metre, far above Thailand's 24-hour limit of 37.5. The cool, dry months that feel best outdoors are exactly the ones with the worst air. If you are sensitive to pollution, come between June and October, when the rain keeps it clean.

Staying longer than a month

Bangkok used to reward drifting in and staying. It no longer does. Since 15 September 2026, visitors from 60 countries get a 30-day visa exemption for tourism, down from 60 days, and an immigration office can extend it once by up to 30 days for about $57. Entries over land under the exemption are limited to two a calendar year, which ends the border run as a way of life. Before any arrival, fill in the Thailand Digital Arrival Card online.

For a real stay there is the Destination Thailand Visa, written for remote workers among others. It costs about $300, is valid for five years and allows stays of up to 180 days, each extendable once.

You need about $14,950 in savings, a test set in Thai baht whose dollar value moves with the exchange rate, and evidence of the remote work, such as an employment contract, pay slips or a portfolio. Since 31 August 2026 you must apply through the Thai mission covering your country of citizenship or legal residence, and many missions, London among them, now ask for a recent police clearance certificate. Our comparison of digital nomad visas shows how that savings test compares with income tests elsewhere.

Stay 180 days or more in a calendar year and you become tax resident in Thailand, and since 2024 foreign income you bring into Thailand in a year you are resident can be taxed. A proposal to exempt income brought in soon after it is earned has not become law. If a DTV stay will cross 180 days, read our guide to how digital nomads are taxed first, and if you need help with an application, our directory lists lawyers and visa agents in Bangkok by the languages they speak.

Is Bangkok the right budget base?

For a capital, yes. At about $1,150 a month with a central flat, Bangkok gives you a metro, fibre, a free 24-hour desk and a $1.80 lunch at the stall on the corner, which few cities of its size can match. The savings come from where you live, not what you eat: take a flat a few stops out, ride the train in, and $800 a month is realistic.

If the budget matters more than the big city, Chiang Mai comes in lower still, at about $840 a month on our figures, though its burning season makes March and April harder than Bangkok's haze. Our comparison of Medellín and Chiang Mai weighs it against the Latin American alternative. Either way, time the trip: arrive in June or November, sort the DTV before you fly if you want more than two months, and budget for the flat first. Lunch will take care of itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to live in Bangkok on a budget?

About $1,150 a month for one person with a central one-bedroom, on our figures priced from Numbeo, or about $800 in a cheaper district. Rent is the largest cost: $700 in the centre, $350 outside it.

How much is street food in Bangkok?

A one-dish plate such as kaphrao costs about $1.50 to $1.80 at a stall, after rises in 2026. An inexpensive sit-down restaurant meal costs about $3.

Which Bangkok neighbourhood is cheapest for digital nomads?

Areas outside the centre but still on a train line, such as On Nut on the BTS Sukhumvit line. On our figures a one-bedroom outside the centre averages about $350 a month, against $700 in the centre.

How long can I stay in Thailand without a visa?

Since 15 September 2026, 30 days for visitors from 60 countries, extendable once by up to 30 days at an immigration office for about $57.

What does the Destination Thailand Visa require?

About $14,950 in savings, proof of remote work and a fee of about $300. It is valid for five years, with stays of up to 180 days, each extendable once.

When is air pollution worst in Bangkok?

From about December to March. In January 2026 the city's average PM2.5 reached 58.1 micrograms per cubic metre, against a national 24-hour limit of 37.5. June to October is cleanest.

Is there free coworking in Bangkok?

Yes. The C asean Samyan CO-OP in the Samyan Mitrtown mall, by MRT Sam Yan, has about 500 seats, is free to use and is open 24 hours; you register on entry.
