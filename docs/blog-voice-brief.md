# Blog voice brief (read with docs/blog-writer-brief.md; where they conflict on HOW to write, this wins)

The owner read the 2026-09-26 batch and said: "these articles read like a chain of facts and not
like an organic article written by a human, I hate them." He is right. The research was good; the
writing was a data dump. This brief is about the writing.

## Who is talking to whom
Write like a well-informed friend who has done the homework, talking to ONE reader who is deciding
whether to go, where to live, or what to do next. Use "you". Have opinions and say them plainly
("Oslo is worth it if...", "Skip Brickell unless..."). Every section answers a question the reader
actually has, in the order they would have it, and ends with what it means for them.

Still true: no invented experiences, visits, quotes, anecdotes or "locals told me". You can be vivid
without pretending to have been there: describe what a place is like from what is documented (light,
seasons, layout, what is on the street, what a normal day costs and feels like), use hypothetical
scenarios ("Say you land in late November: the sun sets before four..."), and draw conclusions.

## What a paragraph looks like
- One idea per paragraph, with its "so what". A fact without its consequence is not finished.
- At most one or two numbers in a paragraph of prose. When a section needs more numbers, put them in
  ONE compact table or an "at a glance" box and let the prose talk about what they mean.
- Link the source on the words of the claim. Do not narrate the source: never "Statistics Norway puts
  X at Y", "according to the Ministry's page updated in March", "as listed on its membership page in
  September 2026". Dates only where the date changes the answer (a rule that changed, a price rise).
- No statute or section numbers in prose unless the reader must quote them to an official.
- Transitions carry the reader from one paragraph to the next. Read it aloud: if a paragraph could be
  shuffled to another position without anyone noticing, the article has no line of thought.
- Vary rhythm. A short sentence lands a point. Then a longer one can explain why it matters and what
  follows from it. Not every sentence needs a figure.

## Banned (these are what made the batch read like a machine)
- Openers that announce structure: "Two facts decide...", "The first is... The second is...",
  "This guide covers...", "Every figure below names its source", "Here is what you need to know".
- Sentences that begin with the source: "X puts...", "According to...", "X's data shows...".
- Stacked lists of prices or statistics in running text; bullet lists of numbers without a verdict.
- "As of <month year>" on every claim.
- Restating our own score as if it were information ("We rate Oslo 2 out of 10 for cost").
- Exhaustiveness. Cut anything a reader deciding about this place would not miss. Aim 1,800 to 2,600
  words for city guides and 2,200 to 3,000 for legal/money pieces: shorter than the first drafts.

## Shape of a good article
1. An opening that puts the reader in the situation and gives the honest verdict in the first
   paragraph or two (not a list of facts): who this place or answer is for, and who it is not for.
2. Sections built around decisions (Can I legally work here? Where should I live? What will it
   really cost me? What is daily life like? When should I go?), each ending in a recommendation.
3. One "at a glance" table near the top for the numbers people scan for.
4. A closing that says what to do next, then the FAQ.

## Keep from the writer brief
Accuracy, every fact traceable to sources.md, USD only, no em or en dashes, no disclaimers or
"consult a professional", no affiliate links in the body, hand-set internal links (keep the
existing ones), FAQ answers consistent with the body, two CTAs.
